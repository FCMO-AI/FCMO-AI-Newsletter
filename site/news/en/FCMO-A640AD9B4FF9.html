OptScientist uses a symbolic model and language-model research loop to search for optimization laws

The preprint introduces OptScientist, which pairs a learned symbolic optimization model with an LLM-based research loop to propose and compare objective and constraint laws from data. On OptFeynman, a synthetic benchmark generated from 99 known physics-derived optimization problems, the authors report exact preference-order recovery on 88 discrete and 78 continuous problems. They also report results on human risky choice and quarterly U.S. monetary policy. The policy evaluation uses 127 quarters split into 90 training, 23 validation and 14 out-of-time test observations; the model reports 78.6% exact policy-stance accuracy and 85.7% direction accuracy, with substantial uncertainty. These paper-reported results do not demonstrate causal mechanisms, broad transfer or independently reproduced scientific discovery.

Evidence

  • Claimed — OptScientist combines a learned model for extracting symbolic optimization problems from data with an LLM-based discovery loop that incorporates literature-derived priors, proposes candidate laws, critiques results and suggests further data.
  • Claimed — On OptFeynman, a benchmark generated from 99 known physics-derived optimization problems, the authors report exact preference-order recovery on 88 of 99 discrete problems and 78 of 99 continuous problems; this tests recovery under the benchmark's generated regime, not open-ended scientific discovery.
  • Claimed — On a quarterly U.S. monetary-policy stance task with 127 observations and a 14-quarter out-of-time test set, the authors report 78.6% exact stance accuracy and 85.7% direction accuracy for the unconstrained symbolic model; bootstrap uncertainty is large.
  • Not established — The paper does not establish that the discovered expressions are causal or true scientific mechanisms, that the findings generalize across domains or future periods, or that the approach improves real scientific work; the research desk has not reproduced the results.

Strongest baseline

The paper compares its policy model with machine-learning, symbolic-regression, optimization-structured and LLM-based discovery baselines. The symbolic-regression baseline has lower mean absolute error, while OptScientist reports stronger point estimates on several other metrics. The out-of-time sample is very small.

Limitations

  • All performance results are reported by the paper authors; the research desk has not reproduced them.
  • OptFeynman is a synthetic benchmark generated from known laws, so success there does not establish autonomous discovery of unknown laws in open scientific settings.
  • The U.S. monetary-policy dataset contains 127 quarterly observations and only 14 out-of-time test quarters; uncertainty is substantial and temporal robustness is unknown.
  • Predictive fit does not establish that a recovered symbolic expression is causal, uniquely correct or a valid domain mechanism.
  • The evaluation covers only two real-world tasks; transfer to other scientific fields and utility for scientists remain unmeasured.

What we still don't know

  • The reported OptFeynman law-recovery results have not been independently reproduced.
  • The real-data discovery claims lack independent replication and expert assessment of whether recovered laws are informative rather than merely predictive.
  • The small monetary-policy test set does not establish stability across policy regimes or future periods.

Why it matters

Automating the search for compact objective-and-constraint models could expand the kinds of scientific hypotheses AI systems can propose. This preprint reports recovery on generated physics problems and two narrow real-data tasks, including a 14-quarter test of U.S. monetary policy. The results are an early claim about a research method; they do not show that the discovered expressions are causal explanations or that the system generalizes as an autonomous scientist.
