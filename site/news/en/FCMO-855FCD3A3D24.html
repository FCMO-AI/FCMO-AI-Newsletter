Groq 3 LPX reaches full-production milestone with ~3.4k tok/s third-party long-context decode measurement

What actually changed

NVIDIA announced Groq 3 LPX in full production and disclosed the first third-party benchmark of the rack-scale LPU system. Artificial Analysis ran Gemma 4 31B on an NVIDIA-hosted private LPX endpoint and measured median output speed of 3,431 OpenAI-standardized tokens/s at 100K input context and 3,382 tokens/s at 10K. NVIDIA positions LPX as the latency-focused complement to Vera Rubin NVL72 for decode-heavy agentic inference, with deterministic compiler-scheduled communication and SRAM-heavy rack-scale execution.

Claim vs. evidence

  • DEMONSTRATED — Artificial Analysis measured median 3,431 output tokens/s at 100K context and 3,382 output tokens/s at 10K on Gemma 4 31B served by an NVIDIA-hosted Groq 3 LPX system.
  • DEMONSTRATED — The measured output-speed figure describes generation after the first token rather than full end-to-end response latency.
  • CLAIMED — NVIDIA states Groq 3 LPX is in full production and that Nebius is the first cloud adopter, with volume shipments to early adopters expected later in the quarter.
  • CLAIMED — NVIDIA documents a 256-LPU rack with 128 GB aggregate SRAM and deterministic compiler-scheduled execution/communication as core ingredients of LPX's low-latency design.
  • INFERRED — The current evidence is sufficient to establish unusually high single-stream decode speed in the tested regime, but not superior cost, energy efficiency, multi-tenant throughput or complete agent-task wall time.

Strongest baseline

Artificial Analysis's public Gemma 4 31B serving endpoints under the same 10K/100K context methodology provide the strongest directly comparable speed baseline; the NVIDIA technical post shows 870 output tokens/s as the fastest public endpoint at 100K and 1,402 at 10K at the time of testing.

The caveat that matters

  • Artificial Analysis tested a private endpoint hosted in NVIDIA's data center rather than a generally accessible production service.
  • The disclosed benchmark used sequential single-concurrency requests; multi-tenant throughput, P95/P99 latency and scheduler behavior under load are not established.
  • Output speed is measured after the first token; the headline 3.4k tok/s number is not time-to-first-token or complete end-to-end latency.
  • The directly benchmarked regime is one 31B dense model; transfer to very large MoE models, VLMs and mixed workloads remains unverified.
  • No matched public measurement of rack power, tokens-per-watt, price, cost per million tokens or total cost of ownership was found.
  • NVIDIA's SPEED-Bench results and large throughput-per-megawatt/economic projections are vendor-run or modeled and should not be conflated with Artificial Analysis's third-party speed measurement.
  • NVIDIA says full production, but its earnings call places volume shipments to early adopters later in the quarter; broad public-cloud availability was not verified.
  • No end-to-end agent benchmark demonstrates that the raw decode-speed ratio translates proportionally into task-completion wall time once tool calls, prefill, network latency and external systems are included.

What remains unknown

  • The Artificial Analysis result cannot yet be independently rerun on a public or separately controlled LPX endpoint.
  • Multi-tenant throughput, TTFT, P95/P99 latency and scheduler behavior under concurrent load are unknown.
  • Energy and economic efficiency are not independently measured in the demonstrated regime.
  • Raw decode speed has not been translated into complete agent-task wall time.

FCMO Lens

If the measured long-context interactivity transfers to production workloads, specialized decode hardware can remove a major serial latency bottleneck in multi-step agents and makes heterogeneous prefill/decode serving materially more attractive. The evidence does not yet establish public-service economics, energy efficiency, multi-tenant throughput, tail latency or end-to-end agent-task acceleration.
