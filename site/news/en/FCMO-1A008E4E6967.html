What changed

A September 11 revision of a preprint formulates transformer-block removal as a constrained binary optimization problem. It uses second-order interactions among binary keep/remove variables, derived from calibration-set gradients, to rank block combinations; several low-energy configurations may be worth testing because the lowest-energy one is not always best downstream. Without retraining, the authors report that removing 40 of 80 Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct blocks yields 76.9 MMLU for their method versus 54.0 for block influence, a 22.9-point difference, with better scores across the listed benchmarks. They also report results on a heterogeneous Nemotron MoE model without retraining. Results are author-reported and benchmark-specific; calibration data matters, candidate selection is manual, and the best pruning method varies by task.

Evidence

  • Claimed — Without retraining, removing 40/80 blocks from Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct yields 76.9 MMLU for the authors’ constrained-binary-optimization method versus 54.0 for block influence, a 22.9-percentage-point difference; the paper reports CBO also leads block influence on its other listed benchmarks for this configuration.
  • Claimed — The method uses second-order interactions among block-removal variables derived from calibration-set gradients; low-energy configurations tend to score well, but the lowest-energy state is not always the best across benchmarks and the authors manually select candidate configurations.
  • Claimed — On a heterogeneous 52-layer Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B-FP8 model, the paper reports selected removal configurations for 2–3 MoE layers and 2 attention layers that retain results on AIME25 and GPQA without retraining; results vary substantially by selected configuration and benchmark.
  • Not established — The preprint does not independently establish broad inference-cost savings or a generally superior compression method across models and tasks; the authors report benchmark-dependent tradeoffs and note calibration-set sensitivity.

Strongest baseline

Block influence (BI) with the same calibration data and retraining procedure where applicable; sliding-window merging and norm-ratio methods are also compared. For the headline 40/80-block no-retraining MMLU result, BI scores 54.0 versus CBO 76.9.

Limitations

  • All performance results are author-reported; independent reproduction was not inspected.
  • Headline 22.9-point MMLU difference is a specific no-retraining Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct 40/80-block comparison against block influence; it does not establish a general compression advantage.
  • The method sometimes loses to other block-removal methods on individual benchmarks, and the authors report calibration-dataset sensitivity.
  • The lowest-energy candidate does not always have the best downstream performance; configurations were selected manually.
  • The paper does not report matched end-to-end latency, energy, memory or serving-cost comparisons for deployed pruned models.

What we still don't know

  • No independent reproduction of the 40/80-block Llama-3.3 MMLU result or the Nemotron MoE configurations was inspected.
  • The paper evaluates benchmark accuracy but does not measure deployed inference latency, memory footprint, energy or end-to-end serving cost after pruning.
  • The low-energy candidate spectrum requires manual downstream selection, and calibration-dataset quality materially changes outcomes.

Why it matters

Selecting which blocks to remove is combinatorial because the value of each block depends on the others. If the reported results transfer, modeling pairwise interactions could retain more capability at very deep compression than local rankings or consecutive-block removal. The paper also shows that architecture and benchmark matter: its strongest headline result is one MMLU comparison, while several other models/tasks show mixed outcomes; improved benchmark retention does not establish inference speed, memory or quality tradeoffs in deployed workloads.
