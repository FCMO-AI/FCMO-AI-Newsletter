CogGym reports a large matched human–model evaluation suite with persistent behavioral gaps

A September 18 preprint introduces CogGym, a human-in-the-loop pipeline that standardizes cognitive-science experiments into a common format for matched human and language-model evaluation. The initial release reports 258 experiments from 100 papers across text, image and video, with 50 language models evaluated. On the paper’s reported human–model fit measure, the best models reach R² of 0.59 for text, 0.58 for image and 0.43 for video, below estimated human split-half references of 0.93, 0.95 and 0.92. A 15-experiment replication with 521 participants reports closer agreement between new and original human responses than between Gemini 3.1 Pro and the original responses. These are author-reported results; the 15-study check is a subset, not an independent reproduction of the full suite.

What changed

A September 18 preprint introduces CogGym, a human-in-the-loop pipeline that standardizes cognitive-science experiments into a common format for matched human and language-model evaluation. The initial release reports 258 experiments from 100 papers across text, image and video, with 50 language models evaluated. Evidence

  • Claimed — The authors report standardizing 258 cognitive experiments from 100 papers and evaluating 50 language models on matched human-response tasks spanning text, image and video.
  • Claimed — The paper reports best model–human fit R² values of 0.59 for text, 0.58 for image and 0.43 for video, below human split-half references of 0.93, 0.95 and 0.92; these estimates use reliability-eligible/recoverable subsets rather than all 258 experiments.
  • Claimed — For a 15-experiment replication subset with 521 participants, the authors report that new human responses agreed more closely with original human responses (mean R² 0.84) than Gemini 3.1 Pro responses did (mean R² 0.62). This subset check does not validate the full suite.
  • Not established — The preprint does not establish that model–human fit is equivalent to general intelligence, transfers beyond its selected commonsense experiments or model set, or causally improves with model scale.

Strongest baseline

Human split-half reliability, estimated from random participant splits and corrected with Spearman–Brown, is the paper’s human agreement reference: R² 0.93 for text, 0.95 for image and 0.92 for video. In the 15-experiment replication subset, newly collected human responses (mean R² 0.84) are compared with Gemini 3.1 Pro (0.62) against original human data.

Limitations

  • All results are author-reported in one preprint; the research desk did not run the code/data or reproduce the comparisons.
  • The initial suite focuses on human commonsense reasoning; it does not measure general intelligence or establish that models think like humans.
  • The 258-experiment inventory is not the analysis denominator for every metric: human reliability references use 172 recoverable archived exports, while the model reliability subset has 179 experiments.
  • A 15-experiment replication with 521 participants is a bounded validity check and does not independently reproduce the full 258-experiment model evaluation.
  • Associations with model size and release date do not establish causal effects of scaling; model lineups and task coverage are heterogeneous.

What we still don't know

  • The framework, corpus conversion and model scores have not been independently audited or reproduced by the research desk.
  • The main release focuses on commonsense tasks, and human split-half references rely on 172 recoverable exports; some analyses cover smaller eligibility subsets. Coverage outside these tasks and modalities is unknown.
  • Cross-sectional associations between model size/release date and human-response fit do not establish causal scaling effects or broad behavioral equivalence.

Why it matters

A reusable, multimodal translation layer for cognitive-science experiments could make human–model behavioral comparisons easier to update as models change. The reported results also caution against treating strong math or coding performance as evidence of human-like judgments. The current release focuses on commonsense tasks, has missing or unrecoverable archived data in some analyses, and does not establish broad cognitive equivalence or a causal effect of scale.
