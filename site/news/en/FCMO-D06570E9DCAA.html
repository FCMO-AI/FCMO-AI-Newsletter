Robot-failure study finds asking depends on evidence access and prompt layout

Pathak and Krishna study when a robot should act, consult its own sensors or ask a person after failure. In an injected-cause LIBERO simulation, their audit found grasp causes recoverable from force and gripper telemetry (0.986 classifier accuracy after leakage/transfer checks) but not reliably from images (best tested classifier 0.545); detection causes were visible in images, while a placement classifier’s apparent 0.998 accuracy failed a held-out-viewpoint check and was excluded. Across six open 7B–16B vision-language models, frame-based diagnosis remained at or below majority-class baselines and stated confidence did not track correctness. In option-order sweeps, refusal rates changed sharply for several model/failure pairs; ask rates did not track a fourfold question-cost sweep. These author-reported results are from a small simulation benchmark with 35–36 decision-test episodes per failure type and a scripted answerer, not physical-robot or real-dialogue validation.

What actually changed

Pathak and Krishna study when a robot should act, consult its own sensors or ask a person after failure. In an injected-cause LIBERO simulation, their audit found grasp causes recoverable from force and gripper telemetry (0.986 classifier accuracy after leakage/transfer checks) but not reliably from images (best tested classifier 0.545); detection causes were visible in images, while a placement classifier’s apparent 0.998 accuracy failed a held-out-viewpoint check and was excluded. Across six open 7B–16B vision-language models, frame-based diagnosis remained at or below majority-class baselines and stated confidence did not track correctness. In option-order sweeps, refusal rates changed sharply for several model/failure pairs; ask rates did not track a fourfold question-cost sweep. These author-reported results are from a small simulation benchmark with 35–36 decision-test episodes per failure type and a scripted answerer, not physical-robot or real-dialogue validation.

Claim vs. evidence

  • CLAIMED — The authors construct three simulated failure families with injected causes and audit whether camera or force/gripper data contain usable diagnostic information. For grasp failures, a force-data classifier reaches 0.986 and passes label-shuffle, appearance/viewpoint transfer and severity checks; the tested image-classifier sequence plateaus at 0.545. A placement image classifier’s 0.998 score fails held-out viewpoint testing and is excluded from the positive claims.
  • CLAIMED — Across six open 7B–16B vision-language models, frame-based diagnosis remains at or below majority-class baselines across tested prompt variants; stated confidence carries no useful relationship to correctness. Moving the refusal option between first and last positions sharply changes refusal rates in several model/failure pairs, including Qwen detection refusals falling from 78–100% to 0–6% in the principal sweep.
  • CLAIMED — The authors calculate act/ask/sense decisions from model accuracy and stated costs. Model ask rates did not track a fourfold question-cost sweep. Providing force telemetry as text produced above-baseline diagnoses in four of six models; the simulated one-question answerer raised diagnosis to 0.70–0.81 when asked, but answer utility depended on matching the models’ vocabulary.
  • NOT_ESTABLISHED — The study does not establish that the findings transfer to physical robots, frontier models, other failure families or unscripted human dialogue.

Strongest baseline

Model diagnosis is compared with family-specific majority-class baselines and audited sensor-classifier certificates; act/ask choices are compared with the minimum-expected-cost fixed policy given calibration accuracy. These are within-benchmark references, not physical-robot or human-performance baselines.

The caveat that matters

  • All measurements are paper-reported; the research desk did not run code or independently reproduce them.
  • The benchmark uses three injected failure families in LIBERO simulation under a scripted pick-and-place policy; planning failures, hardware faults and instruction errors are outside scope.
  • The decision test uses 35–36 held-out episodes per failure family. Six open 7B–16B models were tested, and model coverage differed across analyses.
  • The force-data audit’s 0.986 result is a validated lower bound for the tested grasp family and signals, not a universal claim about sensor sufficiency; the image plateau is limited to the tested classifier sequence and renderer.
  • Question/repair costs are stipulated in retry units. The one-question answerer is scripted, fixed, and does not respond to question wording; the study does not evaluate unscripted multi-turn dialogue.
  • No physical robot validation, frontier-model evaluation or deployment outcome is reported.

What remains unknown

  • The paper reports detailed simulation results, but this activation did not execute the released artifacts or independently reproduce the results.
  • The sensor findings are specific to injected failures in one LIBERO-based renderer and a scripted policy; physical camera cues, policies and robot dynamics may change what is observable.
  • Decision test sets contain only 35–36 episodes per failure family; question costs are stipulated and the answerer is scripted, fixed and insensitive to question wording.

FCMO Lens

The paper shows that a robot’s decision to keep acting, inspect another sensor or interrupt a person depends on what its sensors actually reveal and on its measured competence; verbal confidence and refusal behavior can be misleading. If this evaluation pattern generalizes, robot systems need sensor-aware and cost-aware escalation policies. The current evidence is a narrow simulation benchmark, so it does not establish field failure rates or deployed safety gains.
