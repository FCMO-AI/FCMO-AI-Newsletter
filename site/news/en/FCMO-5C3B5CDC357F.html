PARTS reports targeted real-world RL that improves long-horizon robot tasks on two platforms

In arXiv:2609.21788v2, Su et al. report PARTS, a real-robot reinforcement-learning framework that keeps a pretrained π0.5 vision-language-action policy frozen and trains bounded residual policies only at human-identified bottlenecks. Executable selectors, success verifiers and reset programs route practice and local rewards; periodic success-reweighted retraining returns updated residuals to online rollouts. Across three tasks on bimanual YAM and single-arm Franka FR3 robots, the authors report 32% to 61% full-task success for earbud insertion, 54% to 82% LEGO sorting progress, and 50% to 95% cable unplug-and-plug success, with 20 evaluation episodes per method/task and matched robot-rollout time against RL baselines. These are paper-reported measurements, not independently reproduced results. Human involvement remains in setup, some earbud reward checks and physical resets; the narrower result is no corrective actions or manual policy handoffs during RL rollouts.

Evidence

  • Claimed — The paper describes a frozen π0.5 base policy with bounded residual policies trained on selected bottleneck subtasks, using executable selectors, local success verifiers, reset programs and success-reweighted retraining.
  • Claimed — The authors report 32% to 61% complete-task success on earbud insertion, 54% to 82% LEGO sorting progress, and 50% to 95% success on cable unplug-and-plug, with 20 complete-task evaluation episodes per method/task and matched robot-rollout-time comparisons.
  • Claimed — The paper reports no corrective human actions or manual policy handoffs during RL rollouts, while humans identify bottlenecks during setup, check earbud reward labels, and perform physical resets when the robot cannot restore the scene.

Strongest baseline

The task-specific π0.5 supervised-fine-tuned base policy and three real-world RL methods (DSRL, EXPO-FT and RLT), each allocated the same elapsed robot rollout time for the task. The paper notes wall-clock and compute costs may differ and baseline supervision protocols vary.

Limitations

  • All outcome numbers are author-reported from one preprint; no independent replication, raw evaluation logs or released implementation were inspected.
  • Each method/task is evaluated on 20 complete episodes, so point estimates are sensitive to small sample counts; no confidence intervals are reported in the paper's summary figures/tables.
  • Human involvement is reduced in rollout control but not eliminated: humans identify bottlenecks and supply demonstrations at setup, verify earbud reward labels, and perform resets the robots cannot complete.
  • Matched comparisons control elapsed robot rollout time, not wall-clock time, compute, total setup effort or all human labor; baselines also differ in task-specific reward/reset assistance.
  • The project page states code is coming soon and its failure-analysis section is not yet populated. Transfer to other tasks, robot platforms and VLA policies is unmeasured.

What we still don't know

  • The reported task improvements have not been independently reproduced, and the project page says code is coming soon.
  • Twenty evaluation episodes per method/task provide limited precision, and confidence intervals are not reported in the paper summary materials inspected.
  • The matched budget is elapsed robot rollout time; wall-clock, compute, setup, bottleneck-identification and total human labor are not fully matched across methods.

Why it matters

If independently reproduced, concentrating expensive robot interaction on the few failure-limiting subtasks could make adaptation of capable pretrained robot policies more data-efficient while retaining reliable behaviors. The paper offers a concrete, multi-task test of that design under a matched robot-rollout budget, while also exposing where human supervision and physical resets remain necessary.
