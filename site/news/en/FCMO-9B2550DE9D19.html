OpenMAS-GCom reports controlled diagnosis of multi-agent system components

An arXiv paper introduces OpenMAS-GCom, a benchmark that compares multi-agent configurations with one component changed while tasks, models, prompts and budget limits are held fixed. The paper reports 17 configurations across 29 datasets in six domains, four interventions, and 400 frozen multi-document tasks requiring source-aware conflict resolution. What changed

An arXiv paper introduces OpenMAS-GCom, a benchmark that compares multi-agent configurations with one component changed while tasks, models, prompts and budget limits are held fixed. The paper reports 17 configurations across 29 datasets in six domains, four interventions, and 400 frozen multi-document tasks requiring source-aware conflict resolution. In a five-dataset aggregate, it reports a 3.58 percentage-point mean score loss after specialist removal versus 0.58 points after critic removal. These are author-reported benchmark results; independent reproduction and broader task-weighted interpretation remain open.

Evidence

  • Claimed — The authors report a controlled benchmark that changes one organizational component at a time while keeping task inputs, models, prompts and computation-budget limits fixed. It evaluates 17 configurations across 29 datasets in six domains and four intervention families: graph rewiring, role removal, incorrect-message injection and worker failure.
  • Claimed — Across five datasets, the paper reports mean score falling by 3.58 percentage points after removing specialists and by 0.58 points after removing critics; it also reports exceptions and differing response to incorrect messages across configurations.
  • Claimed — The authors report adding 400 frozen multi-document tasks with cross-document dependencies, conflicting records and structured answers requiring source identifiers. On these tasks, different configurations lead in strict exact-match accuracy and accuracy per token.

Strongest baseline

Within the paper, each intervention is paired against the original configuration with the same task set, model/backend, prompts, parser and budget controls; no independent benchmark comparison was performed here.

Limitations

  • All quantitative findings are author-reported; this activation did not run the benchmark or independently reproduce the reported comparisons.
  • The five-dataset aggregate for specialist and critic removal does not establish that the same effect holds across every dataset, task mix, model or production setting; the paper itself reports task/configuration-specific differences.
  • The authors describe an open-sourced benchmark library, but this activation did not locate and inspect a reproducible code artifact.
  • The G-MAS-Complex benchmark has 400 frozen constructed tasks; the paper does not establish external validity for real-world production workloads.

What we still don't know

  • The multi-agent component-removal and message-corruption results have not been independently reproduced in this review.
  • An accessible benchmark implementation and exact tested configuration artifacts were not located and inspected during this activation.
  • External validity of the 29 datasets and constructed 400-task suite for deployed multi-agent workloads is unestablished.

Why it matters

Final scores alone can hide which collaboration component contributes to performance. If the reported controlled comparisons hold, this benchmark offers a way to test whether roles, communication and worker availability matter under fixed task/model settings, informing multi-agent architecture and evaluation choices. The results do not establish that specialists are universally necessary or that the paper’s aggregates transfer to production systems.
