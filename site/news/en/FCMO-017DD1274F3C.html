OpenAI reports improved jailbreak robustness after adversarial-data RL training

A Nov 12 OpenAI paper describes an automated red-teaming loop that uses iterative attacker feedback and a repeated-success “worst@n” criterion to create adversarial training examples. In a controlled comparison, OpenAI reports that adding these examples to RL post-training raised safety scores from 45% to 68% on held-out fixed jailbreaks and from 71% to 92% on adaptive online attacks. The runs started from the same internal GPT-5.6-Terra checkpoint and used a fixed total RL budget. OpenAI also says it incorporated the approach into GPT-6 Astra and Sol training. These are first-party results, not independent confirmation; the paper discusses safety-judge bias and reports a cybersecurity benign-task utility decrease.

Evidence

  • Claimed — In a controlled RL post-training comparison, OpenAI reports that training with robustified adversarial tasks improved safety from 45% to 68% on held-out fixed jailbreaks and from 71% to 92% on adaptive online attacks.
  • Claimed — OpenAI says it incorporated this approach into GPT-6 Astra and Sol training, whose evaluated models scored more safely than earlier models on the reported fixed and adaptive jailbreak evaluations.

Strongest baseline

Two RL post-training runs initialized from the same internal GPT-5.6-Terra checkpoint; the robustified run added adversarial tasks at about a 3:1 ratio while proportionally downweighting other data to keep total RL budget fixed. Evaluation used a held-out attacker for fixed jailbreaks and 12 synthetic personas for adaptive attacks.

Limitations

  • The quantitative robustness and GPT-6 Astra/Sol integration claims come from OpenAI’s own paper and have not been independently reproduced or audited.
  • The controlled comparison uses an internal GPT-5.6-Terra checkpoint and laboratory-specific attack datasets; the starting checkpoint, robustified data and full model runs are not publicly available in the inspected paper.
  • The paper says repeated worst@n judgments reduce evaluator variance but do not resolve systematic judge bias; it reports identifying and patching judge gaps with human review.
  • The separate benign-task utility evaluation reports a cybersecurity full-compliance decrease from 81.7% to 76.7% for the robustness-trained model; that tradeoff should not be omitted.
  • The GPT-6 Astra/Sol evaluations are reported without production blocking safeguards and do not establish real-world incident reduction or robustness under all deployment conditions.

What we still don't know

  • No independent reproduction of the controlled RL result or GPT-6 Astra/Sol result was found; core checkpoints and attack datasets are internal.
  • The paper acknowledges that worst@n reduces judge variance but not systematic judge bias, and its reported cybersecurity utility score decreases from 81.7% to 76.7%.

Why it matters

If the reported effect generalizes, generating adversarial examples with repeated-success checks and training on them could improve robustness to held-out and adaptive jailbreaks in high-risk domains. The reported controlled gains and transfer to later model training could affect safety post-training choices, while the judge-bias caveat, cybersecurity over-refusal result and lack of independent reproduction limit what can be concluded.
