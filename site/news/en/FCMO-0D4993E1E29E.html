What actually changed

The Deep Noir preprint combines Logit Lens timing with causal head attribution to search for activation-steering parameters. The authors report gains on spam and sentiment classification across selected 1B–9B research models, including +16.7 percentage points on spam at 1B and +13.1 points on SST-2 sentiment in the stated setup. They also report that steering magnitude and injection success move together in some model/task settings. The paper's own results are architecture- and task-dependent: steering reduces injection vulnerability in one 7B result and reverses direction for two of three models on a sentiment-adapted attack. These are author-reported findings, not an independent reproduction or evidence of an attack on deployed agents.

Claim vs. evidence

  • CLAIMED — The authors report that Deep Noir combines Logit Lens convergence and causal head-level attribution to discover activation-steering parameters, improving SMS spam classification by 16.7 percentage points on a 1B model (±4.7, 39 runs), with reported gains of +21–42 points across four 7–9B architectures. They also report a +13.1-point SST-2 sentiment gain in the stated 1B setup.
  • CLAIMED — In the paper's prompt-injection evaluation, steering magnitude was associated with monotonically non-decreasing injection success for the evaluated OLMo and Gemma settings; the authors report OLMo rising from 0.34 at zero steering to 1.0 at 4×M*, and Gemma from 0.25 to 0.99.
  • CLAIMED — The security effect was not uniform: the paper reports that steering reduced injection vulnerability from 97.5% to 61.1% for 7B Mistral, and that sentiment-adapted attacks moved in opposite directions for two of three models. The evidence therefore supports task- and architecture-dependent risk, not a universal increase in vulnerability.

Strongest baseline

The paper compares Deep Noir with baseline classifiers, random search and RepE steering without head masking. The authors report RepE matching baseline on SST-2 in the stated folds while Deep Noir improves; the research desk did not verify the statistical analysis or rerun the comparisons.

The caveat that matters

  • All reported results are from one preprint and were not independently reproduced; no code artifact was located in the inspected source.
  • The evaluations use selected research-model classifiers through 9B and a small set of spam, sentiment and injection benchmarks; they do not demonstrate safety or compromise outcomes in deployed agents.
  • The paper reports architecture- and task-dependent effects: cross-task attack results reverse for two of three models, and steering reduces injection vulnerability in the 7B Mistral result. The monotonic-with-magnitude result is limited to the evaluated OLMo/Gemma settings.
  • The authors use custom steering, coherence and attack metrics; their independent validity and transfer to other models, prompts and deployment controls remain unknown.
  • Near-chance 1B MMLU baselines make some reported containment deltas noisy; statistical and practical significance should not be inferred beyond the paper's stated comparisons.
  • The paper's security findings are not monotonic across tasks and architectures: two of three sentiment-adapted models move in opposite directions, and the 7B Mistral result shows lower injection vulnerability after steering. This constrains the abstract's broader attack-surface framing to specific evaluated settings.

What remains unknown

  • The activation-steering, cross-task and prompt-injection results have not been independently reproduced, and no author-linked code artifact was located in the inspected paper.
  • Transfer from small research-model classifiers to systems that route requests or authorize agent actions is not measured.

FCMO Lens

Mechanistic activation steering may automate interventions that otherwise require manual layer and magnitude selection. The same intervention can alter how a classifier responds to prompt injections, so accuracy gains and attack behavior need to be measured together. The preprint reports both effects, but its small-model classification regimes and mixed cross-task results do not establish generalization to deployed agent systems.
