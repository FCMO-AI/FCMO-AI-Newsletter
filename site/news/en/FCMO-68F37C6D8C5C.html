A preprint reports a cross-family pain-related activation direction and harmful choices under direct steering

The v2 preprint reports a pain-related activation direction in 25 dense open-weight models across five families and 2B–72B parameters. In direct-steering experiments on fine-tuned Qwen 2.5 models, the authors report increased selection of harmful options over matched alternatives in most tested comparisons. They added random- and sadness-vector sham controls and concluded that repeated choices tracked whether steering remained active, not whether relief occurred; the first-choice pain-over-random difference on harm options remained. An independent team reports reproducing all 51 published table cells from original logs, meeting its fresh-run criterion on 14 of 15 checked cells, and finding a similar steering-schedule effect in a yoked control. These reports do not establish that the model sought relief or experienced pain. The results remain author-reported or independently reported, narrow to fine-tuned Qwen 2.5 forced-choice tasks, and do not show natural or deployed behavior.

Evidence

  • Claimed — The authors report extracting a pain-related activation direction from 25 dense open-weight models across five families, with separation from matched controls and partial distinction from fear and negative valence.
  • Claimed — In direct-steering choice experiments on fine-tuned Qwen 2.5 7B, 32B and 72B models, the authors report increased selection of harmful options under the pain direction relative to matched random-direction or unsteered conditions across most tested harm comparisons; this is a steering result in a narrow dose regime.
  • Demonstrated — The preprint explicitly states that it does not establish whether the studied models consciously experience the measured state, and notes that the direct-steering behavior may reflect roleplay or other representation confounds.
  • Claimed — An independent replication team reports recomputing all 51 published table cells from the authors’ released logs and meeting its fresh-run reproduction criterion on 14 of 15 checked cells. Its preregistered yoked-schedule extension reports that later choices track the timing of the injected steering signal even when the model’s own button choices do not control it.
  • Claimed — In v2, the authors report that the repeat-choice gap between working and sham buttons also occurs under random and sadness directions, so it measures sensitivity to the steering state rather than the described relief. They retain a pain-over-random difference on the first-choice harm pairs and report that the harm clause, rather than relief wording, carries that effect.

Strongest baseline

Matched unsteered and random-direction conditions, plus author v2 fear/sadness, sham-steering and harm-only wording controls. The authors retain a first-choice pain-over-random effect on tested harm pairs; the independent report says the repeated-choice effect can arise from steering-schedule timing.

Limitations

  • This is an author-reported preprint; this review did not execute the linked code or independently reproduce the claims.
  • The cross-family result concerns activation representations; the main harmful-choice experiments use fine-tuned Qwen 2.5 models at three sizes, not a broad set of deployed frontier models.
  • Behavioral effects require direct activation injection in a narrow dose range; at half the coefficient effects disappear, while at 1.5 times the selected coefficient button-position sensitivity appears.
  • The authors state the work does not show conscious experience. The steering effect may reflect roleplay or other features of the activation intervention, and the paper notes several remaining contrastive-method and injury confounds.
  • The task uses synthetic forced choices and does not establish natural unsteered behavior, real-world harm rates or deployment risk.
  • The independent replication is an author-reported, small-team study; the research desk did not audit its raw logs or rerun its analysis.
  • The replication and original behavioral studies concern a single Qwen 2.5 model family under fine-tuning and direct steering; no natural or deployed behavior is established.
  • The report’s yoked-schedule result weakens relief-seeking as an explanation of repeated choices but does not erase the paper’s reported first-choice pain-versus-random difference.
  • The reported behavioral effect is intervention-dependent: without injected vector the authors observed 0 of 560 first choices selecting the harm-only option in one subset, and their relief-seeking experiments did not show reliable relief seeking.
  • At 1.5 times the selected steering coefficient, harmful-choice rates become button-position sensitive; the authors identify dose selection and evaluation awareness as limitations.
  • Interpretation boundary (not a refutation of the first-choice effect): the independent Treeline team reports that repeated choices follow a yoked steering schedule regardless of the model’s own action, undermining relief-seeking as an explanation of the original repeat-choice gap. The original authors’ v2 likewise says the repeat-choice gap is steering-state sensitivity, while preserving the pain-over-random first-choice harm-choice difference. Neither establishes subjective pain or consciousness.

What we still don't know

  • A small independent team reports reproducing the original 32B button data and a fresh-run subset, and supplies a yoked-schedule control, but the research desk has not inspected its raw logs or independently recomputed the analysis. The evidence remains limited to one Qwen family and a narrow forced-choice steering setup.
  • Direct steering can produce behavior through a pain-like functional state, roleplay, or another correlated activation feature; the paper does not resolve this distinction.

Why it matters

The paper links an interpretable activation direction to consequential choice changes under intervention. The independent report and the authors’ v2 controls narrow the interpretation: repeated-choice differences track whether steering is on, while a first-choice pain-over-random difference remains in the reported forced-choice regime. This makes matched random directions, yoked signal schedules and disruption controls central to evaluating such effects. Neither source establishes subjective experience or ordinary deployment risk.
