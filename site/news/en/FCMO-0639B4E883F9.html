An occupational audit finds LLM judges rank responses better than they match worker score levels

The authors evaluate 33 LLM-judge configurations against 4,501 held-out worker ratings spanning 307 occupations in a US English text corpus. They report above-chance tie-aware response ranking for all configurations, while predicted acceptance shares range from 3.0% to 97.9% against the sampled workers’ 61.1%. A prespecified validation contrast within one fine-tuned lineage reports that a bundled few-shot/listwise protocol improves ordering while reducing task- and occupation-level agreement. The study audits agreement with sampled worker ratings; it does not measure objective work quality, AI productivity or employment effects.

Claim vs. evidence

  • CLAIMED — The authors compare 33 judge configurations with 4,501 held-out worker ratings from 307 occupations and report above-chance tie-aware ranking for all configurations.
  • CLAIMED — Reported judge acceptance shares range from 3.0% to 97.9%, compared with 61.1% in the sampled worker ratings; these are corpus-relative agreement results, not employment or productivity estimates.
  • CLAIMED — In a prespecified validation contrast within one two-epoch fine-tuned lineage, a bundled few-shot/listwise protocol improves ordering while reducing task- and occupation-level agreement.

Strongest baseline

Sampled worker ratings provide the study’s reference, with acknowledged low reliability at occupation level; they are not objective ground truth.

The caveat that matters

  • Each response has one worker rating; sampled worker judgments are not noise-free ground truth.
  • Occupation-level split-half reliability is low; the source reports 0.093.
  • The US English text corpus is not employment-weighted; occupations and response-generator variants overlap across splits.
  • Most test comparisons were exploratory after repeated inspection; the prespecified protocol contrast bundles several changes.
  • No real workplace output, productivity, hiring or employment effect is measured.

What remains unknown

  • No independent reproduction or validation on multi-rated, employment-weighted occupational data.

FCMO Lens

Occupational AI assessments can change when judge scores are converted into acceptance shares or occupation-level estimates. The paper shows that response ordering and score-level agreement are distinct measurement targets in one corpus, while leaving worker-reference reliability and transfer to real workplace outcomes unresolved.
