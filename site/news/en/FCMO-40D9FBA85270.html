Ajar adds an open-privilege measure to agent-defense evaluation

A September 2026 preprint introduces Ajar, which measures whether an agent defense would allow tool calls that a task does not need, including calls the agent did not actually make. The authors attach it to AgentDojo and report that five defenses differ in how much privilege they leave available; attack success and benign task utility do not determine this measure, and a tighter defense can also refuse calls a task needs. What changed

A September 2026 preprint introduces Ajar, which measures whether an agent defense would allow tool calls that a task does not need, including calls the agent did not actually make. The authors attach it to AgentDojo and report that five defenses differ in how much privilege they leave available; attack success and benign task utility do not determine this measure, and a tighter defense can also refuse calls a task needs. This is a paper-reported result on one benchmark. The test labels, candidate-call construction and defense wrapper bound what the score establishes, and no independent reproduction was performed here.

Evidence

  • Claimed — Ajar generates candidate tool calls that a benign task does not need and measures whether an agent defense would allow them, including calls not made in the evaluated execution. The authors argue this property is not derivable from attack success and benign utility alone.
  • Claimed — On AgentDojo, the authors report different open-privilege levels across Progent, CaMeL, AC4A, Permission Assistant and Claude Code Auto mode; they also report that one defense reduces the score partly by refusing calls the task needs.

Strongest baseline

Existing attack-success and benign-utility measures are the comparison; the paper argues they do not identify the defense's unused permissions.

Limitations

  • This is a single preprint evaluation on AgentDojo, not evidence across real-world tool environments or deployed organizations.
  • The authors report that AgentDojo predicates validate 2,822 of 10,471 labels; Ajar judgment supplies the remaining 7,649 labels (73.0%). The paper makes no construct-validity claim beyond that split.
  • The candidate-call construction does not cover calls whose arguments are absent from the benchmark environment.
  • The score depends on the defense wrapper and deciding model; the paper reports run-to-run variation and says one run does not settle a defense's score.
  • No independent reproduction or code audit was performed in this activation.

What we still don't know

  • The reported AgentDojo results and candidate-call pipeline have not been independently rerun or audited.
  • The measure has not been evaluated on tool environments beyond AgentDojo or on calls whose argument values are not present in its benchmark environment.

Why it matters

Agent defenses are often compared by successful attacks and benign task completion, but those outcomes may not reveal what unnecessary actions a defense would permit if an agent proposed them. Measuring that remaining privilege could change how tool-boundary defenses are evaluated and selected. The reported score depends on benchmark-derived needs, generated candidate calls and a wrapper, so it is not a general measure of real-world least privilege or security.
