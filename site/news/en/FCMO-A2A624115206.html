CLR-Bench tests whether self-evolving agents infer hidden rules from feedback

The 29 October arXiv preprint introduces CLR-Bench, a benchmark of 20 four-level curriculum chains across 10 constructed domains. It tests whether six self-evolution methods infer hidden named groupings from sparse feedback and transfer them to unseen values, rather than retaining corrections as exceptions. The authors report weak feedback-only generalization for GPT-6-Luna, improvement with a fixed lesson, and substantially stronger results for selected methods with GPT-6-Sol. These are author-reported results on a small constructed benchmark; they do not establish general agent learning or operational transfer.

The 29 October arXiv preprint introduces CLR-Bench, a benchmark of 20 four-level curriculum chains across 10 constructed domains. It tests whether six self-evolution methods infer hidden named groupings from sparse feedback and transfer them to unseen values, rather than retaining corrections as exceptions. The authors report weak feedback-only generalization for GPT-6-Luna, improvement with a fixed lesson, and substantially stronger results for selected methods with GPT-6-Sol. These are author-reported results on a small constructed benchmark; they do not establish general agent learning or operational transfer.

Evidence

  • Demonstrated — The paper and linked public repository describe and provide 20 four-level chains across 10 domains, with 80 hidden rules, practice tasks and test tasks; the benchmark uses probe-certified hidden groupings and diagnostics for missed members and false exclusions.
  • Claimed — On the authors’ benchmark, six self-evolution methods show limited feedback-only generalization with GPT-6-Luna; a fixed named-grouping lesson raises some methods’ scores, while ACE and Dynamic Cheatsheet score higher with GPT-6-Sol. Outcomes vary by model and update method.
  • Not established — The study does not establish that benchmark results predict learning from feedback in open-ended, real-world agent deployments, and its reported comparisons have not been independently reproduced.

Strongest baseline

Frozen agent, raw practice-history reference, and fixed-lesson condition on the same 20 chains; comparisons are author-reported and run once per condition per chain.

Limitations

  • The benchmark has 20 constructed chains in 10 domains, with only two chains per domain.
  • The paper reports each condition over the benchmark’s 20 chains; no independent reproduction or external evaluation was found in this review.
  • The results depend on the evaluated models, prompts/update methods and benchmark convention that hidden labels denote named groupings; they do not directly establish performance on open-ended real-world feedback.
  • The linked repository is publicly visible, but its license is listed as undecided; no code or data was executed or adopted here.

What we still don't know

  • The reported model and update-method comparisons have not been independently reproduced on CLR-Bench or broader task distributions.

Why it matters

Persistent agent state is often expected to turn feedback into reusable knowledge. CLR-Bench isolates a particular failure mode—storing individual corrections instead of inferring a rule—and separates it from over-generalization using value-level diagnostics. If the benchmark proves useful beyond its initial tasks, it could improve how self-evolving agents are evaluated; the current results remain narrow and unreplicated.
