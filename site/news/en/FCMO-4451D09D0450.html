Short token cues elicit reasoning behavior associated with training data in tested base models

An arXiv preprint reports that prefilling selected response-opening tokens can raise tested base models’ math accuracy toward that of direct-RL counterparts, and controlled data edits can make arbitrary openings act as reasoning cues. What changed

An arXiv preprint reports that prefilling selected response-opening tokens can raise tested base models’ math accuracy toward that of direct-RL counterparts, and controlled data edits can make arbitrary openings act as reasoning cues. The results support a training-association mechanism in the tested models, while leaving the contribution of RL, generalization and cue-conditioned safety unresolved.

Evidence

  • Claimed — In the authors’ tested settings, prefilling selected two-token openings raised base-model MATH-500 pass@1 from 42% to 78% for Olmo-3-7B (the compared RL-Zero model scored 75%) and from 72% to 87% for Qwen3-14B (matching its compared RL variant). The authors report gains across several math benchmarks; the Qwen coding result stayed near its base-model level.
  • Claimed — The authors report controlled mid-training data edits that made previously ineffective tokens such as “chicken” elicit reasoning behavior in Olmo-3-7B; a similar instruction/data edit made “Think duck duck goose” perform comparably to “Think step by step” in their tested setting.
  • Not established — These experiments do not establish that reinforcement learning adds no reasoning capability, that the reported effects generalize to deployed instruction models, or that any cue is broadly safe; the authors report cue-dependent refusal and harmful-compliance behavior.

Strongest baseline

The paper compares each base model without a cue and its selected-cue version with its RL-trained counterpart. On MATH-500 the reported rates are 42% / 78% / 75% for Olmo-3-7B and 72% / 87% / 87% for Qwen3-14B.

Limitations

  • All performance and data-intervention measurements are reported by the preprint authors; the research desk has not independently reproduced them.
  • Main cue comparisons use selected openings on Olmo-3-7B and Qwen3-14B with specified prompts and up to 32 sampled responses per problem; cue effectiveness varies by model and prompt.
  • The paper/code mismatch on beam-search problem count leaves the figure-producing configuration unresolved.
  • The RL comparison is direct base-to-R1-Zero-style training and does not establish what multi-stage post-training adds.
  • The safety evidence is a cue-specific case study, not a general safety rate; cues can change refusal and harmful compliance.

What we still don't know

  • No independent reproduction of the main cue and data-intervention results has been inspected.
  • Paper §2.1 describes beam proposal over 30 MATH training problems, while the pinned initial public code defaults to 100 beam proposals and 30 screening problems; the configuration that produced published figures is unresolved.
  • Safety behavior is a cue-specific author-run case study; broader prompt/model coverage and independent adjudication are unavailable.

Why it matters

The results make a concrete case that some reasoning behavior in these tested models can be elicited through short output openings associated with training data, complicating simple claims about what direct RL newly teaches. If replicated, this would affect post-training interpretation and evaluation design. The experiments do not establish broad deployment transfer or that RL adds no capability.
