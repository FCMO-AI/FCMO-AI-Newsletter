ServeLearnBench reports that agents struggle to learn changing hidden policies from service feedback

Zheng et al. introduce ServeLearnBench, a benchmark for learning policies that change during service interactions. Its three domains contain 53 environment windows, 4,508 feedback-bearing serving tasks and 3,210 held-out tasks. Across six models and five learning harnesses, the authors report a large gap between following a disclosed policy and inferring it from outcomes: on Retail L3 the best of 28 tested model–harness pairs scored 59.6% and the median 22.0%. They also report higher costs for hidden-policy inference and cases where adaptation reduced performance on fully specified tasks. These are author-reported results from a designed benchmark, not an independent measurement of deployed services; harnesses retained different native resource budgets.

Its three domains contain 53 environment windows, 4,508 feedback-bearing serving tasks and 3,210 held-out tasks. Across six models and five learning harnesses, the authors report a large gap between following a disclosed policy and inferring it from outcomes: on Retail L3 the best of 28 tested model–harness pairs scored 59.6% and the median 22.0%. They also report higher costs for hidden-policy inference and cases where adaptation reduced performance on fully specified tasks. Evidence

  • Claimed — The paper introduces ServeLearnBench, covering retail support, banking case review and sales-pitch generation across 53 environment windows, 4,508 serving tasks and 3,210 held-out test tasks.
  • Claimed — The authors report evaluating 28 model–harness pairs drawn from six models and five continual-learning harnesses. On Retail L3, the best pair scored 59.6% and the median pair 22.0%; Oracle, which receives the hidden policy, scored 95.5% averaged across settings.
  • Claimed — The authors report that hidden-policy inference cost 4–101 times as much as following a disclosed policy in their evaluated settings, and that adaptation can reduce performance on tasks whose rules are fully specified.
  • Demonstrated limitation — The paper compares complete harness systems with their native update schedules, replay access, state capacity and learning-side computation; it does not isolate harness effects under equal information and resource budgets. The benchmark results have not been independently reproduced in this review.

Strongest baseline

Oracle receives the active hidden policy and averages 95.5% on Hidden cases; it is an upper reference that removes policy inference, not a like-for-like deployable agent. Blind receives no serving history and gives a no-adaptation reference. Harness comparisons preserve native resource budgets.

Limitations

  • Author-reported results from one preprint; no independent reproduction was performed.
  • The three designed task domains do not establish performance in deployed services.
  • Native update, replay, state and compute budgets differ across harnesses, so comparisons do not isolate harness effects under equal resources.
  • Cost estimates depend on the paper’s model/API pricing and evaluated task settings.

What we still don't know

  • Independent reproduction of the reported learning, cost and collateral-regression results.
  • Determine whether the benchmark’s adaptation and cost patterns transfer to live service workflows with naturally occurring policy changes.

Why it matters

Reliable agents need to adapt when operational rules change without being explicitly supplied. This benchmark measures that problem across several service settings and makes adaptation costs and collateral regressions visible. Its reported results suggest that tool or memory updates alone do not ensure reliable policy learning, while the native-budget comparison and synthetic task regimes limit causal and deployment conclusions.
