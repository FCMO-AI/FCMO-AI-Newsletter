EvoPathBench tests whether self-evolving agents retain and adapt capabilities

A September 21 preprint introduces EvoPathBench, an author-built benchmark for tracking persistent agent memory and skill updates across 1,800 ordered task streams. It tests held-out generalization, retention after unrelated learning and adaptation after a rule changes. In the reported experiments, gains often weakened under distribution shift, retention loss was concentrated in some paths, and no method showed reliable rule adaptation; adaptation effects were not statistically significant after Holm correction. The paper also reports that update-selection safeguards reduce harmful accepted changes but miss more beneficial ones. These results come from fixed configurations in constructed trading environments combining public Binance data and calibrated synthetic trajectories. They are not independently reproduced and do not establish deployed-agent or real-market performance.

Evidence

  • Claimed — The authors introduce EvoPathBench, a benchmark of 1,800 ordered streams across six task families and two market layers. It evaluates persistent memory/skill artifact updates using held-out checkpoint tests for learning generalization, capability retention and rule adaptation.
  • Claimed — In the reported experiments, gains on familiar conditions often weakened under distribution shift, retention losses were concentrated in a subset of paths, and no evaluated method showed reliable rule adaptation. No revision effect remained statistically significant after Holm correction.
  • Claimed — The authors report that candidate selection is a major bottleneck: safeguards reduced accepted harmful updates but also lowered capture and recall and increased missed improvements. The benchmark reports selected artifact-level methods on its own constructed market tasks.
  • Not established — The preprint does not establish that its measured effects generalize to deployed agents, other model or tool configurations, or real trading outcomes; the research desk did not independently reproduce the results.

Strongest baseline

The non-evolving Baseline is used for capability evolution and retention comparisons; disabling artifact access provides a state-use comparison. The paper also compares memory and skill-evolution methods.

Limitations

  • All empirical findings are reported in one preprint; the research desk did not execute code or independently reproduce them.
  • The benchmark comprises constructed task streams combining public Binance Spot data with calibrated synthetic trajectories, not actual trading outcomes.
  • Results cover ten configurations with fixed base models, tools and executors and five update opportunities; transfer beyond these configurations is unknown.
  • The paper reports no rule-revision effect statistically significant after Holm correction; point-estimate rankings should not be read as reliable superiority.
  • The benchmark and method comparisons are author-designed; independent assessment of task validity, API/model variability and release reproducibility remains open.

What we still don't know

  • No independent execution or reproduction of the benchmark results was performed.
  • The benchmark uses public Binance Spot data combined with calibrated synthetic trajectories; evidence does not establish validity in other agent deployments or actual trading.
  • Rule-adaptation rankings are not reliable evidence of method superiority because no revision effect remains statistically significant after Holm correction.

Why it matters

Persistent memories and skills can change an agent over a sequence of tasks, while a final score can hide what it learned, lost or failed to revise. EvoPathBench reports a structured way to test transfer, retention and adaptation at frozen checkpoints. Its results point to weaknesses in transfer and update selection, but the evidence is an author-run benchmark using constructed trading tasks; the paper does not show real-world trading or deployed-agent effects.
