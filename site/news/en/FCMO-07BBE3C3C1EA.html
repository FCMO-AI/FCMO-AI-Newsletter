Study finds later clarification can leave early task assumptions embedded in dialogue outputs

A study of controlled writing, planning and coding dialogues reports that models can retain traces of an early interpretation even after a later user clarification is present. The authors call this “early posterior collapse” and measure “old-hypothesis contamination” in final outputs. Across 7,160 trials using Gemini-2.5-Pro and Gemini-2.5-Flash, ambiguous-first dialogues performed worse than clarified-first dialogues despite equivalent final task-relevant information. Coding tasks had the lowest success (24.4%) and highest contamination (0.256). Summaries, chain-of-thought and a single-state ledger did not reliably eliminate contamination. Explicit rollback and a two-phase ask-then-regenerate policy improved results in supplementary controlled settings. These are author-reported findings using a model judge and a limited task/model set; they do not establish how often the failure occurs in real deployments.

Evidence

  • Claimed — The authors define early posterior collapse as an unresolved interpretation becoming a committed task state before ambiguity is resolved, and old-hypothesis contamination as residual traces after a later clarification.
  • Claimed — In the paper’s controlled task suite, ambiguous-first dialogues had lower success than clarified-first dialogues: 42.8% versus 50.0% for Gemini-2.5-Pro and 45.7% versus 55.4% for Gemini-2.5-Flash.
  • Claimed — Across task families, coding had the lowest reported success (24.4%) and highest contamination (0.256); summaries and chain-of-thought did not reliably remove contamination.
  • Claimed — In supplementary controlled experiments, explicit rollback increased success from 17.5% to 27.5% and reduced measured contamination from 0.100 to 0.000; a two-phase response policy also produced 0.0% wrong commitment and 0.00 contamination in its tested setting.
  • Not established — The paper evaluates two Gemini model variants on generated and hand-crafted dialogue tasks with Gemini-2.5-Pro as an automatic judge; it does not measure the prevalence of this failure in real user deployments.

Strongest baseline

The same task-relevant information in clarified-first versus ambiguous-first order. For interventions, normal clarification is compared with explicit rollback or a two-phase response policy in the paper’s supplementary controlled settings.

Limitations

  • Only Gemini-2.5-Pro and Gemini-2.5-Flash are tested; model-family and version generalization is unknown.
  • Gemini-2.5-Pro is used as the automatic judge; the study does not report an independent human evaluation of contamination labels in the reviewed sections.
  • Tasks are generated or hand-crafted controlled dialogues, not sampled from real deployments; the observed rates are not prevalence estimates.
  • The paper notes that order effects overlap with recency bias, prompt-position effects and instruction-following brittleness; the proposed failure mode is not the only explanation.
  • Rollback and two-phase interventions are supplementary mechanism checks in limited settings, not general solutions.

What we still don't know

  • Replicate path-order and contamination results across model families, model versions, and independent human or separately calibrated judges.
  • Measure whether the failure occurs in naturally occurring user interactions and whether rollback or uncertainty-preserving state improves task completion without excess clarification cost.

Why it matters

Interactive assistants often revise plans, code and documents as users clarify their intent. If a stale interpretation persists inside structured artifacts, adding the correction to context may not be enough; the system may need to invalidate or rebuild its task state. The paper makes this distinction testable and reports interventions worth checking in other models and real workflows. Its controlled tasks do not establish general deployment prevalence or a universally effective fix.
