Evidence

  • Claimed — The authors report 60/60 in-domain and 52/60 out-of-distribution successes across three bimanual YAM robot tasks. Table 6 reports 94.4% unweighted macro success across six settings and 93.3% pooled micro success (112/120 trials); MolmoAct2 is the strongest reported robot baseline at 62.2% macro and 60.0% micro. The protocol uses five trials per setting and at least six object configurations; task-specific embodiment adaptation and fine-tuning are used.
  • Claimed — On LIBERO-PRO, the authors report 55.6% average success for ThinkWorld versus 48.5% for VLA-JEPA, with larger reported margins on task-shift and environment-shift subsets.
  • Claimed — In a human-video ablation, adding egocentric human video raises reported success by 2.4 percentage points over robot-only training and by 1.2 points over the matched robot-only scale control.
  • Claimed — The paper reports one training seed; its episode-level confidence intervals do not measure variability across separately trained checkpoints.

Strongest baseline

For the reported LIBERO-PRO average, VLA-JEPA is the named comparison at 48.5% versus 55.6% for ThinkWorld. On the real-robot table, the strongest reported baseline is MolmoAct2 at 62.2% macro and 60.0% micro success, versus 94.4% and 93.3% for ThinkWorld. The results are specific to the tested setups.

Limitations

  • Empirical results are author-reported; no independent reproduction was performed.
  • Real-robot evaluation covers three in-house bimanual tasks with five trials per setting and at least six object configurations; it does not establish broad deployment reliability.
  • The real-robot protocol uses embodiment adaptation and task-specific fine-tuning; the results do not establish zero-shot transfer.
  • The paper reports one training seed, and episode-level confidence intervals do not capture variation across trained checkpoints.
  • The human-video ablation reports a modest 2.4 percentage-point gain over robot-only training and 1.2 points over a matched scale control; this does not alone establish broad value from human-video data.
  • Linked code and model artifacts were not inspected or executed.

What we still don't know

  • The paper's reported robot and simulation results have not been independently reproduced, and code/model artifacts were not inspected.
  • Real-robot success rates are based on a limited in-house task and trial set; transfer across hardware, teams, tasks and independently trained checkpoints remains unmeasured.

Why it matters

If the reported gains transfer across tasks, environments, and checkpoints, learning from unlabeled human video through predicted world dynamics could make robot policies less dependent on action-labeled demonstrations. The current paper supports a notable research direction, not a general or zero-shot transfer claim.
