THAW-VLA distills world-model features into a compact robot policy

THAW-VLA uses a frozen world model to produce cached training-frame features, then adds a feature-alignment loss to a compact vision-language-action policy. The teacher and projector are removed before deployment, so inference uses the undistilled student graph. The authors report a 0.8B policy moving from 95.3% to 97.9% mean success on LIBERO and from 48.2% to 50.5% on RoboCasa-GR1; they also report gains over the same student on single-arm and bimanual real-robot tasks, with 30 trials per cell. A single RTX 5090 profile is reported at 32 ms and 1.86 GB. Results remain author-reported and task-specific; independent reproduction and broader robot/task coverage are open.

Evidence

  • Claimed — THAW-VLA trains a compact vision-language-action student with a feature-alignment loss against cached representations from a frozen world model; the teacher and projection head are removed at deployment.
  • Claimed — The authors report a 0.8B student improving LIBERO mean success from 95.3% to 97.9% and RoboCasa-GR1 success from 48.2% to 50.5%, against the same student trained without feature alignment.
  • Claimed — On two real-robot platforms and three manipulation tasks, the authors report 30 trials per cell and gains over the undistilled student; the 0.8B policy is reported at 32 ms per inference and 1.86 GB GPU memory on an RTX 5090.

Strongest baseline

The same student backbone trained on the same setup without the feature-alignment term. Comparisons with larger outside policies are contextual and not fully matched.

Limitations

  • All reported results are author-run and were not independently reproduced by this review.
  • The real-robot results cover two platforms, three manipulation tasks and 30 trials per cell; they do not establish broad transfer across robots, environments or task families.
  • Some table baselines are quoted from prior papers with different protocols; the strongest within-paper comparison is the same student without feature alignment.
  • The latency and memory figures are one RTX 5090 profile; energy, training cost and performance on other hardware are not established.
  • LIBERO simulation gains are 2.6 percentage points in the reported mean; task-level variation and benchmark saturation matter.
  • The reported LIBERO improvement is modest in absolute terms (95.3% to 97.9%) and comes from a high-performing benchmark regime; it does not establish broad robustness.
  • The project page notes remaining real-robot failures are placement errors and reports only selected pick-and-place/handover tasks.

What we still don't know

  • No independent reproduction of simulation, real-robot success, latency or memory measurements was conducted.
  • Generalization across unseen robot platforms, task families, visual conditions and deployment hardware is not measured in the reviewed result tables.
  • The additional offline compute and storage required to generate/cache world-model features are not included in the latency/memory comparison.

Why it matters

The method transfers a world model’s scene representation into a smaller deployed policy without loading the world model or adding test-time generation. If reproduced across tasks and hardware, this could preserve some world-model grounding while reducing deployment latency and memory. Current evidence is an author-run benchmark suite and a small set of real-robot tasks.
