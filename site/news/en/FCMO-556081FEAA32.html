What changed

A measurement study of 373,019 LLM judgments reports a hard limit on the resolution of single-judge pointwise benchmarks: increasing item count cannot remove system-by-judge variance. Pairwise scoring reduces that variance in the tested setup but introduces an 8.6-point presentation-order effect. Across the studied benchmarks, measured 95% floors of 0.41–1.24 points exceeded the median reported gain of 0.28 points; all 17 recovered MT-Bench gains in the matched comparison fell below that benchmark’s measured floor. These are author-reported findings, not an independent replication.

Evidence

  • Claimed — Across 373,019 judgments, the author reports that single-judge pointwise scoring has an item-count ceiling imposed by system-by-judge variance, while paired preference scoring reduces that variance but retains order bias: the same system wins 8.6 percentage points more often when presented first. Measured 95% floors were 0.41–1.24 points on a 0–5 scale, above a median reported improvement of 0.28 points. On the one repeatedly studied benchmark with an exact match, all 17 recovered MT-Bench improvements fell below its measured floor.
  • Demonstrated limitation — The reported floors and biases are author-reported and depend on the tested system panels, judges, rubrics and benchmark sets. They do not establish that every LLM-judged benchmark is unresolved or that pairwise evaluation with one judge is generally adequate.

Strongest baseline

Matched benchmark item sets, judges, rubric and scale across tested systems, with three repeated judgments; pairwise comparisons are presented in both orders.

Limitations

  • Results are author-reported and have not been independently reproduced.
  • The system panels vary across some benchmark sets, limiting unconditional cross-set comparisons.
  • Single-judge ceiling results concern pointwise rubric scoring; paired preference scoring changes the measurement problem but introduces presentation-order bias.
  • Several conclusions are based on a limited number of systems or item sets; transfer to all LLM-judged evaluations is not established.

What we still don't know

  • Independent replication of variance decomposition, benchmark floors and order-bias estimates on current judges and additional benchmark families is absent.

Why it matters

If the findings generalize, they show that many small LLM-judge benchmark gains cannot be resolved by simply adding more items, and that pairwise comparisons exchange one measurement problem for order bias. This could materially change benchmark design and claims; the evidence comes from the paper’s tested judges, systems and benchmark sets.
