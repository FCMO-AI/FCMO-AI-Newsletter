A controlled benchmark reports token-level fixes for language-model data attribution

A preprint introduces the Controlled Attribution Benchmark (CATT), which fine-tunes Qwen3.5-4B models on fictitious facts so the training documents provide known attribution ground truth. Across controlled settings, the authors report that redundancy and lexical distractors degrade gradient-based methods, while token-level negative log-odds gradients combined with top-k token aggregation improve fact-tracing recall by 35–72 percentage points. On FTRACE-TREX, the paper reports that negative log-odds improves counterfactual retraining performance for TRAK, EK-FAC and Grad-Dot by up to 4.9×. The tested tasks use single-token predictions and single-task LoRA fine-tuning; transfer to real pretraining attribution, multi-token outputs and multi-task learning remains unestablished.

Evidence

  • Demonstrated — The authors published arXiv:2610.40655v1 introducing the Controlled Attribution Benchmark (CATT), code/data availability, controlled task design and evaluation of gradient-based attribution methods.
  • Claimed — The authors report that redundancy and lexical distractors degrade attribution performance, and that token-level negative log-odds gradients with top-k token aggregation increase ground-truth recall by 35–72 percentage points across tested CATT settings.
  • Claimed — On FTRACE-TREX, the authors report that negative log-odds improves counterfactual retraining performance for TRAK, EK-FAC and Grad-Dot, with a maximum reported improvement of 4.9× for TRAK at the largest tested removal budget.
  • Not established — The paper does not establish that the reported improvements transfer to multi-token predictions, multi-task training, realistic large-scale pretraining attribution, copyright adjudication or general model-behavior tracing; the authors identify the first two as limitations.

Strongest baseline

The paper compares standard cross-entropy gradient and document-level aggregation against token-level negative log-odds and top-k aggregation, across gradient similarity, Fisher/influence, GGN-based and training-dynamics methods; BM25 retrieval is a strong lexical baseline on synthetic settings.

Limitations

  • All quantitative results are first-party preprint reports and have not been independently reproduced in this activation.
  • The diagnostic CATT task uses fictitious single-token facts, Qwen3.5-4B and single-task LoRA fine-tuning; it does not reproduce realistic pretraining attribution conditions.
  • The authors state that multi-token prediction and multi-task attribution are not evaluated, and top-k token aggregation introduces a hyperparameter without real-world ground-truth validation signals.
  • The authors note strong BM25 performance on some synthetic tasks and call for evaluations where surface lexical overlap is not a reliable shortcut.

What we still don't know

  • No independent reproduction of the CATT or FTRACE-TREX results was inspected, and linked code/data/checkpoints were not audited.
  • Transfer from single-token, single-task LoRA fact tracing to multi-token behavior, multi-task training and realistic pretraining corpora is unestablished.

Why it matters

If the reported effects replicate beyond the controlled benchmark, attribution systems used for training-data audits, copyright inquiries and model-behavior tracing may need token-level loss and aggregation choices that preserve localized evidence. This is a paper-reported method result, not proof that existing attribution systems are generally unreliable or that the correction transfers to large-scale pretraining.
