Paper gives a graph-based guarantee for integrating parallel agent edits

The paper formulates parallel agent edits as verified proposal integration: each proposed change is tested alone against a common starting artifact, and a dependency graph certifies which edits can be combined without interacting objective terms. It proves that fractional-coloring integration retains at least a 1/χ_f(G) fraction of total verified singleton improvement, with a matching worst-case bound for mergers restricted to selecting from the same proposals; geometric convergence follows under additional local-improvement and block-error assumptions. In structured experiments, fractional integration retained 922 units of graph-cut improvement versus 799 for a coloring fixed before proposals across 90 road-network comparisons, but adaptive ordinary coloring matched 922. A separate induced-conflict route-search schedule reduced measured wall time by a median 15% across 12 paired runs; route cost improved in four, tied in five and worsened in three. Results are author-reported and depend on explicit objective and dependency assumptions.

Evidence

  • Claimed — For a factorized, exactly evaluable objective and a sound known dependency certificate, the paper proves that fractional-coloring integration can retain at least a 1/χ_f(G) fraction of summed verified singleton gains; the bound is tight among mergers that select a subset of the same proposals.
  • Claimed — On 90 held-out road-network region–seed comparisons, fractional integration retained 922 graph-cut gain units versus 799 for a fixed pre-proposal coloring, while adaptive ordinary coloring also retained 922.
  • Claimed — In a vehicle-route scheduling experiment with deliberately induced conflicts, fractional scheduling was faster in all 12 paired runs, with a median wall-time reduction of 15%; route cost improved in four pairs, tied in five and worsened in three.
  • Not established — The results do not establish that the method improves general production agent workflows when dependency graphs are unknown, objectives are not exactly measurable, or stronger adaptive integration baselines are available.

Strongest baseline

Fixed ordinary coloring and merge-all/accept-if-improving baselines; adaptive ordinary coloring matches fractional integration on the road-network and timetable cases. In route scheduling, comparison is against a fixed ordinary-coloring schedule with equal candidate-move evaluations.

Limitations

  • The theorem assumes a known dependency certificate for the factorized objective and verified singleton gains measured against a common artifact.
  • Repeated geometric convergence uses additional local-improvement, assignment-coverage and block-error-bound assumptions.
  • In the road-network and timetable experiments, adaptive ordinary coloring matched fractional integration; the observed gain was over coloring fixed before proposals.
  • The route-search conflict graph was deliberately induced under exclusive route locks; immutable-snapshot parallel search with serialized commits was not compared.
  • All empirical results are paper-reported; no independent reproduction was performed in this review.

What we still don't know

  • The theorem and experiments were not independently reviewed or reproduced in this activation.
  • The method depends on a sound dependency certificate and an exactly evaluable objective; underestimating dependencies can incorrectly treat interacting edits as compatible.
  • On the road-network and timetabling tests, adaptive ordinary coloring matched fractional coloring; the route-search dependency conflicts were deliberately induced, and route cost worsened in three of twelve pairs.

Why it matters

Parallel agents can each improve a component yet break the shared solution when their edits are combined. This paper gives a formal way to reason about safe integration and the cost of coordination, while its conditions and adaptive-baseline results show where the reported gain does and does not generalize.
