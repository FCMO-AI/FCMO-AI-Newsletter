ProcessLight applies step-level process supervision to traffic-signal control

ProcessLight/ STeP-PO trains an LLM traffic-signal controller with process-level credit: it scores intermediate traffic claims against observations, estimates each step’s effect on the final phase by removing it, and merges semantically similar steps into a tree for relative advantage assignment. The authors report Qwen2.5-3B trained on one synthetic flow and evaluated zero-shot in CityFlow on five real traffic-flow datasets from Jinan and Hangzhou; the method leads on most reported metrics. On two datasets, the paper reports lower average travel time than outcome-only RL, and a five-seed Holm-corrected analysis finds 81 of 90 baseline comparisons significant in its favor. The appendix also reports Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-32B and Llama3-8B variants and an alternate teacher, with results varying by dataset and metric. All findings remain paper-reported CityFlow simulation results for the paper’s intersection setup, not on-road deployment evidence; this review did not independently rerun the code.

Evidence

  • Claimed — STeP-PO combines observation-grounded step-quality checks, counterfactual step-importance estimates and a semantic step tree to allocate process and outcome advantages to reasoning tokens during traffic-signal policy optimization.
  • Claimed — With Qwen2.5-3B trained on one synthetic traffic flow and evaluated zero-shot in CityFlow on five real traffic-flow datasets from Jinan and Hangzhou, the authors report that ProcessLight obtains the best result on most evaluated metrics among the compared traditional, RL and LLM methods.
  • Claimed — In an ablation on Jinan1 and Hangzhou1, the authors report mean travel time of 289.34 s and 314.99 s with STeP-PO, versus 305.74 s and 334.25 s with outcome-level reward alone. A five-seed analysis with global Holm correction reports 81 significant comparisons favoring ProcessLight among 90 comparisons.
  • Claimed — In appendix evaluations, the authors report ProcessLight results with Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-32B and Llama3-8B, and with an alternate Qwen3-Plus teacher; results vary by dataset and metric, so these tests do not establish broad scaling laws.
  • Claimed — The authors report reference-claim construction using 3k DeepSeek-V4-Pro cloud API calls, 7.07M input and 1.70M output tokens at $9.1, and step-importance precomputation using Qwen2.5-3B for 21k calls, 57.81M input tokens and 1.5 GPU-hours on one RTX PRO 6000 at $1.2; these are reported costs for two data-construction stages, not total training, inference or deployment cost.

Strongest baseline

Within the process-supervision ablation, warm-up plus outcome-level reward alone; broader table comparisons include conventional fixed-time/MaxPressure, RL controllers and other LLM-based methods.

Limitations

  • All results are reported by the authors and were not independently reproduced.
  • The evaluation runs in CityFlow simulation on real traffic-flow datasets, not on public roads or operational signal networks.
  • The traffic task uses standard four-way intersections and predefined reasoning steps; transfer to other network topologies or free-form reasoning is not established.
  • The 81/90 significance result aggregates many pairwise comparisons in two datasets; it does not establish real-world effect size.
  • The paper reports some additional backbone and teacher variants in its appendix, but this bounded set of evaluations does not establish broad model-size or training-budget scaling.
  • The paper itself identifies four-way topology and predefined-step reasoning as current design constraints and leaves more complex intersections for future work.
  • Simulation results do not establish performance, safety or traffic benefits in deployed intersections.

What we still don't know

  • No independent rerun of the public implementation or the CityFlow comparisons was performed.
  • No operational on-road deployment or evaluation beyond the paper’s four-way simulated setting was inspected.

Why it matters

The work applies step-level credit assignment to a structured decision process where intermediate claims can be checked against observed traffic state and the final control action has a clear simulator outcome. If the reported results reproduce, it offers a concrete process-supervision design for LLM policy optimization. Its demonstrated scope is limited to the paper’s simulator, datasets and four-way intersection setup.
