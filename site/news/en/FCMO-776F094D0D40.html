SemantEq uses proof-term auditing to test equivalence between formal mathematical statements

SemantEq defines a reference-based test for whether two Lean statements can be transformed into one another using constrained, auditable steps, and checks the proof terms that Lean verifies. In the authors’ ProofNetVerif test, their search program reports 100.0% precision under both published and author-adjudicated labels, while recall is 51.4% and 65.6%, respectively, on the 1,303 of 1,452 pairs that elaborated. The authors changed 102 labels from equivalent to non-equivalent. A second proof-audit program reports 330 of 407 equivalent pairs accepted and none of 104 non-equivalent pairs under its best prompt. These are paper-reported results. The method depends on a trusted formal reference and does not test whether that reference matches the original informal statement; independent reproduction and external label review remain open.

What changed

SemantEq defines a reference-based test for whether two Lean statements can be transformed into one another using constrained, auditable steps, and checks the proof terms that Lean verifies. In the authors’ ProofNetVerif test, their search program reports 100.0% precision under both published and author-adjudicated labels, while recall is 51.4% and 65.6%, respectively, on the 1,303 of 1,452 pairs that elaborated. The authors changed 102 labels from equivalent to non-equivalent. A second proof-audit program reports 330 of 407 equivalent pairs accepted and none of 104 non-equivalent pairs under its best prompt. These are paper-reported results. The method depends on a trusted formal reference and does not test whether that reference matches the original informal statement; independent reproduction and external label review remain open.

Evidence

  • Demonstrated — The authors posted the SemantEq preprint on arXiv on October 23, 2026, describing reference-based evaluation of equivalence between Lean formal statements.
  • Claimed — The authors define SemantEq as bidirectional reachability through allowed logical rearrangements, library equation or biconditional rewrites, variable changes and structural weakenings; their Lean programs inspect checked proof terms to reject proofs that do not use the reference faithfully.
  • Claimed — On 1,303 of 1,452 ProofNetVerif test pairs that elaborated under the authors’ toolchain, SemantEq-Eval reports zero false acceptances and 65.6% recall against author-adjudicated labels; Table 1 reports 51.4% recall with the original published labels, with 100.0% precision under both label sets. A ported BEq+ baseline reports 95.8% precision and 61.8% recall under adjudicated labels; the paper reports the same ranking under published labels.
  • Claimed — On a 511-pair certification set with 407 author-labelled equivalent and 104 non-equivalent pairs, SemantEq-ProofAudit reports that its best prompt certified 330 equivalent pairs and accepted none of the non-equivalent pairs; GPT-5.6 Sol generated one proof attempt per pair and prompt.
  • Not established — The method compares a candidate formal statement with a trusted formal reference; it does not establish that either formal statement faithfully captures the original informal mathematical source.

Strongest baseline

The authors port BEq+ to the same Lean/Mathlib toolchain and score it on the same elaborating ProofNetVerif test pairs.

Limitations

  • All performance values are author-reported; no independent reproduction was completed.
  • The main test result scores only the 1,303 of 1,452 pairs that elaborated under the authors’ toolchain.
  • The authors changed 102 test labels after review, all from equivalent to non-equivalent; most changes were prompted by system disagreement, and all final decisions were author-made.
  • SemantEq evaluates agreement with a trusted formal reference, not fidelity to the informal source text; errors in the reference can pass through.
  • The certification set contains 407 equivalent and 104 non-equivalent pairs; zero false acceptances in this set does not establish zero error in other domains.
  • ProofAudit was evaluated with one GPT-5.6 Sol proof-generation session per pair and prompt style, so repeat-run variation was not measured.
  • Table 1 reports 100.0% precision under both original published labels and author-adjudicated labels, but recall changes from 51.4% to 65.6%; the difference reflects the authors’ 102 label changes and does not show external agreement.

What we still don't know

  • No independent reproduction or independent adjudication of the benchmark results was found. The paper states that 102 of 1,303 scored test pairs were relabelled from equivalent to non-equivalent after author review; 88 reviews were prompted by the systems’ disagreement pattern and 14 by a model screen, with final decisions made by people.
  • The pinned Lean 4.30.0-rc1 code and data repository was inspected, but this activation did not build or run it.
  • The method requires a trusted formal reference and does not assess whether that reference matches the informal source statement. Generalization beyond ProofNetVerif and the small author-assembled certification set is unmeasured.

Why it matters

Automated theorem proving can verify a proof against a formal statement while missing whether that statement preserves the intended claim. SemantEq targets that gap by checking constrained transformations in both directions and auditing the proof terms. If the reported benchmark results hold, this could make autoformalization evaluation less vulnerable to true-but-wrong statements; the evidence is limited to formal references, author-reviewed labels and small test sets.
