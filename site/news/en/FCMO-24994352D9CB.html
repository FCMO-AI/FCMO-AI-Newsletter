A controlled Gemma 4 study finds learning-rate-sensitive gains in small-model negotiation

Tabacof and Joglekar report a controlled GRPO study of four Gemma 4 seller checkpoints (2.3B–31B effective parameters) in bilateral multi-issue bargaining. With one shared 1e-6 learning rate and 104 steps, author-reported gains over each base checkpoint rise from +0.001 (95% interval −0.019 to +0.021; not significant) at 2.3B to +0.023 at 4.5B, +0.063 at 12B and +0.078 at 31B; each arm has one training run. At early checkpoints, a 3× learning rate improves over the shared rate at all sizes, while after 104 shared-rate steps the difference is not detectable at 12B or 31B. The study does not establish a scaling law: size and architecture co-vary, training is not FLOP-matched, and the authors report only two held-out buyers and one underpowered held-out domain. These are paper-reported results in one synthetic negotiation environment, not evidence of commercial transfer.

Claim vs. evidence

  • CLAIMED — Under the authors’ shared 1e-6 learning-rate, 104-step setup, the reported GRPO gain over base rises from +0.001 at 2.3B to +0.078 at 31B; the 2.3B result is not significant and each size has one training run.
  • CLAIMED — At early checkpoints, the authors report that tripling the learning rate improves the seller score over the shared-rate checkpoint at every tested size; after 104 shared-rate steps the difference is not detectable at 12B and 31B.
  • CLAIMED — The observed size pattern does not isolate parameter-count effects because architecture changes between the 4.5B E-series and 12B conventional checkpoints, compute is not FLOP-matched, and there is one training seed per arm.
  • CLAIMED — The authors report that the 10×-rate 2.3B arm’s pooled reward gain concentrates on the evaluation buyer from the training buyer’s model family and is accompanied by frequent JSON-fragment leakage into buyer-visible prose.
  • DEMONSTRATED_LIMITATION_OF_SOURCE — The study does not demonstrate commercial negotiation transfer, throughput, energy use, or serving-cost advantage.

Strongest baseline

Within each size, the instruction-tuned Gemma 4 base model under the same evaluation configuration. The paper also compares with two frontier model sellers, but decoding and service differ; it includes no scripted seller baseline.

The caveat that matters

  • All performance numbers are author-reported and not independently reproduced by the research desk.
  • Each size/rate arm is trained once; scenario bootstrap intervals do not estimate training-run variance.
  • Model size and architecture co-vary between the E-series 2.3B/4.5B checkpoints and conventional 12B/31B checkpoints; matched steps are not matched FLOPs.
  • Evaluation uses two held-out buyer models, only one outside the training buyers’ model family, and one underpowered held-out domain.
  • The task is a synthetic, bilateral, seller-side bargaining environment with a single issue schema; no live procurement, marketplace or customer outcome is measured.
  • Authors report no measured wall-clock GPU-hours, throughput, latency, energy or serving cost; no scripted baseline seller is included.

What remains unknown

  • No independent multi-seed reproduction across model sizes and buyer families.
  • Transfer from the synthetic bilateral task to live procurement, marketplaces, or multi-party negotiation is untested.
  • Authors did not report metered training FLOPs/GPU-hours or serving throughput, latency, energy, and cost.

FCMO Lens

The study shows why post-training comparisons on agent tasks need tuned learning rates, multiple training seeds and adversarially varied counterparties: a fixed-rate size trend can mix model capacity, architecture, compute and optimization, while pooled reward can conceal buyer-family-specific behavior. Its bounded results give a useful design baseline for negotiation-agent evaluation, but they do not establish that smaller models can replace frontier systems in procurement or marketplaces.
