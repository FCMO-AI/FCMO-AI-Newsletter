What changed

A September 18, 2026 preprint introduces Designer-RSI, a method for adapting an agent through an external procedural skill library while leaving model weights fixed. In five reported rounds over 1,406 design briefs, the library grew from 76 seeded skills to 139. The authors report that widening the library and revising existing skills worked better together than either alone on 200 held-out briefs (58.5% versus 49.4% and 48.6% win rates against the no-skill agent; p=0.025). Results span three backbones and design benchmarks, but some individual metrics regress. The replay gate protects only the sampled cases; the user-derived prompts and full internal evaluation set are not public, and no independent reproduction or human preference study is reported.

Evidence

  • Demonstrated — The arXiv preprint exists and describes a continual-adaptation system for a professional graphic-design agent: a frozen language model uses an external natural-language skill bank that is widened with recurring uncovered procedures and deepened by revising failure-prone procedures, with candidate changes checked through matched replay.
  • Claimed — The authors report five evolution rounds over 1,406 briefs and 1,869 automatically graded trajectories, with no model-weight updates or human reward labels; the skill bank grew from 76 documentation-derived skills to 139. On 200 held-out briefs, widening and deepening alone yielded 49.4% and 48.6% win rates over the no-skill agent, while the combined system yielded 58.5% (reported p=0.025). These are preprint-reported results, not independently reproduced here.
  • Claimed — Across the paper’s reported benchmark suite, the evolved skill bank improved overall pairwise win rates over the no-skill agent on the four specialized graphic-design benchmarks for Claude Opus 4.6 (67.6%) and Claude Sonnet 4 (61.8%), and average generation-quality scores across the three tested backbones; individual benchmark metrics regressed in some conditions, including Qwen3.6-27B execution success on DPG-Bench.
  • Claimed — The authors’ replay gate admits a proposed skill change only when sampled paired replays show at least one improvement and no detected regression; the authors explicitly caution that this local gate does not guarantee monotonic gains across the full deployment distribution.

Strongest baseline

Same agent, tools, renderer and evaluator with skill retrieval disabled; for rewrites, matched replay against the incumbent version; for new skills, matched replay against the no-skill agent.

Limitations

  • This is a single author-reported preprint; the research desk did not independently reproduce its results or audit the code/artifacts.
  • The system is a professional graphic-design agent with a particular software/tool stack and tested model set; transfer to other agent tasks and domains is unestablished.
  • The offline evolution data include pre-existing user traffic and LLM-augmented variants. Appendix K says user-derived prompts and trajectories are not released, and the full internal held-out prompt set is withheld; independent replication of the principal experiment is therefore constrained.
  • Most outcomes are graded by automated multimodal judges rather than user studies; the authors plan a future small-scale user study. Some benchmark metrics regress, and the replay gate protects only the finite sampled replay set, not the full deployment distribution.
  • The paper reports modest added prompt-token use for retrieval and low measured latency overhead in its setup, but does not establish product-scale economics, user satisfaction, or durable production impact.

What we still don't know

  • No independent reproduction or accessible user-traffic/internal prompt set was established; the main outcome is largely automated-judge evaluation.
  • The evidence does not establish whether local no-regression replay gating provides adequate protection under distribution shift, repeated rounds, or benchmarks with more objective success criteria.

Why it matters

If reproduced beyond this design system, an external skill memory with a conservative change gate could offer a practical way to adapt deployed agents without fine-tuning model weights. The paper gives a concrete tested pattern for balancing wider task coverage against regressions, while leaving the key transfer, judge reliability and deployment-distribution questions open.
