In brief · Agents and memory

Preprint reports predictable failures in rarity- and relevance-based memory for LLM group decisions

The arXiv preprint introduces CrossProfile, a diagnostic with 60 main templates and 480 ownership variants that keeps fact wording fixed while varying which agents hold supporting, decoy and irrelevant information. Across Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B and exploratory hosted DeepSeek runs, the authors report that rarity-first and relevance-first memory heuristics can reverse performance depending on who holds decoy evidence. In the classic hidden-profile condition, relevance-first selection lowers accuracy by about 20–22 points for the two local models, and removing or randomizing holder-attribution tags raises accuracy by about 13–14 points. The study uses constructed tasks and a mechanically defined answer key; it does not estimate failure rates in deployed teams.

Evidence

  • Demonstrated — The authors submitted arXiv:2610.37624v1 on 27 October 2026, presenting CrossProfile, a diagnostic of how LLM group-decision memory policies select evidence.
  • Claimed — The paper reports that CrossProfile uses 60 main templates, each with eight ownership variants (480 tasks), holding fact wording and roles fixed while crossing which agents hold support, decoy and irrelevant facts. Its mechanically checked answer key reflects a fixed additive scoring rule that is not stated in the model-facing prompt.
  • Claimed — Across Qwen2.5-7B and Llama-3.1-8B, the authors report that rarity-first selection is approximately tied with random selection when averaged across ownership variants, while their relative performance reverses sharply depending on whether supporting or decoy facts are private versus shared.
  • Claimed — In the classic condition with private support, shared decoys and private irrelevant facts, relevance-first selection lowers reported accuracy versus rarity-first by 22.2 points for Qwen and 20.0 points for Llama; an oracle relevance filter also loses 23.3 and 31.1 points, respectively. These are results on constructed tasks, not deployed decision outcomes.
  • Claimed — In the classic constructed condition, the authors report that removing or randomly reassigning contributor-attribution tags while holding line order fixed raises accuracy by roughly 13–14 points on Qwen and Llama. This supports sensitivity to social-proof cues in this prompt setup, not a general claim about real groups.

Strongest baseline

Random-order selection for average policy performance and first-mention selection for ownership-reversal contrasts; all-facts and empty memories are additional controls.

Limitations

  • The benchmark consists of constructed tasks with mechanically derived keys; the prompt does not state the additive scoring rule, so accuracy measures agreement with the constructed key rather than externally adjudicated decision quality.
  • Only four model variants are evaluated; DeepSeek-Pro uses 30 templates, and hosted-model runs are described as exploratory.
  • Ownership changes when a fact first appears, contributor tags and token cost as well as selection priority; the paper adds controls, but transfer to natural team discussion remains uncertain.
  • No independent reproduction, external answer-key validation, real-world team study or deployed failure rate was established.

What we still don't know

  • The CrossProfile effects and model outputs are author-reported and were not independently reproduced.
  • The mechanically defined answer key is not an external measure of real group decision quality, and transfer to naturally occurring deliberation is not established.

Why it matters

If independently reproduced beyond this constructed benchmark, memory selection and attribution cues could change how multi-agent teams preserve and weigh evidence. The paper shows why average accuracy can conceal large ownership-dependent failures, while its synthetic task key and narrow model regime limit transfer.
