What changed

An arXiv preprint analyzes 259 papers reporting agentic-security attack success rates from a query-retrieved English-language arXiv corpus. The authors report that 58.0% of a seeded hand-coded sample of 50 papers (95% Wilson CI 44.2–70.6) reported neither a variance estimate nor repeated runs; revised automated detectors estimate 65.3% across all 259. They identify six choices that can change what an ASR measures, including evaluation unit, success oracle, attacker adaptivity, and defense knowledge. The paper argues these differences can make cross-paper values incomparable. It does not claim any individual paper is wrong, and its ranking-reversal estimates are analytical rather than observed defense comparisons.

Evidence

  • Claimed — In the authors’ query-retrieved arXiv corpus of 259 papers reporting quantitative agent-security ASR, 58.0% of a seeded hand-coded random sample of 50 reported neither a variance estimate nor repeated runs (95% Wilson CI 44.2–70.6); revised automated detectors estimated 65.3% across all 259.
  • Claimed — The paper identifies six design choices that can alter ASR meaning and argues that four can change defense rankings in system-dependent ways. Its analysis estimates that, under its assumptions, a 100-instance benchmark has an 18.2 percentage-point minimum detectable difference at 80% power and that a single-run evaluation reverses a true five-point difference about 21% of the time.
  • Not established — The paper does not establish that any named defense is ineffective or that observed agent-security papers actually have incorrect rankings; the ranking reversal results are analytical and no defense was run end to end.

Strongest baseline

For the paper’s reporting-practice comparison, revised full-text detector estimates are cross-checked against the random hand-coded sample for two constructs; no empirical competing ASR standard or end-to-end defense comparison is tested.

Limitations

  • The corpus is query-retrieved, arXiv-only and English-only; 15 screened papers without HTML were omitted.
  • The two headline constructs receive hand-coding on a random sample of 50; all adjudication is single-coder, and kappa measures detector agreement against one adjudicator.
  • 88 other papers mention ASR without a detected numeral; 26 show possible HTML-to-text numeral loss, so the included population may be 259–347. Independence of this artifact from reporting practice is unverified.
  • Most additional detector rates have precision estimates from small match samples and recall is unmeasured; the authors caution these may be lower bounds.
  • Ranking calculations are analytical under independent-unit assumptions and no defenses were run end to end; real benchmark dependence can reduce effective sample size.
  • The proposed reporting checklist has not been validated to improve comparability.

What we still don't know

  • No independent reconstruction of the 12-query corpus or blinded recoding of the 50-paper sample was conducted.
  • The analysis does not test whether observed agent-security defense rankings change under repeated matched evaluations.
  • The checklist is proposed but its effect on reporting quality or cross-paper comparability is not measured.

Why it matters

Security teams and researchers often compare ASR numbers as if they measured the same outcome. If the reported omissions are common, headline percentages may not establish which agent defense is stronger; reporting units, oracles, attacker budgets, stochastic variance and benign utility would make comparisons more interpretable.
