Controlled tests show decision-model reliability depends on rule conflict, wording, and supplied information

A 2026-10-21 arXiv paper proposes controlled tests of decision models using executable arithmetic rules, crossed rule/state cases, equivalent wording changes and manipulated value tables. The authors report that transfer gains depend on agreement with source examples; under fully conflicting rule quartets, results differ in sign across two source splits and joint correctness is low. In tests of TypeSafe's Jev service, answers change more across equivalent wordings than across identical-text repeats and often follow altered table values or mappings. These are author-reported results on synthetic finite-domain tasks and one service version; code and data are stated as forthcoming, and this activation did not independently reproduce the analyses.

Evidence

  • Demonstrated — An arXiv v1 paper submitted on 2026-10-21 describes an oracle-checked evaluation using finite-domain arithmetic rules, crossed rule/state tests, equivalent rewrites, and controlled changes to external value tables and mappings.
  • Claimed — The authors report that adaptation gains rise with nearest-source answer agreement, while fully conflicting rule quartets produce low joint correctness and opposite accuracy changes across two source splits (−6.10 and +8.88 percentage points). The fully conflicting cohorts contain 39 and 32 quartet memberships, respectively.
  • Claimed — For the tested TypeSafe Jev service version, the authors report more answer changes across equivalent wordings than across identical-text repetitions, and frequent shifts toward answers implied by altered supplied tables or mappings.
  • Not established — The paper does not establish transfer to natural-language rules, real-world or high-stakes decisions, other decision services or service versions, or independently reproduced behavioral effects.

Strongest baseline

Within-paper paired comparisons use fixed questions and model settings, oracle labels, two source splits and repeated requests for the Jev wording analysis. They are author-run results, not independent evaluation.

Limitations

  • Results concern a fixed-depth arithmetic grammar over bounded integer states and do not establish natural-language rule-following.
  • The full-conflict cohorts are small and have different task coverage; opposite split gains are retained rather than averaged into a general conclusion.
  • The Jev experiment covers service version jev-1.13.0 during one observation window; the authors say this is not evidence about other versions or periods.
  • Changing answers toward manipulated table targets demonstrates response sensitivity to supplied information, not an internal decision mechanism.
  • The results are not independent reproduction or evidence of consequential-decision reliability.

What we still don't know

  • The paper's analyses and service experiments have not been independently reproduced; its abstract says code and data will be made available but the inspected v1 has no linked repository.
  • Transfer from synthetic arithmetic rules to natural-language policies and other decision services is unknown.

Why it matters

Aggregate accuracy can hide whether a model follows the stated rule, responds to the observed state, or uses supplied values consistently. These controlled diagnostics may improve evaluation design, but this paper's synthetic scope does not establish reliability in actual policy, legal or operational decisions.
