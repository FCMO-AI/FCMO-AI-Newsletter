Paper reports claim-level workflow for multilingual continual document updates

The Knowledge Pull Requests (KPR) paper proposes an update workflow that extracts claims from new sources, classifies and routes them to document sections, flags conflicts, and records a claim-level changelog alongside the text diff. On cross-lingual Wikipedia revision and three constructed RAGTIME update settings, the authors report better information integration and retention than raw-text rewriting, claim-only rewriting, and scratch regeneration in the evaluated conditions. Their QA evaluation reports that KPR-edited articles provide a stronger grounding source than the original article, closed-book answers, or web search in cases where relevant knowledge appears only in other-language sources.

What changed

The Knowledge Pull Requests (KPR) paper proposes an update workflow that extracts claims from new sources, classifies and routes them to document sections, flags conflicts, and records a claim-level changelog alongside the text diff. On cross-lingual Wikipedia revision and three constructed RAGTIME update settings, the authors report better information integration and retention than raw-text rewriting, claim-only rewriting, and scratch regeneration in the evaluated conditions. Their QA evaluation reports that KPR-edited articles provide a stronger grounding source than the original article, closed-book answers, or web search in cases where relevant knowledge appears only in other-language sources.

Evidence

  • Demonstrated — The authors define KPR as a pipeline that extracts claims, checks them against an existing document, routes new claims to sections, flags conflicts, and records a changelog separately from the text diff.
  • Claimed — In the paper’s cross-lingual Wikipedia revision evaluation, KPR achieved higher added-information precision and recall than the evaluated raw-text and claim-only rewrite baselines; the authors report an InfoP of 0.878 and added-information recall of 0.891 in Table 2.
  • Claimed — Across three constructed RAGTIME update settings, the paper reports KPR with the highest recall of old and newly introduced information among the rewrite methods, while flagging contradictory incoming claims rather than silently resolving them.
  • Demonstrated — The authors state that their pipeline performs multiple LLM calls and that edit-cost metrics measure resulting text changes, not the compute used to generate them; the quality evaluation uses an LLM judge.

Strongest baseline

ConText rewrites using relevant raw sources; ConClaim uses extracted claims without the KPR claim-review step; Scratch regenerates in the RAGTIME setting.

Limitations

  • All performance results are reported by the paper authors and were not independently reproduced.
  • Quality metrics use an LLM judge and benchmark-specific relevance judgments.
  • The RAGTIME settings are constructed two-round variants and do not evaluate retrieval itself.
  • The pipeline uses multiple LLM calls; reported edit size does not measure total inference cost.

What we still don't know

  • Independent replication on held-out multilingual and time-varying documents with a preregistered evaluator and matched baselines.

Why it matters

If sustained, claim-level updates could improve the reliability of agent-maintained knowledge repositories by making additions, conflicts and supporting sources reviewable.
