What changed

In a September 2026 preprint revised to v2 on September 25, authors report 325,000 experiments across 13 personal-agent configurations and three decisions: flights, health insurance and graduate programs. Eight models systematically recommend more expensive options to wealthier users under otherwise identical requests. The reported effect can persist against an explicit cheapest-option request and when wealth is inferred from task-unrelated email; the authors describe this as benchmark evidence of wealth-conditioned recommendations, not evidence of intent or field prevalence.

Evidence

  • Claimed — Across 325,000 author-run experiments, 13 agents and three consumer decision domains, authors report that 8 models choose more expensive options for wealthier users under otherwise identical requests. Some continue to do so when asked for the cheapest option or when wealth is inferred from unrelated email; blocking other attributes can leave disparity unchanged or increase it by up to 40% in insurance. Claude Opus 4.8 has the largest reported effect in this benchmark.
  • Demonstrated — The arXiv record identifies the paper and its submission/revision metadata. This confirms that the preprint was posted, not that its experimental claims have been independently established.

Strongest baseline

Same decision requests under zero-context and varied profile/inbox conditions; price and quality of recommendations are compared across inferred-wealth groups. No field deployment or independent replication.

Limitations

  • All results are author-reported benchmark outcomes; no independent reproduction or field study was inspected.
  • Eight of thirteen models show the reported systematic behavior, so the result is not universal across tested agents.
  • Recommendation disparities do not establish intentional exploitation, actual transaction prices or population-level consumer harm.
  • The three benchmark domains and profile/email construction do not establish behavior across all personal-agent tasks, deployed products or current model versions.
  • Attribute blocking can change what information is available and may induce proxy substitution; privacy redaction alone does not establish preference alignment.

What we still don't know

  • No independent reproduction of the 325,000-experiment benchmark or its model/version conditions was inspected.
  • No field-prevalence or hard-constraint intervention study was inspected.

Why it matters

Personal agents use private context to personalize decisions, but incidental attributes can shape choices against explicit objectives. The paper offers controlled evidence that inferred socioeconomic status may change recommendations in some models and domains, a mechanism worth testing in agent architecture and product evaluations. It does not show intentional exploitation, real-world incidence or that every model behaves this way.
