WeatherNext 3 moves operational global AI forecasting toward raw observations, hourly refresh and high-resolution probabilistic outputs

Google Research and Google DeepMind report and operate WeatherNext 3, a global probabilistic weather model that augments conventional atmospheric analyses with low-latency geostationary satellite observations, produces a new forecast every hour, predicts single-level variables at hourly 0.1-degree resolution, and directly models observation-derived precipitation, sparse station measurements and cyclone attributes. In year-long 2024 evaluations it beats WeatherNext 2 on all tested surface variables and most upper-level analysis targets, while observation-based heads show large short-lead improvements against held-out stations and multiple precipitation ground truths. A six-week quasi-real-time 2026 comparison reports roughly 10% average first-week upper-level CRPS improvement versus ECMWF AIFS ENS v2. The advance is operationally meaningful because it begins collapsing data assimilation, forecasting and post-processing into a single learned system that can exploit observations newer than the latest global analysis state. The paper also documents important limits: some baseline/ground-truth mismatches, small real-time sample duration, under-spread cyclone ensembles, mesh artifacts and temporal discontinuities, no extreme-precipitation validation, and one poorly performing output withheld from operations.

What actually changed

Google Research and Google DeepMind report and operate WeatherNext 3, a global probabilistic weather model that augments conventional atmospheric analyses with low-latency geostationary satellite observations, produces a new forecast every hour, predicts single-level variables at hourly 0.1-degree resolution, and directly models observation-derived precipitation, sparse station measurements and cyclone attributes. In year-long 2024 evaluations it beats WeatherNext 2 on all tested surface variables and most upper-level analysis targets, while observation-based heads show large short-lead improvements against held-out stations and multiple precipitation ground truths. A six-week quasi-real-time 2026 comparison reports roughly 10% average first-week upper-level CRPS improvement versus ECMWF AIFS ENS v2. The advance is operationally meaningful because it begins collapsing data assimilation, forecasting and post-processing into a single learned system that can exploit observations newer than the latest global analysis state. The paper also documents important limits: some baseline/ground-truth mismatches, small real-time sample duration, under-spread cyclone ensembles, mesh artifacts and temporal discontinuities, no extreme-precipitation validation, and one poorly performing output withheld from operations.

Claim vs. evidence

  • DEMONSTRATED — WeatherNext 3 directly ingests low-latency geostationary satellite observations and produces a new global forecast every hour, while predicting hourly 0.1-degree single-level variables.
  • DEMONSTRATED — In 2024 evaluation WN3 outperforms WN2 on all tested surface variables and most upper-level targets, while observation heads substantially improve held-out station and precipitation skill.
  • DEMONSTRATED — Hourly initialization supplies roughly 2-3 hours of latency-adjusted precipitation lead-time gain versus six-hour initialization, consistent with the fresher observational input rather than purely extra learned forecast skill.
  • DEMONSTRATED — During a six-week July-August 2026 quasi-real-time comparison, WN3 reports roughly 10% average first-week upper-level CRPS improvement versus ECMWF AIFS ENS v2 and superior performance on most tested variables and lead times.
  • DEMONSTRATED — WN3 sample quality is not uniformly superior: cyclone ensembles are more under-spread than WN2, mesh artifacts and six-hour-boundary discontinuities are visible, extreme precipitation remains unevaluated, and one cdir output was withheld because its skill was anomalously poor.
  • INFERRED — Observation-conditioned global AI models can begin replacing separate assimilation/forecast/calibration stages for some operational outputs, but the current evidence does not justify eliminating physics-based or human meteorological systems.

Strongest baseline

WeatherNext 2 is the prior AI probabilistic SOTA baseline for comparable analysis outputs; ECMWF ENS is the physics-based ensemble baseline and ECMWF AIFS ENS v2 is the leading operational AI baseline in the six-week 2026 comparison. Observation-ground-truth evaluations use held-out stations, MRMS and rain gauges to reduce dependence on the same analysis products used for training.

The caveat that matters

  • The core performance evidence is produced by the model's developers; independent operational evaluation of WN3 was not recovered in this pass.
  • The quasi-real-time comparison spans only July 1-August 11 2026; the authors explicitly warn against over-interpreting smaller differences from six weeks of weather.
  • Some AIFS/ENS comparisons use regridding, different sources or different ground truths, which the paper says can slightly penalize baselines or favor WN3 on particular single-level analyses.
  • WN3 cyclone ensembles are more under-spread than WN2 for intensity and extent, potentially from overfitting with the larger model.
  • Individual ensemble samples show mesh-shaped spatial artifacts and some sparse/precipitation heads show discontinuities across outer six-hour boundaries; ensemble summary statistics mask much of this but do not remove the underlying joint-distribution issue.
  • Extreme precipitation is not robustly evaluated because true positives are too sparse; headline precipitation gains are capped to moderate accumulations in several metrics.
  • One direct-radiation output (cdir) showed anomalously poor skill during development and is excluded from operations/evaluation.
  • WeatherNext outputs are experimental and are not a replacement for official severe-weather warnings.
  • WN3 is not superior at every nominal lead time: some variables show exceptions around six- and occasionally twelve-hour leads.
  • Cyclone track/intensity gains over WN2 are described as relatively small, and the newer model is more under-spread on intensity/extent.
  • Higher marginal CRPS skill can coexist with unrealistic spatial/temporal sample artifacts because the training objective does not directly optimize the full joint covariance structure.

What remains unknown

  • Independent meteorological verification of WN3's 2026 operational forecasts is missing from this record.
  • Extreme precipitation and other rare-event tails are not established by the current evaluation.
  • Joint-distribution artifacts remain despite strong marginal CRPS.
  • Full training/inference economics and energy costs were not recovered.

FCMO Lens

AI weather systems have already challenged physics-based forecasting on medium-range skill, but most global models still inherit the cadence, latency and biases of analysis products produced by traditional numerical pipelines. WeatherNext 3 attacks that dependency directly. If its reported skill and operational robustness persist, global learned forecasting is moving from 'fast emulator of an analysis-to-forecast pipeline' toward a multimodal observation-conditioned world model that updates more often, predicts at operational resolution and folds some calibration/post-processing into the same system. That is consequential for disaster warning, energy, agriculture, logistics and the broader scientific question of whether learned systems can absorb traditionally separate simulation-pipeline stages without losing reliability.
