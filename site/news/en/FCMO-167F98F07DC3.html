A preprint separates long-horizon stability from changed-law transfer in physical world models

Wang et al. report a double dissociation in learned physical simulators: evolving a learned energy with a symplectic update keeps rollouts bounded for up to 100× the training horizon in tested conservative systems, while an explicit linear factorization of a physical coupling enables transfer to a never-trained sign of that coupling. Each structure alone fails the other target. Matched controls, including tuned neural ODEs, energy penalties, stronger architectures and domain randomization, probe alternative explanations. The paper also maps boundaries: conservation is the wrong prior under genuine dissipation, and an incorrect assumed coupling form can fit the training regime yet fail under intervention. All evidence is from synthetic simulators and rendered observations; independent reproduction and real-video or physical deployment remain open.

Claim vs. evidence

  • CLAIMED — The authors report that a symplectic model evolving learned energy remained finite across 192 tested episodes to 2000 steps (100× the training horizon), while matched unstructured, energy-penalty and tuned neural-ODE controls diverged or showed larger long-horizon energy drift.
  • CLAIMED — For a softened three-body gravity model trained only on attractive coupling G>0, the authors report that an explicitly factored linear coupling achieved regime match 1 across tested repulsive values G in {-0.5,-1.0,-1.5}, while a matched non-factored symplectic model stayed bounded but had regime match 0.
  • CLAIMED — In a matched ablation, the authors report a double dissociation: conservation supports stability without changed-law inversion, and coupling factorization supports inversion without stability; the combination achieved both in the tested model.
  • CLAIMED — The authors report explicit boundaries: conservation is unsuitable for genuinely dissipative systems, while factoring can fail under an incorrect assumed coupling form even when in-distribution fit appears good.
  • CLAIMED — The paper states that its evidence comes from synthetic simulators and rendered observations, not real video or physical deployment.

Strongest baseline

Matched unstructured predictor, physical-energy-penalty predictor, tuned neural ODE, and non-factored symplectic model; the paper also tests stronger relational architectures and domain randomization.

The caveat that matters

  • Results are author-reported; no independent code execution or reproduction was performed.
  • Evidence comes from synthetic physical simulators and rendered observations, not real video or physical deployment.
  • Energy conservation is the wrong prior for genuinely dissipative systems; the authors model dissipation separately.
  • Counterfactual transfer depends on the supplied coupling functional form and can fail silently when misspecified.
  • Pixel-grounded results still rely on constructed systems, specified anchors or learned object binding; joint learned detection and binding remain open.

What remains unknown

  • No independent execution or reproduction of the simulations and matched ablations was performed.
  • Results are from synthetic simulators and rendered observations; real-video and measured physical-system transfer is unestablished.
  • The counterfactual prior assumes the correct functional form; misspecified coupling can fail without an in-distribution warning.

FCMO Lens

Physical world models face different failure modes: prediction errors can destabilize long rollouts, while interventions can expose a model that simply continues the training law. The paper argues those targets require distinct structural commitments and shows how each can fail outside its assumptions. This sharpens evaluation and modeling choices, while synthetic evidence and the risk of prior misspecification limit deployment claims.
