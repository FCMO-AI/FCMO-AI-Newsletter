Working paper finds AI-native startups have lower entry-level shares and flatter structures

Harvard Business School Working Paper 26-090 analyzes U.S. venture-backed startup workforce data linked to Y Combinator cohorts and PitchBook records. The authors report entry-level employee shares 4 percentage points lower in the Y Combinator sample (2,233 firms) and 6 points lower in the broader PitchBook sample (41,214 firms). Adjusted firm-size estimates are about 25% smaller for Y Combinator AI-tagged firms and 12% smaller for PitchBook; estimated hierarchy differences are about 0.5 fewer levels and 0.146 fewer levels, respectively. AI status comes from founders’ public tags for Y Combinator and a text classifier for PitchBook; the workforce records end in January 2025. This cross-sectional draft does not identify whether AI caused the organizational differences or imply aggregate job losses: founder selection, product design, startup entry and other firm differences remain possible explanations.

Evidence

  • Claimed — In two U.S. venture-backed startup samples, the authors report AI-native/AI-tagged firms have lower entry-level workforce shares than comparison firms: 4 percentage points in the Y Combinator sample (2,233 firms) and 6 points in the broader PitchBook sample (41,214 firms), using cohort/industry or deal-quarter/industry fixed effects.
  • Claimed — The authors report AI-tagged startups are about 25% smaller and have roughly 0.5 fewer hierarchy levels in the Y Combinator sample; in PitchBook, the adjusted estimates are about 12% smaller and 0.146 fewer hierarchy levels. These are cross-sectional associations, not identified causal effects of AI adoption.

Strongest baseline

Comparisons use batch-by-industry fixed effects for Y Combinator and deal-quarter-by-industry fixed effects for PitchBook. These comparisons adjust for stated cohort/industry timing but do not remove unobserved selection or establish causality.

Limitations

  • This is an author-reported working paper explicitly distributed in draft form for discussion; no independent audit was performed.
  • The Y Combinator measure uses founders' public AI tags; PitchBook AI probability is predicted from text and may introduce classification or composition effects.
  • Cross-sectional estimates cannot establish that AI caused the differences; product architecture, internal efficiency and founder selection are competing explanations.
  • The venture-backed U.S. startup sample is selective, and Revelio workforce records extend only through January 2025.
  • Smaller employment per AI-native firm does not establish lower aggregate employment; the paper explicitly discusses startup entry as a possible offset.
  • For PitchBook, the AI classifier achieves 77.5% held-out accuracy and 55.2% recall according to the paper; imperfect classification may affect estimates. Adjusted size and hierarchy effects differ materially between the Y Combinator and PitchBook samples.

What we still don't know

  • No independent replication or raw microdata audit of the reported workforce estimates was conducted.
  • AI status is self-identified in the Y Combinator sample and classifier-predicted from text in PitchBook; the authors report held-out PitchBook-classifier accuracy of 77.5% and recall of 55.2%. Cross-sectional design and classification error leave founder, funding, product and firm-selection differences unresolved.
  • Revelio workforce records cover through January 2025 and the study concerns U.S. venture-backed startups; persistence during later scaling and transfer to other employers/sectors are unknown.
  • Firm-level employment differences do not determine aggregate labor demand because AI-enabled startup entry or growth could offset lower staffing per firm.

Why it matters

The paper adds a firm-level organizational result to debates that often focus on task-level productivity: AI-tagged startups in its samples have lower entry-level workforce shares and flatter structures. If similar patterns survive longitudinal and independent checks, they could matter for junior career pathways and how firms scale. Its selective, cross-sectional startup evidence cannot establish economy-wide job loss or causal effects.
