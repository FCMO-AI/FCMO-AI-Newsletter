Evidence

  • Claimed — On 1,133 RewardBench pairs answered by its ten-judge open-weight bank, the paper reports mean pairwise error correlation 0.206 and variance-effective ensemble size 3.51; the authors explicitly state that effective size does not predict majority-vote accuracy or imply that 3.5 judges can replace the bank.
  • Claimed — Across resampled 100-pair RewardBench Focus panels, a pooled-vote test that treats votes as independent finds significance while an item-level test does not in 28% of panels; the paper reports 22% for Factuality and at most 2% for Math and Precise IF.
  • Claimed — For three cross-provider frontier judges on 400 RewardBench pairs, the paper reports mean pairwise error correlation 0.56 and effective size 1.41; its appendix notes that outside Gemini it evaluates only one flagship judge per provider and that estimates use relatively few residual errors.
  • Claimed — In tested preference tasks, different shared-error patterns favor different aggregation filters; the authors report that none of 11 screened factuality/code settings met all criteria for a dependence-aware filter to beat majority, and no single filter consistently outperformed majority.
  • Not established — The paper does not establish the natural prevalence of controlled dependence regimes, universal judge dependence, or that its numerical estimates transfer unchanged to other judge banks and tasks.

Strongest baseline

Plain majority voting, item-level uncertainty estimates, and the paper's matched-retention supermajority are the relevant baselines. Which filter ranks best changes with the error pattern; no dependence-aware method consistently beats majority.

Limitations

  • All numerical results are author-reported in one preprint; the research desk did not run code or reproduce them.
  • The main open-weight bank has ten logical judges from five 7–9B models. The paper says its effective ensemble size is a variance-based summary, not majority-vote accuracy or a replacement count.
  • The frontier cross-provider comparison covers three flagship judges and 400 RewardBench pairs; outside Gemini, the authors evaluate one flagship judge per provider, and residual-error counts are small.
  • Controlled global and subgroup failure injections isolate mechanisms but do not estimate their natural prevalence. Position bias is the shared vulnerability measured in depth; other failure structures may differ.
  • Dependence-aware filters and the selector require trusted calibration labels; the paper reports approximately 100 labels for key procedures and says no tested filter consistently beats majority.
  • The GRPO comparison uses one seed, three base models and 100 training steps on about 200 pairs from the calibration set, so it is not fully held out and does not show that preference optimization generally reduces dependence.
  • The authors caution that estimates should not transfer unchanged to substantially different judge banks or tasks, and proprietary models may change over time.

What we still don't know

  • the research desk did not independently reproduce the reported error-correlation, effective-size or significance-test estimates.
  • The paper does not measure natural prevalence for its controlled global co-failure and vulnerable-subgroup interventions; position bias is the main shared vulnerability studied in depth.
  • Numerical estimates may not transfer to substantially different or larger judge banks, tasks, or changing proprietary frontier models; frontier comparisons use few models and residual errors.

Why it matters

LLM-judge agreement is often treated as multiple independent signals in model evaluation and preference-data pipelines. If these reported patterns replicate, vote counts can overstate statistical evidence, and a single aggregation rule may fail when error structure changes. The paper also gives counterexamples to simple remedies: measured correlation alone misses some subgroup failures, and the tested filters do not consistently improve on majority voting. Its estimates remain specific to evaluated judges, tasks and calibration regimes.
