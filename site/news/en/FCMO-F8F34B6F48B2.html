The Trinity reports capability-driven harness evolution across multimodal agent benchmarks

The Trinity paper proposes recursive evolution of tool-using agent harnesses by grouping tasks around shared capability requirements, generating simplified training worlds, and selectively inheriting validated modules while keeping the foundation model fixed. In the current arXiv v2 paper, the authors report mean performance gains of 34 points across 28 training benchmarks and 26 points across 18 unseen benchmarks, including two physical-robot tasks. The results are author-reported; the official code repository was released on 30 October, but this activation did not run or independently reproduce it. The paper itself notes variable benchmark-discovery coverage and limits its present contribution to harness-level self-improvement.

Limitations

  • Performance gains are reported by the paper authors and were not independently reproduced in this activation.
  • The framework currently improves the harness with a fixed foundation-model backbone; it does not demonstrate general improvement of model weights.
  • Benchmark discovery relies on web search, whose coverage varies across queries.
  • The two physical task evaluations are narrow; the paper notes that recorded deployments differ in message handling, interfaces, guidance and acceptance rules, so the results do not isolate module evolution from task-specific skill preparation.
  • The repository was released under an MIT license, but only its README/license metadata were inspected; no code execution or audit was performed.

What we still don't know

  • No independent reproduction or audit of the benchmark results has been inspected. The public repository exists, but it was not run or audited in this activation.
  • The paper’s discussion says benchmark discovery uses web search whose retrieval coverage and result quality vary; broad completeness of the task inventory is not established.
  • The paper focuses on harness-level improvement with a fixed backbone. The two embodied settings compare full harness-skill configurations with differing interfaces/guidance, so they do not isolate module evolution or establish broad real-world transfer.

Why it matters

If the reported transfer holds under independent matched evaluation, capability-conditioned harness evolution could offer a reusable way to improve multimodal agents across task families without updating model weights. The evidence remains one author-run study, and the two embodied settings are narrow task-level demonstrations rather than evidence of general physical deployment.
