A structured pipeline cut false positive grading of research level mathematical proofs, but generality remains untested

The Ramanujan Challenge authors report an AI-assisted workflow for reviewing research-level mathematical submissions in natural language, computer algebra and Lean. It executes submitted Python under a fixed environment, has separate agents summarize the proof and code, compares those summaries, then presents the result to a human reviewer. Against one-shot Claude Opus 5 judgments on the same challenge set, the authors report that false 'Correct' verdicts fell from 23.4% to 5.8% for 137 natural-language proofs and from 38.3% to 3.3% for 120 CAS/Lean artifacts. Agreement with one human evaluator rose from 65.7% to 66.4% for proofs and from 45.8% to 58.3% for code. The paper says each artifact was reviewed by one human without cross-checking; participants self-selected and reported their tools. These are one paper's results, not independent confirmation of generality or reduced expert workload.

What changed

The Ramanujan Challenge authors report an AI-assisted workflow for reviewing research-level mathematical submissions in natural language, computer algebra and Lean. It executes submitted Python under a fixed environment, has separate agents summarize the proof and code, compares those summaries, then presents the result to a human reviewer. Against one-shot Claude Opus 5 judgments on the same challenge set, the authors report that false 'Correct' verdicts fell from 23.4% to 5.8% for 137 natural-language proofs and from 38.3% to 3.3% for 120 CAS/Lean artifacts. The paper says each artifact was reviewed by one human without cross-checking; participants self-selected and reported their tools. These are one paper's results, not independent confirmation of generality or reduced expert workload.

Evidence

  • Claimed — The authors report a workflow that executes submitted Python under a fixed environment, separately summarizes natural-language proofs and code with AI agents, compares the summaries, and presents them to a human reviewer.
  • Claimed — On the authors' Ramanujan Challenge evaluation, false-positive Correct verdicts by Claude Opus 5 fell from 23.4% (32 of 137) to 5.8% (8 of 137) for natural-language proofs and from 38.3% (46 of 120) to 3.3% (4 of 120) for CAS/Lean artifacts when using the pipeline rather than one-shot prompting. Agreement with the single human evaluator changed from 65.7% to 66.4% for proofs and from 45.8% to 58.3% for code.
  • Claimed — The authors report one correct and complete Lean-formalized proof of one published conjecture among the evaluated submissions; they state the conjecture solutions were not yet fully evaluated and one remained open.
  • Not established — The paper does not establish independent reproduction, agreement with multiple blinded expert evaluators, reduced real-world expert workload, or transfer of the measured grading changes to other models, mathematical problems, or scientific domains.

Strongest baseline

Claude Opus 5 with one-shot prompting is compared with the same model using the workflow, against the human evaluator's verdicts as reference labels. Proof agreement moved from 65.7% to 66.4%, while false acceptance fell from 23.4% to 5.8%. CAS/Lean agreement rose from 45.8% to 58.3%, while false acceptance fell from 38.3% to 3.3%. The paper's single-reviewer uncertainty also applies to reference labels.

Limitations

  • The quantitative results are reported by the paper's authors and were not reproduced in this activation.
  • Each artifact's reference assessment came from one human evaluator without cross-checking; the authors identify potential error and evaluator-background bias.
  • Participants self-selected and tools were self-reported; participation varied from one to all ten problems.
  • Proof-level agreement with the human evaluator increased only from 65.7% to 66.4%, even as false-positive 'Correct' judgments declined. The work does not establish reduced expert time or generality beyond this challenge.
  • The reported Lean-formalized conjecture proof is paper-reported; this activation did not inspect its source or verify its theorem statement and dependencies.

What we still don't know

  • No independent reanalysis or replication with multiple blinded expert evaluators was inspected. The single-human reference may contain errors or evaluator-specific bias, as the authors acknowledge.
  • The reported effect is limited to one self-selected number-theory challenge and one Claude Opus 5 comparison; transfer to other models, proof domains or scientific claims is not established.

Why it matters

When AI-generated scientific claims arrive faster than experts can check them, false acceptance can become a bottleneck. This study reports that separating proof and code analysis and comparing their summaries reduced one model's false 'Correct' verdicts on a research-level mathematics challenge. The modest change in proof agreement, the single-evaluator reference labels, and the narrow challenge set make this a promising evaluation method to test, not evidence that AI can yet verify research proofs reliably across domains.
