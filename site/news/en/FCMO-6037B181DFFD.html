A paper reports a nonlinear difficulty scale that changes interpretation of METR time horizons for short software tasks

Nguyen and Fithian reanalyze METR’s public Time Horizon 1.1 data for 228 software-engineering tasks and 26 AIs using shared-slope logistic, monotone-spline and item-response models. Their preprint reports that task difficulty is nearly flat against human completion time from 2–30 minutes, so the same 10× increase in a displayed time horizon can represent different changes in task difficulty across regions. Under five-fold cross-validation across 79 task families, their proposed models reportedly outperform the selected shared-slope baseline on all 12 proper-scoring metrics; most of the gain comes from estimates in the 2–15 minute band. They do not challenge the exponential trend itself. This is a single author-reported analysis of one public software-task suite, not an independent reproduction or evidence that the metric fails outside the analyzed interval. METR’s own January Time Horizon 1.1 note says the suite was revised from 170 to 228 tasks and reports a different post-2023 doubling estimate under the new suite, while warning that the compared model sets overlap but are not identical and that task composition affects the quantity measured; this is context, not a reproduction of the preprint’s nonlinear fit.

What actually changed

This is a single author-reported analysis of one public software-task suite, not an independent reproduction or evidence that the metric fails outside the analyzed interval. METR’s own January Time Horizon 1.1 note says the suite was revised from 170 to 228 tasks and reports a different post-2023 doubling estimate under the new suite, while warning that the compared model sets overlap but are not identical and that task composition affects the quantity measured; this is context, not a reproduction of the preprint’s nonlinear fit.

Claim vs. evidence

  • CLAIMED — The authors report that the mapping from human time to latent task difficulty is nearly flat for tasks assigned 2–30 minutes, making equal time-horizon multipliers non-comparable across that region and other regions.
  • CLAIMED — The authors report that their spline and item-response estimates outperform a shared-slope logistic baseline on all 12 displayed proper-scoring metrics under five-fold cross-validation by task family; most gains come from short horizons.
  • DEMONSTRATED — The authors state that release dates are not used in fitting and that they do not challenge the observed exponential growth in the displayed time horizons.
  • DEMONSTRATED — METR’s own Time Horizon 1.1 announcement says the suite changed from 170 to 228 tasks (73 added, 15 removed, 53 updated) and reports a 131-day post-2023 doubling estimate under TH1.1 versus 165 days under TH1 for mostly overlapping but non-identical model sets; METR attributes the difference to shifts in older and newer model estimates and notes task-distribution sensitivity. This is context about the benchmark revision, not an independent reproduction of the new paper’s nonlinear analysis.

Strongest baseline

The authors compare against METR’s original log-linear logistic specification and a shared-slope logistic baseline, which they select based on prior work and their own reported cross-validation. Their Models 1 and 2 reportedly outperform the shared-slope baseline across all 12 proper-scoring metrics. METR’s first-party TH1.1 announcement documents the suite revision (170 to 228 tasks) and reports a 131-day versus 165-day post-2023 doubling estimate across mostly overlapping, non-identical model sets; METR attributes the shift partly to task composition and revised model estimates.

The caveat that matters

  • The results are reported in a single arXiv v1 preprint and have not been independently reproduced in this activation.
  • The analysis uses one software-engineering benchmark suite and does not establish generality to other capability domains.
  • Twenty-nine percent of task human-time annotations use expert estimates because direct human timing was unavailable or judged invalid.
  • Most reported differences are localized to tasks with assigned human times of roughly 2–30 minutes; the authors do not challenge the exponential trend itself.
  • The scoring results do not establish that human time is a valid annotation for all tasks; the authors assume several earlier construct-validity questions and focus their diagnostics on predictiveness and comparability.
  • No code repository was located during this check.

What remains unknown

  • No independent implementation has reproduced the spline/item-response fits and 12-metric cross-validation results.
  • The analysis is limited to METR’s software-engineering task suite and does not validate time-horizon annotations for other domains or future longer-task suites.
  • No author code repository was located in the paper or bounded source check; the paper describes methods but reproducibility still requires implementation.

FCMO Lens

Time-horizon estimates are used to communicate AI software-task capability and may inform benchmark design and policy discussion. If the paper’s analysis holds, raw equal-multiplier jumps in the 2–30 minute region are not comparable to jumps elsewhere; reporting success curves and task-to-difficulty diagnostics would make the measure easier to interpret. The paper leaves the overall exponential trend intact and does not establish external validity beyond this task suite.
