Models choose between two directions before seeing later outcomes; a question counts correct only when the choice is correct in both candidate orders, making chance accuracy 25%. Evidence

  • Claimed — The authors introduce Taste-Bench, a 502-question set of trajectory decision forks from software engineering and AI research, with labels derived from later branch outcomes and a strict two-order accuracy metric whose chance baseline is 25%.
  • Claimed — On this benchmark the authors report GPT-5.6 Sol as the best model at 59.7% average strict accuracy; accuracy averaged across models falls from 62.3% for in-prefix evidence to 21.0% when the justification needs more substantial later work.
  • Claimed — In a task-disjoint held-out engineering split, the distilled Qwen3.6-27B student reports 47.9% strict accuracy versus 30.0% for the base model. On the same 41 held-out SWE-bench Pro tasks, a fixed executor reports 14.6% success with no advice, 33.7% with student advice and 39.0% with correct advice.
  • Not established — These paper-reported results do not establish independent replication, broad real-world agent improvement, or transfer beyond the benchmark and the 41-task SWE-bench Pro regime.

Strongest baseline

Taste-Bench: GPT-5.5 at 59.5% and GPT-5.6 Sol at 59.7% under the strict two-order metric. Held-out transfer: the same frozen Qwen3.6-27B base model at 30.0%. End-to-end: same fixed executor and 41 tasks at 14.6% without advice and 39.0% with oracle/correct advice.

Limitations

  • All benchmark, training and end-to-end results are paper-reported; the research desk did not reproduce them.
  • The 502 questions are mined from available task trajectories and selectively filtered by generator/judge models; they do not represent all agent decisions.
  • Human review sampled 100 questions; 172 explicit A/B judgments were made, and reviewers could see continuation summaries and outcomes.
  • Held-out engineering accuracy is from a two-fold task-disjoint split within the source trajectory pool; broad task-distribution transfer is not established.
  • End-to-end SWE-bench Pro evidence is limited to 41 held-out tasks and one Qwen3.6-27B executor; advice comes from prior trajectories on those tasks.
  • Exact bootstrap interval endpoints for the student/base contrast and end-to-end comparison were not available in the inspected text extraction; no uncertainty values are asserted here.
  • Additional reasoning budget did not improve performance only in the tested models and settings.

What we still don't know

  • Benchmark construction, model scores, distillation effects and end-to-end advice gains have not been independently reproduced or audited against the released artifact.
  • The end-to-end advice comparison covers 41 SWE-bench Pro tasks and one executor; transfer across task types, agent scaffolds and repeated trials is unknown.
  • The benchmark relies on generated forks and judge-model filtering; sensitivity to mining choices, judge selection and domain composition is not established.

Why it matters

End-to-end task success does not reveal whether an agent chose a useful direction before delayed consequences appeared. Taste-Bench makes that judgment observable using recorded trajectory forks and reports that additional reasoning did not improve accuracy in the tested settings. Its paired no-advice comparison also bounds an end-to-end improvement from distilled advice on 41 held-out tasks, while synthetic fork mining, small task scope and lack of independent replication limit generalization.
