Logit perturbations reproduce many reported Neural Thickets gains without changing weights

A preprint re-evaluates the Neural Thickets perturb-select-vote procedure and reports that much of its measured gain does not require perturbing model weights. The authors apply a fixed random bias to output logits, select members using 200 labeled task examples, then vote across selected members. In author-reported tests on language models up to 7B parameters, this approach is statistically indistinguishable from full-weight perturbations on 22 of 28 matched model–task pairs and significantly better on three and worse on three; effects vary by task, and on USPTO the raw accuracy partly reflects a majority-class predictor. On vision tasks with train-test shift, the authors attribute gains primarily to output recalibration. The work offers an alternative explanation for the measured gains, not evidence that nearby weight-space structure is absent or that the method improves general model capability.

What changed

A preprint re-evaluates the Neural Thickets perturb-select-vote procedure and reports that much of its measured gain does not require perturbing model weights. The authors apply a fixed random bias to output logits, select members using 200 labeled task examples, then vote across selected members. In author-reported tests on language models up to 7B parameters, this approach is statistically indistinguishable from full-weight perturbations on 22 of 28 matched model–task pairs and significantly better on three and worse on three; effects vary by task, and on USPTO the raw accuracy partly reflects a majority-class predictor. On vision tasks with train-test shift, the authors attribute gains primarily to output recalibration. The work offers an alternative explanation for the measured gains, not evidence that nearby weight-space structure is absent or that the method improves general model capability.

Evidence

  • Claimed — The authors formalize perturb-select-vote (PSV) as sampling perturbed predictors, selecting the top members on a labeled task-specific set, and aggregating their predictions. Their logit-PSV variant keeps the pretrained model frozen and reuses a sampled vocabulary bias through generation and across inputs.
  • Claimed — Under the matched original protocol reported in the preprint, logit PSV and full-weight PSV were statistically indistinguishable on 22 of 28 model–task pairs; logit PSV was significantly better on three and worse on three. The tested language models range up to OLMo-3-7B-Instruct, with N=5000 and K=50 in the matched comparison and a 200-example selection set.
  • Claimed — For OLMo-3-7B-Instruct on MATH-500, the authors report 81.3% accuracy for logit PSV, 74.7% for full-weight PSV, and 63.3% for the unperturbed base under the stated comparison; on USPTO, logit PSV's higher raw accuracy is partly explained by majority-class predictions and its balanced accuracy is lower than full-weight PSV.
  • Claimed — In the reported vision experiments, logit-space methods improved worst-group or worst-region accuracy by 5–6 points on Waterbirds and FMoW under distribution shift, while showing essentially no gain on balanced ImageNet; deterministic class-frequency recalibration recovered much of the shift-task gain.
  • Not established — The evidence reviewed does not establish independent reproduction, applicability above 7B language models, benefit on open-ended generation, improvement in general model capability, or lower total inference cost. The method requires a labeled selection set and K model evaluations per test input.

Strongest baseline

The paper's matched comparison to the cited Neural Thickets full-weight perturbation method, using author-released code and original prompts, splits, scorers and N=5000/K=50 settings; additional baselines include temperature-tuned sampling with majority voting and deterministic recalibration for vision.

Limitations

  • All new empirical results are author-reported; no independent reproduction was performed.
  • Language experiments stop at 7B parameters and cover a bounded set of models and benchmark tasks.
  • The method requires a labeled task-specific selection set; the paper says it is less suitable for open-ended generation.
  • At inference, the method requires K model evaluations per test input; no total-cost or latency advantage over alternatives is established.
  • On USPTO, raw accuracy can obscure class collapse: the authors report lower balanced accuracy for logit PSV than for full-weight PSV in the cited OLMo comparison, and both methods can match a constant majority-class baseline in some Qwen comparisons.
  • The authors report that selection can overfit the 200-example set and that the first-order analysis applies only at sufficiently small perturbation scales.
  • Vision gains are concentrated in datasets with train-test distribution shift; balanced ImageNet shows essentially no improvement, and the authors attribute the shifted-data result largely to output recalibration.

What we still don't know

  • No independent reproduction of the new logit-PSV results was inspected; author code and expected outputs are available, but the full reported run is estimated at about 140 H100 GPU-hours.
  • Transfer beyond language models up to 7B, short-answer benchmark tasks, small labeled selection sets and the vision datasets is unknown; total inference cost is not compared across practical serving regimes.

Why it matters

The result separates an observed inference-time gain from the geometric explanation proposed for it: validation selection and voting over output diversity may account for much of the reported effect without changing weights. If reproduced beyond the tested models and tasks, that would shift how researchers interpret weight-space adaptation and design cheaper or more direct test-time methods. The method still uses a labeled selection set and K model evaluations per test input, so the paper does not establish lower total inference cost.
