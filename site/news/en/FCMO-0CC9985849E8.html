What changed

Paul and Nandy's arXiv paper presents Governance-as-Code, a framework of 43 machine-checkable Rego acceptance criteria across six modules for selected EU AI Act obligations affecting generative systems. It makes operational thresholds and proxies explicit and emits Article-indexed audit evidence. In two author-reported case studies, the framework reproduced findings from manual expert audits; the authors report 12 findings reproduced across the cases and an 18-hour versus 72-hour chatbot audit. These results are paper-reported, not independently validated.

Evidence

  • Demonstrated — The authors published a paper describing 43 machine-checkable acceptance criteria in six modules, with example Rego policy code and Article-indexed evidence outputs.
  • Claimed — Across two author-reported system case studies, Governance-as-Code reproduced 12 findings from manual expert audits; for the chatbot case the paper reports 18 hours of framework-assisted audit labor versus 72 hours manually.
  • Claimed — The paper reports that the chatbot's fixed 500-question probe measured hallucination rates rising from 3.2% to 7.1% across three retrieval-corpus rebuilds, and that a 200-vector adversarial suite found prompt extraction and prohibited-advice rates of 23% and 8%.
  • Demonstrated — The paper states that its evaluation covers two systems, that its legal readings and thresholds may be disputed, and that the eight fairness proxies simplify framing and can miss biases they do not measure.

Strongest baseline

A legal-plus-engineering manual expert audit. The paper reports matching all eight chatbot findings and all four content-generator findings, with the chatbot comparison taking 18 versus 72 hours.

Limitations

  • Reported case-study metrics, audit findings and labor estimates are author-reported; no independent audit or reproduction was performed.
  • The evaluation covers only two systems. The authors call for broader validation across additional high-risk sectors.
  • The framework operationalizes contested readings of Articles 8–15 and Article 50; the paper explicitly notes that reasonable legal disagreement remains possible.
  • Eight text-framing proxies simplify fairness and may miss biases they do not measure; robustness conclusions depend on the adversarial suite's quality and domain curation.
  • The paper does not establish that passing its encoded criteria is equivalent to legal compliance or improved real-world compliance outcomes.

What we still don't know

  • Independent implementation or reproduction of the 43 checks, two reported case studies, audit-finding coverage and labor comparison is unavailable in this review.
  • Transfer of the authors' operational readings and thresholds to other sectors and legal interpretations is unmeasured.

Why it matters

If the framework's checks transfer beyond two author-described deployments, it offers a concrete way to turn parts of AI Act compliance into repeatable deployment-pipeline checks and can shift audit effort from evidence collection toward review. Its narrow evaluation and contestable legal mappings mean it does not establish that the approach improves compliance outcomes generally.
