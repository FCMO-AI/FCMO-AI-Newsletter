What changed

A September 21, 2026 paper proposes selecting a small regression-test subset for software-engineering agents from prior task outcomes and sanitized trajectory embeddings. Across 76 configurations and three code-agent regression scenarios using SWE-Bench Verified and SWE-Rebench, the authors report that their 10% deterministic subset kept median resolve-rate estimation error below 5% and reduced token consumption from 3.44 billion to about 345 million in the analyzed multi-model runs. They analyze 31,779 trajectories from 58 runs across five agent frameworks and use temporal splits so selection uses earlier runs than evaluation runs. The method requires prior full-run history, dataset-specific trajectory sanitization and refresh when behavior changes; evidence is limited to code-repair benchmarks and the paper has no independent reproduction in this record.

Evidence

  • Claimed — The authors report that selecting 10% of SWE-bench tasks using historical outcome groups and geometric selection over sanitized trajectory embeddings kept median resolve-rate estimation error below 5%, with 90% lower token use than the full benchmark in their evaluated runs. The study covers SWE-Bench Verified and SWE-Rebench across same-setup reruns, model/configuration changes and framework changes. This is a paper-reported estimate on those datasets, not evidence that a 10% subset can replace full evaluation across other domains or after large architecture shifts.

Strongest baseline

The paper compares with outcome-stratified random selection and other random, embedding, clustering and hybrid methods. It reports the centroid-based trajectory embedding method as best among the evaluated configurations; at 5%/10%, error gains are measured relative to typical and 95th-percentile baseline draws.

Limitations

  • The result is restricted to software repair benchmarks, SWE-Bench Verified and SWE-Rebench; external validity beyond coding agents is untested.
  • Subset selection requires at least one prior full run and uses prior trajectories and outcome groups; embeddings and strata may become stale after behavior or benchmark changes.
  • The paper evaluates error in estimating full-suite resolve rate, not preservation of every rare failure mode or all safety-relevant properties.
  • Results are authors’ offline analyses; no independent reproduction has been conducted in this record.
  • For the 1,000 synthetic distributions sampled from 500 instances, distributions overlap. The authors note that this makes Wilcoxon p-values optimistic; they also report effect sizes and checks across difficulty levels.
  • The 10% / 90% headline is an evaluated token-cost regime, not a universal guarantee: the selected centroid subset used 10.48% of token cost on average, while random 10% subsets ranged 5.7–15.0% across the widest spread.

What we still don't know

  • The paper links a replication package, but no independent execution or reproduction has been performed in this record.
  • Evaluation covers SWE-Bench Verified and SWE-Rebench code repair; transfer to web navigation, general tool-use, scientific or other agent benchmarks is not established.
  • The subset depends on historical trajectories and may miss unusual failures after large agent architecture or behavior changes; the paper recommends refreshing embeddings when typical behavior changes.

Why it matters

Full SWE-agent benchmark runs are expensive because each issue triggers many model calls and tool steps. A subset that estimates resolve rate with lower error at a fraction of task count could support more frequent regression checks between full runs. The practical claim is bounded to the paper’s two code-repair benchmarks and historical-trajectory setup; it does not establish that organizations can safely abandon full evaluations.
