Anthropic reports task-level patterns in scientists’ use of Claude

Anthropic’s 5 November 2026 report analyzes 207,049 consumer-plan Claude and Claude Code sessions classified as scientific work from 2 August to 30 September. It reports that data analysis and writing are common uses, while data gathering and ideation appear less often than their estimated share of researcher time. An LLM-based task-capability measure is associated with greater use: the top fifth of tasks receive about two to four times the use expected from researcher time. A separate survey of 528 active AI-using scientists broadly mirrors the task mix. These are first-party, classifier-based usage findings; the underlying conversations are not public for independent audit. The report does not measure research quality, causal productivity or realized scientific discoveries, and its future-output scenarios are hypothetical. Its 528-person survey recruited from Anthropic grant-program applicants in the US and Canada, likely a high-AI-use sample; the task-capability scores are also LLM-derived, so their relationship with product usage is not independent validation of capability.

Evidence

  • Demonstrated — Anthropic published its report on 5 November 2026, describing an analysis of 207,049 consumer-plan sessions classified as scientific work from 2 August to 30 September 2026.
  • Claimed — Anthropic reports that data analysis accounted for 33.7% of Claude and 48.1% of Claude Code research sessions, compared with an estimated 27.6% of researcher time; data gathering accounted for 11.5% and 11.9% of sessions versus an estimated 28.6% of time, and ideation accounted for 4.2% and 2.1% versus 7.1% of time.
  • Claimed — Anthropic reports that usage relative to estimated research time rises with its LLM-derived task-capability measure; tasks in the highest fifth receive about twice expected use on Claude and about four times expected use on Claude Code. The report also describes a survey of 528 scientists whose self-reported AI use broadly follows the observed task pattern.
  • Not established — The report’s scenarios that widespread adoption of current capabilities could double output in some fields, or that full automation of non-physical work could raise output up to 15-fold in some fields, are modeled hypotheticals; the report does not demonstrate those output effects.
  • Not established — This report does not establish that Claude or Claude Science caused higher-quality research, increased scientific output, or more discoveries.

Strongest baseline

Task-use shares are compared with estimated researcher time from the SciNet taxonomy; survey responses from 528 active AI-using scientists provide a separate but self-reported comparison.

Limitations

  • All behavioral measurements are reported by Anthropic from its own consumer-plan sessions and proprietary classifiers; no raw transcript-level data or independent classification audit was inspected.
  • The survey sample contains 528 scientists and is described as primarily life- and health-science researchers who actively use AI; it is not representative of all scientists.
  • Theoretical task-capability scores and usage ratios are associations; they do not establish that capability caused usage or that use improved research outcomes.
  • The output-acceleration estimates are scenarios, not measured productivity effects.
  • The 528-person survey recruited applicants to Anthropic AI-for-Science and related grant programs in the US and Canada; Anthropic says this sample likely contains more intensive AI users than typical scientists.
  • The task-capability score is constructed from LLM pairwise judgments of task steps and a Bradley–Terry aggregation; it is an LLM-derived theoretical measure and its association with use does not independently validate task capability.
  • Claude Science task differences may partly reflect selection by users with specialized or demanding use cases; Anthropic explicitly reports them as patterns of use.

What we still don't know

  • The 207,049-session categories, task-capability labels and estimated researcher-time baselines were not independently audited; source transcripts are not public. The accompanying 528-person survey was recruited from AI-for-Science grant applicants in the US/Canada and is likely not representative of typical scientists.
  • The report measures session use and models scenarios but does not measure causal changes in research quality, completion time or output.
  • The accompanying survey samples applicants to Anthropic AI-for-Science and related grant programs in the US/Canada, likely intensive AI users; product comparisons may also reflect user selection.

Why it matters

Large-scale behavioral evidence about where scientists currently use AI can inform research on adoption and bottlenecks. The report suggests current use is concentrated in tasks that its capability measure rates highly, while data gathering and hypothesis generation remain less represented. This is evidence about reported use patterns, not proof of scientific productivity gains or a general acceleration rate.
