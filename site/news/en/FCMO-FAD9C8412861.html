What changed

Google Research publicly released Deep Review on Oct 14, expanding its Paper Assistant Tool from opt-in pre-submission feedback for computer-science papers into a document-review product with two free weekly reviews and API access. Google reports more than 16,000 submissions across four conference pilots, while the underlying preprint details the first two pilots and an author-run test on 26 papers with 29 documented mathematical or proof errors. The authors report 89.7% error detection against 55.2% for a Gemini 3.1 Pro zero-shot baseline, using an LLM grader audited by an author. The pilots were not part of official peer review; independent accuracy, false-positive burden and workload effects remain unmeasured.

Evidence

  • Demonstrated — Google Research announced on Oct 14 that Deep Review is publicly available worldwide for reviewing documents, with two complimentary reviews per user each week and API access for higher-volume use.
  • Claimed — Google reports that the earlier Paper Assistant Tool received opt-in use on more than 16,000 submissions across STOC, ICML, NeurIPS and ICLR; the public post does not give conference-by-conference inclusion, usage or outcome denominators.
  • Claimed — In a 26-paper, 29-error subset of SPOT, the authors report PAT detected 89.7% of defined math/CS equation or proof errors, versus 55.2% for Gemini 3.1 Pro in a zero-shot single-call baseline. An LLM grader judged reports and an author audited grader outputs; these are small, author-run preprint results, not an independent evaluation.
  • Claimed — The described STOC and ICML deployments offered pre-submission feedback to authors who opted in; PAT was kept separate from the official conference-review process. The paper reports known risks including hallucinated or mistaken critiques and PDF parsing issues.

Strongest baseline

A single Gemini 3.1 Pro zero-shot generation on the same selected SPOT subset (55.2% as scored by the authors’ LLM grader). The paper notes its grading protocol differs from SPOT’s original strict keyword grader, so the figures are not directly comparable to the original SPOT score.

Limitations

  • The 16,000+ usage figure comes from Google’s launch post; the linked preprint describes only the earlier STOC and ICML pilots and does not independently establish all four pilots or their outcome effects.
  • The pilots provided opt-in pre-submission feedback to authors and were separate from formal conference review; the public release does not establish that Deep Review is suitable for acceptance decisions.
  • The SPOT experiment covers 26 papers and 29 previously documented errors in a selected mathematics/computer-science subset. Its LLM grader and author audit are not independent ground truth across all error types.
  • The reported result is a system-level comparison on one narrow benchmark subset, not evidence that the tool reliably verifies arbitrary scientific, legal or financial documents.
  • Known limitations include false claims that arguments are incorrect and PDF parsing problems; independent precision, false-positive cost and user outcomes remain open.

What we still don't know

  • Independent, prospective evaluation of Deep Review’s error detection and false-positive rate across varied technical documents is unavailable in the reviewed materials.
  • The public post aggregates 16,000+ submissions across four opt-in pilots without conference-level denominators, author opt-in rates, paper outcome linkage or measured effects on official review workload.

Why it matters

A broadly accessible system that helps check long technical documents could reduce the cost of finding errors as scientific output grows. The evidence supports a public launch and author-reported pilot performance, while the actual deployments were pre-submission assistance outside formal peer review. Independent precision, false-positive costs and effects on human review remain unknown.
