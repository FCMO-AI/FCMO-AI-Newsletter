What changed

A September 2026 preprint defines Loopjacking as product logic applying a human decision for an operation A to a materially different operation B. In purposively selected tests, the author reports post-approval substitution in seven sampled Agno AgentOS releases and 12 sampled in-memory LangGraph Agent Server compositions with a declared authorization policy, plus an incomplete shell-command approval representation in OpenClaw 2026.2.23. OpenClaw 2026.2.24 rejected the tested mismatch. OpenAI Agents SDK 0.22.0 and 0.22.2 served as negative controls that rejected same-call mutation. The tests used synthetic principals and harmless recording sinks; they do not estimate prevalence, establish user susceptibility, or show all versions are affected.

Evidence

  • Claimed — The paper defines Loopjacking as product-owned logic using a human decision understood as approval for operation A to authorize or release a materially different operation B, and separates incomplete representation before approval from state substitution after approval.
  • Claimed — The author reports reproducing post-approval substitution in seven sampled Agno AgentOS releases (2.5.6, 2.9.0, 3.0.1, 3.0.2, 3.0.3, 3.0.6, 3.0.9) and 12 sampled LangGraph Agent Server versions from 0.7.5 through 0.14.0 in a specified in-memory composition with a custom authorization policy.
  • Claimed — The author reports that OpenClaw 2026.2.23 released a shell-command vector whose approval view omitted materially different arguments, while 2026.2.24 rejected the tested mismatch; the 0.22.0 and 0.22.2 OpenAI Agents SDK negative controls rejected same-call-ID mutation and executed unchanged approved calls.
  • Claimed — In the tested traces, exact use-time comparison or a policy denying unauthorized pending-state updates blocked the substituted operation while preserving execution of the unchanged approved operation.

Strongest baseline

Unchanged approved A execution, direct unapproved B denial, actor/session boundary controls, the OpenAI Agents SDK negative control, OpenClaw's 2026.2.24 fixed behavior, and a research exact-action comparison or deny-update policy.

Limitations

  • The preprint reports one researcher's controlled tests; the research desk did not independently reproduce them or execute the archive verifier.
  • The evidence cutoff is September 10, 2026. Exact tested releases are reported; later versions and deployments are not classified.
  • Agno and LangGraph have no vendor-fixed release established in the inspected archive. The LangGraph result is conditional on the tested in-memory composition and authorization policy.
  • Synthetic credentials and harmless recording sinks establish the tested operation dispatched by the product but do not show real funds, production data, or customer systems were affected.
  • The OpenClaw approval role was scripted after observing the product event; the study does not measure human comprehension or interface deception.
  • The tested set was purposive and does not estimate ecosystem prevalence or establish an uninterrupted affected-version range.
  • The LangGraph result depends on a declared custom authorization policy and in-memory composition; production Postgres was not evaluated.
  • OpenClaw 2026.2.24 rejected the tested representation mismatch; the paper states that the approval role was scripted, so human comprehension or deception rates were not tested.
  • OpenAI Agents SDK 0.22.0 and 0.22.2 rejected mutation in the tested ordinary per-call approval path, showing that continuation and serialization alone are not sufficient causes.

What we still don't know

  • Independent execution of the archived Agno, LangGraph, OpenClaw, and OpenAI negative-control bundles has not been performed in this activation.
  • No comprehensive current affected-version map or prevalence estimate is established; the paper's release evidence cutoff is September 10, 2026.

Why it matters

If these traces generalize to deployed approval workflows, a visible approval can fail to constrain what an agent ultimately executes. Reconstructing the complete operation and comparing it at use time, or preventing unauthorized changes to pending state, gives implementers a testable security invariant.
