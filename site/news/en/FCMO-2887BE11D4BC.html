Rewindable proofs give weak verifiers a new route to check robust computations

A new paper introduces rewindable interactive proofs, in which a verifier can restore an AI prover to an earlier saved state and continue the interaction from there. For robust oracle computations, the authors prove a relativizing protocol with a succinct verifier and a linear-time honest prover, without assuming that a prover is truthful or relying on cryptography. The paper reports a proof-of-concept MNIST-derived experiment and a BLAKE3 comparison, but the authors say the experiments are not a realistic scalable-oversight benchmark. The measurements use selected hardwired circuits; general preprocessing and real model-state restore costs remain unresolved, so deployed oversight benefits are not established.

Evidence

  • Demonstrated — The arXiv v1 record identifies a paper submitted on 2026-10-29 by Ziyi Guan, Yael Tauman Kalai, Ron Rothblum and Zoe Xi; its full text defines rewindable interactive proofs (RIPs), allowing a verifier to restore a model prover to a saved interaction state and ask new questions from there.
  • Claimed — The authors prove a relativizing RIP for robust oracle circuits: after one-time preprocessing, their informal Theorem 1.1 gives a verifier running in Õ(n+m+1/ε) time with O(1/ε) oracle queries, an honest prover running in O(n+m+S)+Õ(1/ε) time, and soundness error at most 1/3 against a computationally unbounded cheating prover. The guarantee is for inputs where the computation is ε-robust and assumes the verifier can actually rewind the prover state.
  • Claimed — In a proof-of-concept experiment on 50 selected MNIST-derived circuits built from hardwired random sparse views, the authors report 74–80 verifier oracle queries as circuit size grows to 2^26 views; at that size their verifier took 0.12 times the direct table-lookup evaluation time. The authors note that these circuits do not demonstrate the protocol on realistic scalable-oversight tasks and do not need the general preprocessing step.
  • Claimed — For a BLAKE3 proof workload, the authors report that their rewindable compilation of Flock's IOP prover ran 1.8–2.1 times faster than Flock's SNARG prover across tested sizes, based on medians of three runs on one Graviton 4 core. The comparison uses modified interactive security parameters and a 64-bit base field that Flock's release does not offer.
  • Not established — The paper does not establish that rewindable proofs improve deployed AI oversight, that general workloads amortize preprocessing, or that full model-state save/restore costs preserve the reported efficiency. In the MNIST setup the prover is about 60 times slower than direct table-lookup evaluation at the largest size, and the experiments treat rewinding as free.

Strongest baseline

For BLAKE3, the authors compare their RIP compilation to Flock's deployed SNARG prover and report 1.8–2.1× lower prover time, medians of three executions on one Graviton 4 core. The comparison changes the interactive security parameters, field configuration and commitment mechanism, so it is a bounded implementation comparison rather than a matched deployment cost study. For the selected MNIST circuits, direct table lookup is the baseline; the verifier is faster only at the largest sizes and the prover remains about 60× slower than direct evaluation.

Limitations

  • This is a single v1 preprint; the formal proof and experimental implementation were not independently reviewed or reproduced in this activation.
  • The MNIST experiment is a proof of concept on 50 selected circuits with hardwired random sparse views, not a realistic scalable-oversight benchmark; the authors note that the verifier could estimate these majority outputs without a prover.
  • The general preprocessing step is not measured or amortized in the experiment. The implementation uses Ligero openings whose verifier cost grows with the square root of the trace rather than achieving the theorem's polylogarithmic behavior.
  • The largest MNIST case leaves the prover about 60× slower than direct table-lookup evaluation, and the protocol treats saving and restoring a real model's state as free.
  • The BLAKE3 comparison uses modified interactive parameters and three runs on one core; it does not show end-to-end cost under matched deployment security assumptions.

What we still don't know

  • No independent proof review or pinned reproduction of the authors' implementation was performed in this activation.
  • The experiments use selected hardwired circuits and omit the cost of saving/restoring a real model's complete state; the general preprocessing cost is not measured.

Why it matters

If independently checked and made practical, rewinding a model controlled by the verifier could give scalable oversight a new way to check some robust computations without trusting a prover or using cryptographic commitments. The formal result is restricted to robust oracle computations, and the experiments do not show that real model-state save/restore costs or realistic workloads retain the reported efficiency.
