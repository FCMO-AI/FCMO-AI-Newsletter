What changed

A new preprint proposes Misbehavior-driven RL (MiRL), which profiles recurring errors in a terminal agent, resumes training rollouts from replayable prefixes before those errors, and requires both task-test success and avoidance of the target behavior for reward. On Qwen3.5-4B, the authors report 51.8% on Terminal-Bench Lite and 21.3% on Terminal-Bench 2.1 using 35% of TMax-4B's rollouts; a separate 16-seed result strengthens only the Terminal-Bench 2.1 comparison. Results are from one model backbone and no independent reproduction was performed.

Evidence

  • Demonstrated — The authors posted arXiv:2610.35697v1 on 2026-10-26 and describe Misbehavior-driven RL (MiRL), which profiles recurring terminal-agent errors, starts training rollouts from replayable prefixes immediately before those errors, and rewards a rollout only when it passes task tests and a behavior judge says it avoided the target misbehavior.
  • Claimed — On Qwen3.5-4B, the authors report that eight recurring error types cover 52.1% of profiled wrong steps. With 189 retained training environments, MiRL scores 51.8% on Terminal-Bench Lite and 21.3% on Terminal-Bench 2.1, versus their TMax-4B results of 47.4% and 20.6%, using 35% of TMax-4B's rollouts. A separate 16-seed Terminal-Bench 2.1 comparison reports means of 21.14% for MiRL and 20.22% for TMax-4B.
  • Not established — The preprint does not establish that the gains transfer to other model backbones or agent domains, remain stable across independent training runs, or persist when prefix-retention bias and checkpoint selection are controlled. Its reported benchmark results are not independent confirmation.

Strongest baseline

TMax-4B is the principal scaled-data baseline. On the authors' evaluation hardware it scores 47.4% on TB-Lite and 20.6% on TB-2.1. MiRL is 51.8% and 21.3%, respectively, with 35% of TMax-4B's rollouts. The 16-seed TB-2.1 means are 21.14% and 20.22%; this indicates a small margin on that benchmark, not a decisive broad win.

Limitations

  • Author-reported preprint results; no independent reproduction was performed in this activation.
  • Only one policy backbone, Qwen3.5-4B, was tested.
  • Training recipes were each run once; the standard three evaluation runs do not measure training-seed variance.
  • Only 219 of 640 candidate prefixes passed exact-output replay, potentially biasing retained examples toward deterministic environments.
  • Headline checkpoint selection uses Terminal-Bench Lite score among the top five smoothed-reward checkpoints, so TB-Lite is involved in model selection.
  • The 16-seed evaluation addresses Terminal-Bench 2.1 only; its small mean gap does not establish a broad advantage.

What we still don't know

  • No independent reproduction of MiRL or its reported benchmark results was inspected.
  • Each training recipe was trained once; the 3-run headline error bars cover evaluation runs, not variation across separately trained policies. The 16-seed evaluation covers TB-2.1 only.
  • Only Qwen3.5-4B was tested, and exact-output prefix replay retains 219 of 640 candidate prefixes, which may bias training toward deterministic environments.

Why it matters

The paper treats failure analysis as a way to allocate training rollouts and reward signals, rather than only as a diagnostic tool. If its result transfers, teams may gain more from selecting where rollouts begin and what they penalize than from scaling environment count alone. The evidence currently supports a bounded result for one terminal-agent backbone, not a general recipe across models or tool domains.
