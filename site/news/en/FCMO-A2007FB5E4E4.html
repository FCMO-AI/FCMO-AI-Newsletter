What changed

Anthropic’s 6 November engineering article describes persistent agent memory as a hierarchy of session logs, notes, linked representations and selectively injected context, with offline “dreaming” used to correct or reorganize it. The company reports gains on CL-Bench, a Pokémon navigation example, and internal document-corpus evaluations, including cases where wiki organization improved retrieval and cases where it added no clear benefit or grew larger than its sources. The results are first-party and task-specific; they do not establish general production reliability or transfer.

Evidence

  • Demonstrated — Anthropic published an engineering article on 6 November 2026 describing a memory hierarchy in which agents retain session logs and notes, build linked representations, and use offline “dreaming” passes to test, revise or promote memory across sessions.
  • Claimed — On a CL-Bench database task, Anthropic reports that access to persistent memory raised mean reward for Opus 4.7 from 0.383 to 0.700, Opus 5 from 0.564 to 0.828, and Opus 5.5 from 0.655 to 0.838; Sonnet 5.5 reached 0.833 with memory versus 0.644 without it. These are provider-reported results on the stated task.
  • Claimed — In one Pokémon Red run, Anthropic reports that an offline pass checked a mistaken location note against the transcript, corrected it and enabled the agent to reach Cerulean City; the run with the uncorrected note repeatedly backtracked and ended without reaching the city.
  • Claimed — Across 128 sequential memory-building sessions over an internal 350,000-file, roughly 420-million-token corpus, Anthropic reports that an Opus 5 wiki improved criteria met on 25 questions from 13% to 48%. With search plus the growing wiki, its aggregate score rose from 54% to 61%, compared with 53% for search alone, while saving about 20,000 tokens per question. The sample and evaluation are internal and narrow.
  • Claimed — On one contract-focused legal-brief task, Anthropic reports that changing wiki instructions from concurrent summaries in a shared table to separate pages with verbatim terms increased criteria met from 41/57 (72%) to 57/57 (100%); this illustrates a specific information-loss mechanism, not general legal-task performance.
  • Not established — The article does not establish that these memory mechanisms improve reliability or outcomes across independent systems, organizations, tasks or long-lived production deployments. It describes examples where wikis added no clear gain or grew larger than their source documents, and notes unresolved risks from stale or incorrect memory and maintenance compute.

Strongest baseline

For the internal document corpus, search alone scored 53% against 61% for search plus a growing wiki; wiki alone scored 48%. Some legal tasks showed no clear benefit, demonstrating that the effect depends on task structure.

Limitations

  • All numerical results are reported by Anthropic and were not independently reproduced.
  • The Pokémon memory-correction example is a single reported run, not a reliability estimate.
  • The internal wiki evaluation used a 25-question set over one company corpus; document selection and grading were not independently audited.
  • The article reports mixed outcomes: some tasks gained little from wikis, and one wiki grew to 1.77 times the size of its source documents.
  • Incorrect or stale memory can be carried into future sessions; dream-time maintenance consumes additional compute, and the article leaves cadence and cost tradeoffs open.

What we still don't know

  • The CL-Bench, Pokémon and document-corpus results are provider-reported and have not been independently reproduced.
  • Performance beyond selected benchmark tasks and one internal company corpus is not established.
  • Memory maintenance cost, stale-memory rate and correction precision are not comprehensively quantified.

Why it matters

Persistent memory is a practical bottleneck for agents working across sessions. The article gives concrete examples of memory helping, becoming stale, losing facts through concurrent edits, and requiring offline maintenance; those mechanisms can guide system design and evaluation, but the reported gains need independent, broader checks.
