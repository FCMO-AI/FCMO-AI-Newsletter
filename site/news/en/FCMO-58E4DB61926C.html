Authors report poisoned-claim uptake under surprise-based continual learning

A new preprint reports that surprise-only continual learning does not filter poisoned claims in controlled streams and can absorb most presented lies, including nearly all counterfactual lies about facts a model already knows. The study evaluates Qwen2.5-1.5B, Qwen2.5-3B and Llama-3.2-3B with atomic factual claims, invented entities and CounterFact examples. The authors report that a prior-conflict filter reduces some known-fact lies but blocks many legitimate updates; a source-evidence route helps only under assumptions about source integrity. These are author-reported small-model results, not independent or deployment-scale validation.

What changed

A new preprint reports that surprise-only continual learning does not filter poisoned claims in controlled streams and can absorb most presented lies, including nearly all counterfactual lies about facts a model already knows. The study evaluates Qwen2.5-1.5B, Qwen2.5-3B and Llama-3.2-3B with atomic factual claims, invented entities and CounterFact examples. The authors report that a prior-conflict filter reduces some known-fact lies but blocks many legitimate updates; a source-evidence route helps only under assumptions about source integrity. These are author-reported small-model results, not independent or deployment-scale validation.

Evidence

  • Demonstrated — The authors posted arXiv preprint 2610.39840v1 on 29 October 2026, with a linked code repository.
  • Claimed — Across controlled streams using Qwen2.5-1.5B, Qwen2.5-3B and Llama-3.2-3B, the authors report surprise-only learning absorbed 84–93% of streamed lies and 99–100% of known-fact counterfactual lies; in a matched-budget comparison it absorbed lies at a similar overall rate to random selection and more known-fact lies.
  • Claimed — The authors report that a prior-conflict filter reduced known-fact lie absorption but also withheld many legitimate updates, and that source-evidence routing recovered some updates when sources were trusted and unavailable to the attacker; planted matching sources compromised that route.
  • Claimed — In the tested setup, regression tests over unrelated facts reduced learning throughput without filtering targeted factual poisoning.
  • Not established — The small controlled experiments do not establish the size of poisoning risk or mitigation performance in larger models, long or multi-hop content, online user streams, adaptive attacks on verifiers, or deployed continual-learning systems; independent reproduction was not performed in this activation.

Strongest baseline

A matched-training-budget random subset control absorbed streamed lies at 254 versus 257 per 1,000 learned claims (paired 95% CI for difference −15 to +9), while surprise selection absorbed more known-fact counterfactual lies in that setting.

Limitations

  • All numerical results are author-reported; the code was inspected but not executed, and no independent reproduction was found in this activation.
  • Claims are atomic facts in controlled streams, not long documents, multi-hop knowledge, opinions or real user feedback.
  • The largest tested students have 3B parameters; deployment-scale transfer is unknown.
  • Prior-conflict filters detect contradiction with existing beliefs rather than truth and can block legitimate updates; an attacker with model access can screen lies to pass the filter.
  • The evidence route assumes sources that attackers cannot plant or copy; the paper reports that planted matching sources compromise it.
  • The authors did not measure human/retrieval review load or evaluate a deployed continual-learning system.

What we still don't know

  • No independent reproduction of the reported poison rates, gate tradeoffs or cross-model pattern was located or performed.
  • The paper tests a prior-screening attacker and planted matching sources, but does not establish robustness against adaptive attacks jointly targeting the verifier and source route.
  • Only small models and atomic factual claims were tested; scale transfer and performance on long or multi-hop content remain unknown.

Why it matters

As models gain mechanisms for updating from incoming claims, an admission signal based on surprise may select some false claims precisely because they conflict with existing beliefs. The paper reports controlled evidence about this failure and the tradeoffs in tested filters, while leaving deployment transfer open.
