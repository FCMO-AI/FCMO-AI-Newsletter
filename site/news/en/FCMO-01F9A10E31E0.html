UK regulator issues operational AI governance guidance for pension schemes

The UK Pensions Regulator published guidance on 29 October 2026 explaining how existing trustee and scheme-manager duties apply to AI used in pension schemes. Evidence

  • Demonstrated — The UK Pensions Regulator published 'Use of artificial intelligence (AI) in pension schemes' on 29 October 2026, explaining how existing trustee and scheme-manager duties apply to AI use.
  • Demonstrated — The guidance recommends proportionate, lifecycle-based governance, continued human accountability, provider assurance, continuity planning, appropriate data handling, and fair and accessible member outcomes, with oversight scaled to the use's risk.
  • Demonstrated — TPR states that the guidance is intended to help apply existing regulatory expectations and does not establish new separate regulatory requirements.
  • Not established — The guidance does not establish trustee or provider adoption, compliance, safety improvement, incident reduction, or changed member outcomes.

Strongest baseline

The guidance says existing trustee responsibilities remain in force; no measured pre-guidance practice or post-guidance adoption baseline is reported.

Limitations

  • This is sectoral regulator guidance, not a new AI regulation or evidence of changed legal duties; TPR explicitly says it does not establish separate regulatory requirements.
  • The source documents recommendations and examples but reports no trustee or provider adoption, compliance, incident, safety, or member-outcome measurements.
  • Its scope is UK pension scheme governance and should not be generalized to other financial sectors or jurisdictions.

What we still don't know

  • The guidance reports no trustee/provider uptake, compliance assessment, or resulting change in AI governance practice.
  • No evidence is provided about AI-related incidents, fairness, service quality, or member outcomes before or after the guidance.

Why it matters

Pension schemes may use AI in administration, communications, data analysis, governance and provider services, including member-facing interactions. The guidance gives trustees a concrete oversight frame for these uses, while leaving the effect on actual practice and members unknown.
