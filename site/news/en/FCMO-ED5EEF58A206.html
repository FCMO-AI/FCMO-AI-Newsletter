Mistral preprint reports small time-series models leading three forecasting benchmarks

An October 27 arXiv report describes Timestral, four probabilistic time-series forecasting models from 5M to 209M parameters. The authors report that Timestral-209M ranks first on six aggregate metrics across GIFT-Eval, TIME and fev-bench, with smaller variants on the reported size–accuracy Pareto front. The method separates forecast tokens from causal context and assigns target, past-only-covariate and known-future-covariate roles. On a specified one-day, univariate forecast from 2,048 time steps of history, the paper reports 17–113 ms latency on eight CPU threads. In a separate author-run IND-Eval analysis on 187 industrial forecasting tasks, the paper also reports Timestral-209M leading eleven compared models on two aggregate metrics (0.646 nmase and 0.435 ncrps versus t0-gamma at 0.697 and 0.469). That evaluation is not independent. The model family was announced on October 15, and the models, report and inference code are public. Benchmark and latency results are author-reported; no independent reproduction or realized operating-cost advantage is established.

Evidence

  • Demonstrated — Mistral’s report was submitted to arXiv on 2026-10-27 and describes a four-size Timestral family, its forecasting method and benchmark/evaluation setup. This establishes the report and its contents, not correctness or independent validation of the reported outcomes.
  • Claimed — The authors report that Timestral-209M ranked first among compared models on all six aggregate metrics across GIFT-Eval, TIME and fev-bench; the 123M, 28M and 5M models also lie on the reported parameter-count/performance Pareto front. These are results from the paper’s benchmark protocol, not an independent leaderboard audit. In a separate author-run IND-Eval evaluation on 187 industrial tasks, they report Timestral-209M at 0.646 nmase and 0.435 ncrps, versus 0.697 and 0.469 for t0-gamma; this also is not independent confirmation.
  • Claimed — For a 96-step univariate forecast from 2,048 context steps, the authors report median 8-thread CPU latency of 17, 31, 85 and 113 ms for the 5M, 28M, 123M and 209M models, respectively, using one 48-core AMD EPYC 9454 server, bfloat16 weights, torch.compile and symmetric averaging. These figures do not establish comparative total cost on production workloads.
  • Demonstrated — The report links public weights for all four sizes and an inference-code repository; the official repository page exposes source files, tests and an Apache-2.0 license. Availability does not establish that this activation reproduced the benchmarks.
  • Not established — Independent reproduction, dataset-leakage audit, operational forecast improvement, and realized cost or energy savings have not been established by the sources inspected in this activation.
  • Claimed — The authors report an additional zero-shot evaluation on the public IND-Eval benchmark’s 187 industrial tasks, comparing Timestral with 11 models under the benchmark protocol. Timestral-209M has reported aggregate nmase 0.646 and ncrps 0.435, versus 0.697 and 0.469 for t0-gamma. These are author-run results, not an independent reproduction.

Strongest baseline

The paper compares public time-series foundation models including Chronos-2, Toto-2.0, TimesFM-3, Falcon-2.0 and t0-gamma; exact scores and protocol are in the report. The comparison has not been independently rerun here. Table 3 comparator set is explicitly frozen at the October 15 model-release date, twelve days before the technical-report submission; it is not a live October 27 leaderboard. IND-Eval’s separate 11-model author-run comparison uses the official protocol on 187 tasks.

Limitations

  • All performance and inference figures are authored by Mistral contributors and were not independently reproduced in this activation.
  • The six-metric benchmark lead is conditional on the paper’s model set, benchmark protocols, checkpoint and aggregation; no independent leaderboard audit was performed.
  • The reported CPU timing covers one univariate workload, context length, hardware, precision, software path and symmetric-averaging procedure. It does not measure a matched production workload, energy use, cloud price or total cost.
  • The models are pretrained only on synthetic data and post-trained on mixed real and synthetic data; transfer to operational domains and distribution shift remain unmeasured here.
  • DHF retains observations as context and forecast targets but roughly doubles training-token count according to the authors; training compute and total resource cost were not quantified in the inspected sections.
  • The October 15 first-party page identifies the original model-release date. The October 27 date belongs to the arXiv technical report, not a new model launch.
  • The GIFT-Eval, TIME and fev-bench comparison table includes models plotted at the October 15 model-release date, not a leaderboard refreshed at the October 27 technical-report submission; the reported ranking is bounded to that comparator snapshot.

What we still don't know

  • No independent rerun or audit of the six reported GIFT-Eval, TIME and fev-bench metric rankings was conducted.
  • The reported CPU latency is not a matched operational total-cost or energy comparison, and no production forecast improvement is measured.
  • DHF’s training-token overhead and generalization under domain shift are not independently measured in the inspected evidence.

Why it matters

If independently reproduced, the reported accuracy at 5M–209M parameters and CPU inference could change forecasting-model choices for operations that currently fit separate models or rely on larger accelerators. The evidence is limited to the paper’s benchmark regimes and one defined latency workload; it does not establish improved outcomes or lower costs in a production setting.
