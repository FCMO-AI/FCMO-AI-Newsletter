DecisionRAG uses hierarchical decisions for retrieval without a first-stage retriever

A Cornell and ASAPP preprint introduces DecisionRAG, which navigates a document hierarchy by scoring candidate branches with shared-context decision-model judgments rather than relying on a conventional first-stage retriever at query time. Across four author-run benchmarks, the paper reports rank-one gains over selected dense-retrieval pipelines with an 8B reranker, including 13.8 percentage points on MultiDoc2Dial and 17.4 points on Natural Questions. Reported query latency is 0.68–1.76 seconds and API cost 0.22–2.33 cents across the benchmark configurations. These are preprint-reported measurements; independent reproduction and transfer remain open. Navigation prunes branches irreversibly, so poor hierarchies or low exploration budgets can lose relevant evidence.

Evidence

  • Demonstrated — A version 1 preprint introducing DecisionRAG was submitted to arXiv on October 26, 2026.
  • Claimed — The authors report that DecisionRAG, without task-specific training or a conventional query-time first-stage retriever, improves rank-one retrieval over selected dense-retrieval pipelines with an 8B reranker by 13.8 percentage points on MultiDoc2Dial and 17.4 points on Natural Questions; results cover four benchmarks, including a 2.68-million-paragraph Wikipedia corpus.
  • Claimed — In the reported serving configurations, retrieval latency ranged from 0.68 to 1.76 seconds per query and API cost from 0.22 to 2.33 US cents per query across four benchmarks. The combined configuration reports 47.3% judged answer correctness on MTRAG versus 34.0% for GPT-5 reranking, using Qwen3.5-27B as answer generator.
  • Not established — The preprint does not establish independent reproduction, production-scale reliability, transfer to other corpora or serving conditions, or uniform superiority; irreversible pruning can discard relevant branches and hierarchy quality is a stated limitation.

Strongest baseline

Selected strongest baselines differ by task: dense retrieval with Qwen3-Reranker-8B, GPT-5 reranking of dense candidates, and published task-trained references. Published reference results were not rerun; no single baseline is strongest on every metric.

Limitations

  • All reported experiments are from one preprint; this activation did not independently reproduce results or inspect code and serving artifacts.
  • Reported baselines differ by dataset and configuration; some published reference results are not reruns, so cross-dataset comparisons do not establish a single matched ranking.
  • The method depends on a useful corpus hierarchy and irreversible branch pruning can miss relevant passages; quality may degrade when hierarchy structure or descriptions are poor.
  • Latency and API cost are specific to the authors' models, endpoints, prompts, corpus structures and query mix; operational concurrency, reliability and total preprocessing cost were not established.

What we still don't know

  • No independent reproduction of retrieval, answer-quality or serving measurements was located or performed.
  • This review did not inspect the linked implementation, prompt/configuration files or offline hierarchy construction artifacts.
  • Transfer beyond four benchmarks, hierarchy quality, production concurrency and sensitivity to irreversible pruning remain unmeasured.

Why it matters

The work explores a retrieval design that applies model judgment directly to corpus navigation and may reduce the accuracy bottleneck imposed by a first-stage retriever. Its results make quality, latency and cost tradeoffs visible across several benchmarks, but do not establish deployment performance or general superiority.
