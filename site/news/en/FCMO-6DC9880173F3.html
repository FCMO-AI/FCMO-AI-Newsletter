Instrument-mounted demonstrations train an appendectomy policy tested in live rabbits

A Sept. 22 preprint reports an end-to-end pipeline that records instrument pose and jaw state from sensors mounted on a surgeon’s ordinary laparoscopic tools, aligns the sensor channels against robot ground truth, and trains a bimanual policy without robot-collected demonstrations. After closed-loop simulator selection among 31 configurations, the authors retrained on 849 demonstrations from four live rabbits and tested on four additional rabbits; the policy completed appendectomy in three of four under shared autonomy with electrosurgery armed. A surgeon chose the surgical phase because the vision-based phase predictor failed under the deployment endoscope. This is a small animal study, not full autonomy or clinical evidence.

Evidence

  • Demonstrated — The authors describe an instrument-mounted logger using inertial, time-of-flight and Hall sensors to estimate tool state without an external camera or tracker, with per-channel latency matching against robot ground truth.
  • Claimed — The policy trained from hand-held instrument demonstrations completed appendectomy in three of four additional live rabbits under shared autonomy with the surgeon selecting the surgical phase.
  • Claimed — The authors report training on 849 in-vivo demonstrations from four live rabbits and use of a policy configuration selected through 31 simulator configurations.
  • Claimed — The study is limited to four deployment animals on one day; the surgeon selected surgical phase because the vision-based predictor agreed with the surgeon on only 24.6% of policy control steps under the deployment endoscope.

Strongest baseline

In simulator ablations, selected DINOv3 policy with one unfrozen block reached 81% full-approach completion versus 12% for ResNet-18 and 44% with frozen DINOv3; these are simulator configuration comparisons, not an independent baseline for live-animal completion.

Limitations

  • Author-reported, not independently reproduced in this activation.
  • The deployment set contains four live rabbits; the policy completed the task in three.
  • This was shared autonomy: a surgeon selected the phase because the vision-based phase predictor performed poorly under the deployment endoscope.
  • No human-patient or clinical safety evidence is reported.
  • The robot provides calibration ground truth and executes the policy, even though it did not collect training demonstrations.

What we still don't know

  • No independent animal replication or human clinical evaluation was inspected; current report uses four deployment animals.

Why it matters

If the pipeline generalizes, it could turn otherwise discarded motion from hand-held minimally invasive surgery into policy-training data and reduce dependence on robot-specific demonstrations. The current result is an early in-vivo animal demonstration with explicit human phase control, not evidence of patient safety or autonomous surgery.
