EU issues binding DMA specifications for Google AI interoperability and search-data access

On July 16, 2026, the European Commission issued two binding specification measures to Google under the Digital Markets Act. One addresses Android interoperability so users can invoke competing AI assistants and delegate actions in apps; the other specifies access for eligible third-party search engines, including AI chatbots with search functions, to anonymized Google Search ranking, query, click and view data. The rules define eligibility, data scope, use limits, privacy safeguards, audits, access processes and FRAND pricing. Google's own program page says the specified dataset covers the European Economic Area and that qualifying recipients must accept licensing terms. This review did not establish that applicants have received data or that the measures have changed competition or user outcomes.

Evidence

  • Demonstrated — The European Commission announced two binding specification measures for Google under the DMA on July 16, 2026: one for AI-service interoperability with Android features and one for Google Search data sharing with eligible third-party search engines.
  • Demonstrated — The Commission's Search data Q&A says eligible providers may include AI chatbots with search functionality; shared data covers anonymized ranking, query, click and view information, while use is limited to developing and optimizing search services and excludes training general-purpose AI models.
  • Demonstrated — The Commission specifies anonymization and contractual safeguards, independent audits, eligibility criteria, a minimum seven-day data latency and access for up to five years per beneficiary. Google's program page says the dataset is from Google Search in the European Economic Area and access is subject to licensing terms.
  • Not established — The official materials describe binding obligations and implementation milestones, not demonstrated third-party access, service launches, privacy performance or resulting competitive effects. Google may screen for specified security and data-protection risks, and the Commission may revisit measures based on new evidence.

Strongest baseline

Before the specification measures, the Commission said Google's initial search-data proposal removed between 90% and 100% of unique queries and excluded AI chatbots offering search, with no meaningful uptake by potential beneficiaries. This is the Commission's characterization of the earlier proposal, not an independent audit in this review.

Limitations

  • The Commission's announced objectives are not measured user or competition outcomes.
  • The shared search dataset is limited to eligible recipients and anonymized data under security, audit and purpose restrictions; it is not unrestricted access to Google's index, algorithm or raw user histories.
  • The Commission's baseline description of Google's earlier proposal is an official institutional account, not independently audited here.
  • The review did not establish applicant approvals, delivered data, actual prices, the quality of anonymization or effects on AI assistant and search competition.
  • The decision permits reassessment as implementation evidence and technical developments emerge.

What we still don't know

  • The review used official summaries and Google's licensing-program page but did not inspect the complete operative decision annexes or independently parse all technical obligations.
  • No public applicant-level record was checked to establish whether qualifying providers have completed audits, received data or launched services using the access route.
  • The effectiveness of anonymization, dataset utility, pricing and actual effect on competition has not been independently measured in this review.

Why it matters

The decision targets two inputs that shape AI product competition: access to Android capabilities and large-scale search data. It also constrains use of shared data to developing and optimizing search services, with explicit anonymization, auditing and privacy conditions; recipients may not use it to train general-purpose AI models. The measures create a legally specified route for some rival services, but real effects depend on implementation, applicant eligibility, data usefulness, price and privacy performance.
