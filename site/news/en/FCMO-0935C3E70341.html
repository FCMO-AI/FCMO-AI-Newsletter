Evidence

  • Demonstrated — On October 26, OpenAI published an announcement describing an AI-generated ETH refutation and linked a natural-language manuscript and Lean formalization in its public repository.
  • Claimed — OpenAI reports that its system produced a refutation of ETH. The linked Lean scope states that, for each fixed k at least 2, a deterministic machine exactly counts satisfying assignments of canonical k-CNF formulas in subexponential time with polynomial space; the manuscript states a sharper exponent for m clauses. The currently inspected theorem source contains a proof body, while a separate comparator challenge file retains a sorry placeholder. This repository inspection does not establish a pinned, reproducible build, full dependency closure, or correspondence to standard ETH.
  • Claimed — OpenAI says an external group led by Russell Impagliazzo, Ramamohan Paturi and Ryan Williams will examine the result and that the group requested public raw materials; their participation does not certify the claim.
  • Not established — Community contributors now report pinned-byte ports of both labs’ proof chains to the same standard multitape-machine statement, each checked in a single Lean environment with Lean’s judge, nanoda and leanchecker; they report planted wrong variants were refused, while four of six bundled Lean judge checkers hit resource limits on the OpenAI export. This is still an attributed community report, not an the research desk replay or independent mathematical conclusion. The correspondence between the formalized machine predicate and textbook ETH, as well as practical speedup, remains unestablished.

Limitations

  • OpenAI’s theorem and the recent port/checker evidence remain source-reported; the research desk did not independently replay any build or proof.
  • The Nov 5 community report describes pinned-byte checks with several kernels/checkers, but its author says Claude agents assisted in writing the bridges, ports and checker; no independent outside replay is documented.
  • The same report says four of six checkers bundled with Lean’s judge stopped at resource limits on OpenAI’s 2.45 GB export; the final verdict relied on checkers that completed.
  • The formalized machine predicate’s equivalence to textbook ETH, and the natural-language manuscript’s correspondence to the checked theorem, remain interpretive and unestablished.
  • Subexponential time is not polynomial time; no practical speedup on real instances is established.

What we still don't know

  • A Nov 5 community contributor reports same-environment, pinned-byte ports of both labs’ chains to a common multitape-machine statement and multiple checker results, but the author disclosed Claude-assisted work and no outside replay was inspected. The formalized definition-to-textbook-ETH correspondence remains an interpretive step.
  • The reported subexponential counting bound does not establish a practical speedup on real instances or polynomial-time solvability.

Why it matters

If the formalized and manuscript claims are correct and match standard ETH, complexity results that rely on ETH would need reassessment. A subexponential exact algorithm would not by itself establish polynomial-time solvability or settle P versus NP.
