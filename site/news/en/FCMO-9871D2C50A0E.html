A preprint measures the limits of generic OS defenses for self-hosted agent state

Chen, Denis, Di Pietro and Schmidhuber define self-state attacks as corruption of an agent’s persistent memory, instructions or configuration through the agent’s own authorized host writes. Their arXiv v2 paper evaluates prevention, detection, provenance and recovery on one Linux reference backend with four contrastive workloads. It reports that broad generic detectors catch many attacks but trigger on substantial legitimate activity, while selective write-specific detectors apply to only 23 of 55 admitted attacks. Full barriers stop all six tested file mutations but also reject the paired authorized update. Protected backups restore all tested corruptions only when held beyond the compromised principal’s reach; choosing a trustworthy recovery point and limiting rollback loss remain open. The paper does not establish these tradeoffs across production deployments or other operating systems.

What actually changed

Chen, Denis, Di Pietro and Schmidhuber define self-state attacks as corruption of an agent’s persistent memory, instructions or configuration through the agent’s own authorized host writes. Their arXiv v2 paper evaluates prevention, detection, provenance and recovery on one Linux reference backend with four contrastive workloads. It reports that broad generic detectors catch many attacks but trigger on substantial legitimate activity, while selective write-specific detectors apply to only 23 of 55 admitted attacks. Full barriers stop all six tested file mutations but also reject the paired authorized update. Protected backups restore all tested corruptions only when held beyond the compromised principal’s reach; choosing a trustworthy recovery point and limiting rollback loss remain open. The paper does not establish these tradeoffs across production deployments or other operating systems.

Claim vs. evidence

  • CLAIMED — The authors define self-state attacks as malicious corruption of agent memory, instructions or configuration through host operations that use the agent’s legitimate authorization, and organize the attack space across target, mutation mechanism, edit granularity and timing.
  • CLAIMED — In the authors’ 55-attack / 60-natural-execution evaluation, broad detector baselines cover most or all attacks but produce substantial natural-workload positives; write-specific methods produce fewer natural positives but are evaluable on only 23 of 55 attacks.
  • CLAIMED — In a six-operation replay on one durable-memory object, the tested immutable-file, AppArmor and Landlock configurations blocked all six replayed mutation paths and also rejected the corresponding approved direct update.
  • CLAIMED — Protected backup storage survived same-principal backup destruction and restored all tested corruptions, while recovery still depends on selecting a trustworthy point and can roll back legitimate updates.
  • DEMONSTRATED — The reported empirical design uses one Linux reference backend and four contrastive workload profiles, not a representative sample of deployed self-hosted agents; 20 of the 55 attack executions are directly instantiated under the compromised agent principal rather than induced end-to-end by a model.

Strongest baseline

Within the paper, the broadest detector baselines (AIDE, Falco, STIDE) have 55/55 attack coverage, but natural positive rates from 21/60 to 58/60; the more selective write-specific methods lower natural positives but are defined on only 23/55 attacks. The study does not compare a self-state-aware context mechanism.

The caveat that matters

  • All performance and defense outcomes are author-reported; no independent reproduction was performed.
  • The 55-attack corpus combines 35 model-driven persistent-write cases with 20 attacks instantiated directly under the compromised principal; it does not show that every case is model-inducible.
  • The 60 held-out natural executions span 20 scenarios; profile-level rates from five scenarios are descriptive, not deployment prevalence estimates.
  • The main live results use one Linux reference backend. Cross-platform expectations are discussion, not measured results. Prevention replay covers six operations against one MEMORY.md object and is not a proof over all Linux authority paths or policy designs.
  • Artifacts are described as forthcoming upon paper acceptance; raw telemetry is excluded and no external code or independent result reproduction was available in this review.

What remains unknown

  • No independent team has reproduced the detector, prevention or recovery results; code and derived artifacts are not yet available in the inspected version, and raw telemetry is excluded.
  • Generality beyond the single Linux reference backend and four contrastive workload regimes is unmeasured; the workloads are not a probability sample of deployments.
  • The paper’s result motivates a self-state-aware update boundary but does not implement or evaluate which combination of task context, state role or update phase achieves adequate selectivity.

FCMO Lens

Self-hosted agents can write files that shape their future behavior, so ordinary filesystem permission and telemetry controls may not distinguish an authorized memory update from a malicious one after the agent is compromised. If the paper’s scoped measurements hold more broadly, defenses need a separate, context-aware update boundary alongside isolation and protected recovery.
