OpenProblemBench reports judge-assessed model progress on 82 unresolved mathematics and physics problems

OpenProblemBench tests seven model-execution configurations on 82 literature-grounded unresolved questions in mathematics and theoretical physics. Four evaluator models review each frozen solution against explicit proof or computational obligations and score solved, partial or unsolved progress. The paper reports a 14.02% evaluator-averaged solve rate for GPT-6-Astra and 2.44–7.01% for the other configurations; substantive partial progress is also counted. These are author-reported evaluator judgments, not domain-expert validation of discoveries. The authors identify evaluator error correlation, uneven problem coverage, single runs per condition and varying execution resources as limitations.

Evidence

  • Claimed — The authors introduce OpenProblemBench, a literature-grounded benchmark of 82 stated unresolved questions in mathematics and theoretical physics. Seven model-execution configurations each submitted a final solution to all 82 items, and four evaluator models independently reviewed each submission against the problem's obligations, classifying solved, partial or unsolved outcomes and recording overclaims.
  • Claimed — Across the authors' evaluator-averaged judgments, GPT-6-Astra achieved a 14.02% solve rate, with other configurations between 2.44% and 7.01%. The paper reports 84.45% solved, breakthrough-partial or nontrivial-partial outcomes for Astra; evaluator judgments are not expert verification of correctness, novelty or scientific discovery.
  • Claimed — In one paired Qwen3.8-Max comparison on the same 82 problems, changing the execution harness changed both solve and substantive partial judgments; enabling web access under Claude Code left the judged solve count at 5 but increased nontrivial-plus-breakthrough partial outcomes from 59 to 68. This is one benchmark-specific comparison and does not isolate a general model effect.

Strongest baseline

The strongest comparison is the same 82 problem statements evaluated across seven solver-execution configurations using the same four evaluator models and shared outcome definitions. A paired Qwen3.8-Max comparison varies harness and web access; the design does not isolate model weights from every execution-system difference.

Limitations

  • All solve and partial-progress rates are judgments by four models; independent domain-expert review is needed to establish correctness and novelty, and evaluator agreement does not estimate shared false-positive or false-negative rates.
  • The benchmark contains 82 selected mathematics and theoretical-physics questions, with uneven subfield composition and comparatively checkable resolution criteria; it does not represent all sciences or all research work.
  • There is one final run per model/problem condition. Execution harnesses, resource use and historical run conditions vary, so fine performance differences and run-to-run reliability are not established.
  • Literature review can miss later solutions, and a problem statement or relevant result may occur in model training data. The paper does not establish generalization to new problems or deployed research programs.
  • The paper reports that evaluator judgments vary and that rankings below the top configuration are less stable. It explicitly says evaluator agreement alone cannot estimate error rates and calls for blinded expert review.
  • The benchmark itself shows execution-context sensitivity: a paired harness change altered Qwen3.8-Max outcomes, and web access changed partial-progress judgments without changing the judged solve count in the reported comparison.
  • The 82 questions are selected for comparatively clear checking and are uneven across fields; success rates cannot be generalized to broad scientific discovery.

What we still don't know

  • No blinded expert audit of judged-solved submissions, correctness, novelty or evaluator false-positive rates was available in this review.
  • One final run per configuration and varying harness/resource conditions leave run-to-run variance and model-versus-execution effects unresolved.

Why it matters

Most AI reasoning benchmarks have known answers, while research often concerns questions without a reference solution. A benchmark that supplies literature context and evaluates proof obligations, computational checks and partial progress could improve how research-agent capability is measured. Here the headline solve rates come from model judges, and the paper itself calls for blinded expert validation; they do not establish that the models made verified scientific discoveries.
