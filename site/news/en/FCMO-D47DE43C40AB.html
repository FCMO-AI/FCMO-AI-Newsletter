A 41-country paper reports a shift toward senior workers at AI-adopting multinationals

A September 2026 Stanford Digital Economy Lab working paper analyzes 1.25 billion job postings and 154 million employment records across 41 countries, inferring firm AI adoption from generative-AI job advertisements. In an instrumented event study of foreign multinational affiliates, the authors report a 1.9-percentage-point decline in junior workforce share by March 2026 relative to matched controls. Senior employment rises 6.7%; the 2.5% decline estimate for junior employment is not statistically significant, and total employment rises 3.3% at marginal significance. The authors interpret their model-based decomposition as suggestive of modest, seniority-biased productivity gains. These are paper-reported estimates, not independent replication or a representative global labor census. The paper notes platform coverage and adoption-timing limits and an untestable instrument exclusion restriction. Its population and adoption measure differ from Anthropic’s October 2026 internal workforce report, so it is comparative evidence rather than a replication.

Evidence

  • Demonstrated — Stanford Digital Economy Lab lists a working paper dated September 21, 2026, by Bharat Chandar and Bouke Klein Teeselink, with a linked 156-page paper analyzing job-posting and employment records across 41 countries.
  • Claimed — The authors report that by March 2026 the junior share at treated foreign affiliates was 1.9 percentage points below matched controls. They report senior employment 6.7% higher, a 2.5% lower junior-employment estimate that is not statistically significant, and total employment 3.3% higher at marginal significance.
  • Claimed — The authors infer generative-AI adoption from job advertisements and use an instrumented event study for foreign affiliates of multinational companies; their model-based decomposition suggests modest productivity gains with a seniority bias.
  • Demonstrated — The paper states that LinkedIn/Revelio profiles over-represent managers and professionals and vary in country coverage; the instrument exclusion restriction cannot be fully tested, treatment timing is proxied using job postings, and the IV results apply to larger, internationally integrated foreign affiliates.

Strongest baseline

Matched foreign multinational affiliates in the same country and sector plus the paper’s instrumented event study, instrumenting adoption with AI adoption among other multinational parents headquartered in the treated parent’s metropolitan area. Alternative matched event-study and synthetic difference-in-differences estimates are reported on a broader affiliate sample.

Limitations

  • One working paper using platform-visible employment records; estimates are not independently reproduced.
  • Job-advertisement adoption proxy may lag actual adoption; instrument exclusion restriction remains untestable.
  • Instrumented estimates concern foreign multinationals and should not be generalized to all firms or aggregate national employment.

What we still don't know

  • No independent replication of the reported affiliate event study or model-based decomposition was completed.
  • Platform-visible affiliates and job-ad-based adoption do not establish effects for domestic firms, informal work, or national aggregate employment.

Why it matters

If future work confirms the reported pattern, AI adoption could expand firm employment overall while reallocating opportunities away from junior workers and toward senior workers. That is a consequential distributional outcome for workforce entry and policy. The current evidence remains one paper's estimates for digitally attached multinational affiliates and does not establish a global causal law.
