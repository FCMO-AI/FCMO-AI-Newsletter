Cache-to-Cache reports faster cross-model communication through direct KV-cache transfer

The Cache-to-Cache (C2C) paper proposes transferring a source model’s key/value cache into a target model through a learned fuser, bypassing text generation between models. Across four question-answering benchmarks and several Qwen, Llama and Gemma combinations, the authors report 6.4–14.2% higher average accuracy than individual models and about 3.1–5.4% above text-to-text handoffs; latency was 2.5× faster on average in their tested settings. Their detailed timing table reports 3.46×, 1.51× and 14.41× speedups over text handoff for three model pairs. The fuser uses trainable pair-specific components, and scaling to many communicating models with O(N) training cost remains open. These are paper-reported results; no independent reproduction was performed.

Evidence

  • Claimed — C2C uses a learned module to project and fuse source and receiver KV caches, with a dynamic weighting mechanism and trainable layer gates.
  • Claimed — Across four benchmarks, the authors report 6.4–14.2% higher average accuracy than individual models and about 3.1–5.4% higher accuracy than text-to-text communication, with an average 2.5× speedup in their tested settings.
  • Claimed — In a detailed MMLU-Redux comparison, authors report replacing 80 text handoff tokens and 1,312 ms of decoding with 90 ms of KV-cache fusion; timing used a single A100 with batch size one.
  • Claimed — The paper reports that a weaker sharer can mislead a receiver, and identifies O(N) fuser training cost for scaling to multiple communicating models as unresolved.
  • Not established — No independent reproduction or production deployment result was obtained in this review.

Strongest baseline

The paper compares C2C with text-to-text collaboration, query-level routing, and each sharer/receiver alone. Evaluations use zero-shot prompts at temperature zero and report four knowledge/reasoning benchmarks; inference timing uses one NVIDIA A100 at batch size one.

Limitations

  • All performance and timing results are reported by the paper authors; no independent replication was performed.
  • The evaluated tasks are four multiple-choice QA benchmarks with zero-shot, temperature-zero prompts; this does not establish gains on open-ended agent workflows.
  • Timing uses one NVIDIA A100 at batch size one; end-to-end serving costs and integration constraints are not measured.
  • A weak or inaccurate sharer can mislead the receiver; the paper includes failure examples.
  • Pairwise fuser training scales with the number of communicating model pairs; the authors identify O(N) training cost as an open issue.
  • Direct internal-state transfer is less human-readable than text handoffs; the paper does not evaluate auditability or security implications.

What we still don't know

  • Independent reproduction of accuracy and latency results across model pairs and benchmarks.
  • End-to-end matched comparison including fuser training, cache transformation overhead, serving costs and realistic multi-turn agent workloads.
  • Measure transfer robustness, failure containment and audit/security properties of internal-state communication.

Why it matters

C2C treats model internal state as a communication medium rather than converting it to readable text. If its reported gains transfer, multi-model systems could avoid serial decoding overhead and transfer information that text handoffs lose. The design also creates a less inspectable channel and requires learned alignment/fusion; the paper’s results are benchmark-bounded and pairwise training cost remains a scaling issue.
