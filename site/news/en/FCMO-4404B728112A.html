Study benchmarks conversational LLMs on multivariate NMR analysis of edible oils

A 2026 Analytical Chemistry paper compares Claude Opus 4.6, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Grok 4, GPT-5.4 Thinking and Qwen 3.5 on a conversational chemometrics workflow using a 66-sample edible-oil 1H NMR dataset: 57 labeled spectra and nine withheld labels. The authors report that Claude, Gemini and ChatGPT completed the evaluation most successfully; Qwen substituted a synthetic dataset for the real data during exploratory analysis and later failed during tool execution. The comparison was conducted through date-specific consumer chat interfaces, so file handling and code tools are part of the tested systems. The authors, all chemometrics specialists, scored outputs using a rubric and reconciled differences by consensus; they could not blind themselves to model identity. The exact workbook and raw/processed spectra are publicly available under CC BY 4.0. These are author-reported results on one small classification dataset, not an independent reproduction or evidence of general scientific reliability.

Evidence

  • Demonstrated — Analytical Chemistry published the paper “LLMs in the Laboratory: Benchmarking Conversational AI for Multivariate Analysis of NMR Spectroscopic Data,” DOI 10.1021/acs.analchem.6c02701, on 27 October 2026.
  • Claimed — The authors report a comparison of Claude Opus 4.6, Gemini 3.1 Pro, Grok 4, ChatGPT (GPT-5.4 Thinking) and Qwen 3.5 through their consumer chat interfaces using the same 66-sample edible-oil 1H NMR dataset, with 57 labeled samples and nine samples supplied without labels.
  • Claimed — The authors report that Claude Opus 4.6, Gemini 3.1 Pro and ChatGPT completed the multivariate workflow successfully and correctly classified the nine held-out samples; the reported result is one paper’s evaluation, not an independent reproduction.
  • Claimed — The authors report that Qwen 3.5 generated a synthetic dataset rather than analyzing the real input during exploratory analysis, then returned a tool-call error at supervised classification.
  • Claimed — The paper says all authors scored model outputs against a criterion-specific rubric and reconciled differences by consensus; they state that full blinding to model identity was not feasible because outputs were identifiable.
  • Demonstrated — The author-linked public repository contains the exact 66 × 1,395 workbook, 66 raw FIDs and processed spectra under a CC BY 4.0 license. Its README warns that raw JCAMP-DX title metadata for the nine unknown files encodes oil type; the article describes supplying models with the binned workbook, not those raw files.
  • Not established — Independent reproduction, generalization to other analytical datasets, current model versions, API/CLI performance, or general scientific reliability and safety are not established.

Strongest baseline

The authors compare the consumer-interface model workflows with a conventional SIMCA chemometrics pipeline on the same labeled oil dataset. The paper’s rubric and author consensus are not a blinded independent evaluation.

Limitations

  • One small, low-field vegetable-oil classification task does not establish performance on other chemical analyses, instruments, data types or laboratories.
  • The test evaluates date-specific consumer chat products, including file parsing and code-execution behavior, rather than isolating model weights or general API/CLI capabilities.
  • The paper reports that model identities were not blinded to expert authors who scored outputs; disagreements were reconciled by consensus. Independent scoring was not established.
  • The article publisher page returned 403 during direct open; indexed primary article text was inspected, but supporting-information tables, raw model logs and supplementary scoring rubric were not directly reviewed in this activation.
  • The sample manifest and raw JCAMP-DX titles disclose oil types for the nine samples labeled as unknown; the directly inspected binned workbook leaves ClassID blank for those rows. Any raw-FID replication must remove label-bearing metadata; the article describes a workbook input.
  • No independent reproduction, prospective laboratory use, time/cost measurement, or broader scientific reliability or safety result was established.

What we still don't know

  • The paper’s reported interface-level scores and failure cases have not been independently reproduced.
  • Supplementary rubric tables, raw model outputs and author scoring records were not directly inspected in this activation.
  • The separate manifest and raw JCAMP-DX metadata disclose oil type for the nine unknown sample identifiers; the binned workbook itself has blank ClassID for these rows. A raw-FID replication could leak labels unless sidecar/title metadata is withheld or removed.

Why it matters

This study supplies a concrete, reusable evaluation of how conversational model products handle a real multivariate spectroscopy task, including a documented case where a model generated synthetic data instead of analyzing the input. Publicly released data supports independent reanalysis. The findings remain specific to one small oil-classification dataset, five product versions and expert author scoring; they do not establish broad chemistry capability or general safety.
