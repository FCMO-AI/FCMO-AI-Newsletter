What changed

A Nov 10 technical report introduces the Multimodal Knowledge Agent (MKA) benchmark: 200 private, multi-file professional tasks spanning 10 sectors and 83 occupations, plus 10 disjoint public showcase tasks. The tasks require agents to combine visually dense files and often produce standalone deliverables. The authors evaluate four frontier models in a common Stirrup harness and report that removing image viewing lowered Gemini 4 Carbon’s Climb-WSR by 5.94 percentage points while increasing turns and output tokens. Reported quality differences among the top three models were statistically indistinguishable; model scores and cost estimates remain paper-reported.

Evidence

  • Demonstrated — Google DeepMind published a dated technical report introducing MKA on 2026-11-10, with a separate 10-task public showcase and an Artificial Analysis-hosted leaderboard.
  • Claimed — The report says MKA contains 200 private professional tasks across 10 sectors and 83 occupations, with 2–45 input files per task; 88% require standalone deliverables. Four frontier models were run in Stirrup v0.2.1 under a common tool set and resource budget.
  • Claimed — In a 50-task, 9-sector study, the report says its sandboxed agentic autorater achieved 96.2% weighted pairwise criterion agreement with human raters (95% CI 94.7–97.6; Cohen’s κ=0.91). The study filtered toward high-weight criteria and a balanced pass/fail mix; this agreement may not transfer to lower-weight formatting checks.
  • Claimed — The report says Fable 5.2, Gemini 4 Carbon and Opus 5.5 were statistically indistinguishable on Climb-WSR in this evaluation; Carbon used 5.74× fewer tokens and had 6.26× lower list-price API cost than Fable 5.2. Removing image viewing from Carbon reduced Climb-WSR by 5.94 percentage points and increased turns by 1.28×. These are report results, not independent measurements.
  • Not established — The report does not establish that MKA scores predict performance across professional work outside these tasks, or that the autorater agreement generalizes to every criterion or population. Each model ran once per task and received one autorater pass, so run-to-run agent and grader variance are not captured; the private task set was not independently inspected here.

Strongest baseline

Within the paper, four models share Stirrup v0.2.1, tools, sandbox and budgets. The top three reported Climb-WSR estimates are statistically indistinguishable; cross-benchmark superiority is not established.

Limitations

  • The 200 evaluation tasks are private; only 10 disjoint showcase tasks are downloadable.
  • Each model runs once per task and receives one autorater pass; confidence intervals cover task sampling but not run-to-run agent or grader variance.
  • The human agreement study covers 50 tasks across 9 sectors and focuses on high-weight criteria with a balanced pass/fail mix; agreement may not transfer to low-weight formatting checks.
  • The vision ablation is performed only on Gemini 4 Carbon.
  • The sandbox has internet access, so some information may be retrieved online rather than extracted visually.
  • API cost estimates use provider list prices at report time and may change; they are not invoices or total deployment costs.
  • The report is by Google DeepMind, and Artificial Analysis is a benchmark partner and leaderboard host; the research desk did not independently reproduce the results.

What we still don't know

  • No independent rerun of MKA scores or inspection of task-level outputs was conducted; the 200-task evaluation set is private.
  • Each model was run once/task and graded in one autorater pass, so run-to-run agent and grader variance are not captured.
  • Human-rater agreement was tested on 50 tasks and weighted toward high-weight criteria; transfer to low-weight formatting checks is uncertain.

Why it matters

MKA targets a gap between single-image QA, text-heavy agent benchmarks and real work that combines visual files with finished documents, spreadsheets or presentations. Its task design and tool ablation can help researchers distinguish native visual reasoning from code/OCR workarounds. The private evaluation set limits outside auditing, and the reported scores do not establish general workplace performance.
