ProbeGuard reports risk-controlled abstention from medical answer-consensus trajectories

Wang et al. introduce ProbeGuard, which uses the formation history of a multi-round medical question-answering consensus to decide when to abstain. Its process features track agreement, minority persistence and retrieval; for immediately unanimous answers it also checks rationale agreement and probes with counter-evidence. On MedQA, the authors report a process-score AUROC of 0.696 versus 0.512 for terminal agreement. Their stratified Learn-then-Test rule answered 55.3% of the unanimous layer at 9.0% observed risk against a 15% target across 50 splits, under an assumption that calibration and deployment questions are i.i.d. within each stratum. In specialty analysis, emergency medicine had 19.1% realized risk at the same layer-level threshold, and was not separately certified. These are author-reported multiple-choice benchmark results, not clinical deployment evidence.

Evidence

  • Claimed — On the authors’ MedQA multi-round RAG substrate, ProbeGuard’s process score achieved 0.696 AUROC for distinguishing correct from incorrect consensus, versus 0.512 for terminal agreement. The authors evaluated three 8B backbones and several medical QA benchmark sets.
  • Claimed — For the immediately unanimous MedQA layer, the paper reports 0.553 coverage at 9.0% observed selective risk against a target α=0.15 across 50 calibration/test splits. The distribution-free risk statement assumes calibration and deployment questions are i.i.d. within each fixed stratum.
  • Claimed — Under the layer-level threshold, the emergency-medicine subgroup had 19.1% realized risk at target α=0.15; this specialty-level breakdown was descriptive and was not separately certified.
  • Claimed — The counter-evidence probe had median latency of 114 seconds and 4,381 completion tokens per probed MedQA question, compared with 72 seconds for the underlying answer process; it is applied to the unanimous subset.
  • Demonstrated limitation — The paper evaluates multiple-choice medical QA with one published consensus substrate and three 8B backbones; it does not establish prospective clinician-use, free-text clinical performance, or safety in deployment.

Strongest baseline

For MedQA process discrimination, terminal agreement achieves 0.512 AUROC, near chance; first-round agreement reaches 0.677. The paper compares several abstention signals on the same substrate but does not provide an external clinical baseline.

Limitations

  • Results are reported by the paper authors; no independent reproduction was performed.
  • The 15% selective-risk bound assumes calibration and deployment samples are i.i.d. within each fixed stratum.
  • Emergency medicine had 19.1% realized risk under the aggregate layer threshold; the subgroup was not separately certified.
  • Evaluation uses multiple-choice medical QA, one multi-round retrieval substrate and three 8B backbones; no clinician-use or prospective deployment was studied.
  • The active probe adds a median 114 seconds and 4,381 completion tokens per probed MedQA question, versus 72 seconds for the underlying answer process.

What we still don't know

  • Independent reproduction of the discrimination, calibration, subgroup and cost results.
  • Prospective performance and calibration under real clinical case mix, free-text workflows and clinician routing.
  • Availability of the paper-linked code and data could not be confirmed in this activation.

Why it matters

A unanimous panel can share the same misconception, so final agreement alone may hide error. The paper tests whether interaction traces and targeted counter-evidence can support abstention decisions with a stated statistical bound. Its aggregate guarantee depends on within-stratum distribution stability, while the emergency-medicine subgroup and probe cost show why deployment would need case-mix monitoring, specialty-level calibration and workflow evaluation.
