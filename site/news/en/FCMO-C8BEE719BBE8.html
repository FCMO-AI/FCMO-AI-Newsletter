Anthropic survey finds a wide gap in public and staff expectations for AI and work

Anthropic published an August 2026 survey comparing weighted responses from 10,980 US adults with unweighted responses from 1,334 Anthropic staff. The report finds large differences in respondents’ expectations about AI capability, adoption, time savings and automation of their own work by 2030. For example, 39% of US adults and 91% of staff expected AI to make a Nobel-level discovery by 2030; 5% of employed US respondents versus more than half of staff expected AI to perform at least 80% of their current work. Anthropic also maps complete-case responses through its economic scenario model: median answers imply 5.4% GDP growth for US adults and 17.1% for staff. These are survey responses and conditional model outputs, not measured capability, productivity, employment or forecast accuracy.

Evidence

  • Demonstrated — Anthropic published a report comparing an August 2026 weighted US-adult survey of 10,980 respondents with an unweighted internal poll of 1,334 Anthropic staff. The report states that survey responses are expectations, not capability measurements or company forecasts.
  • Claimed — In the report, 39% of US adults and 91% of Anthropic staff expected AI to be able to make a Nobel-level scientific discovery by 2030. The figures are respondents’ survey answers.
  • Claimed — Among US adults who use AI at work, 26% said a task that current AI is well suited for takes one-quarter of the time or less when using AI, counting checking and fixing; 59% of Anthropic staff gave that answer. This is self-reported task time, not a measured productivity effect.
  • Claimed — Among employed US respondents, the average expected share of their current work hours AI could perform by 2030 was 28%, compared with 75% among Anthropic staff. Five percent of US workers, versus more than half of staff, expected AI to perform at least 80% of their current work.
  • Claimed — When the authors map complete-case survey answers through their economic scenario model, median answers imply GDP growth by 2030 of 5.4% for US adults and 17.1% for Anthropic staff. This is a model-implied scenario based on five survey inputs, with other parameters held at the “Substantial” scenario; it is not a forecast or observed GDP result.
  • Claimed — Anthropic reports a September repeat US-adult survey of 7,613 respondents in which the median answers imply 5.6% GDP growth by 2030 and 44% expected a Nobel-level AI discovery by then, compared with 5.4% and 39% in the August survey. The report says the main results changed little; this does not address the internal staff sample.

Strongest baseline

The report directly compares a weighted US-adult online-panel sample with an unweighted Anthropic internal poll. The samples differ in recruitment and weighting and are not matched populations; this is a comparison of reported expectations, not an estimate of causal group effects.

Limitations

  • The general-public survey used an online opt-in panel. Responses were weighted on demographic variables, which cannot remove all selection differences from a probability sample.
  • The Anthropic poll was an internal, unweighted staff survey; employees may have different information and work exposure than the public. It is not a company forecast.
  • Reported beliefs are not measurements of model capability, adoption, task performance, productivity, employment displacement or macroeconomic outcomes.
  • Time-savings answers are self-reported. The questions ask about tasks respondents consider well suited to current AI and do not measure completed output or causal productivity.
  • The model-implied GDP values use complete-case respondents (3,259 US adults and 1,093 staff), map five answers through a stylized scenario framework, and hold other model parameters at the Substantial scenario. The framework assigns no probabilities and is not a forecast.
  • The linked working paper omits potentially important mechanisms including policy responses, business cycles, aggregate demand effects, financial disruptions, within-group worker heterogeneity and rapid robotics advances; the authors call the model a simplification.
  • The appendix’s 120-order decomposition ranges describe sensitivity to answer-swap order, not statistical confidence intervals. Its state-level comparison uses Anthropic service-usage data and is not independent validation of productivity or economic forecasts.
  • No respondent-level data, full questionnaire or analysis code was found in the inspected sources; this limits independent reanalysis but does not prevent reporting what the authors published.

What we still don't know

  • No independent respondent-level reanalysis or independently recruited comparison survey was located. The public report and appendix disclose some methods but do not provide respondent data or executable analysis code.
  • The report measures expectations, not whether AI capabilities, productivity, job exposure or model-implied GDP outcomes occur.

Why it matters

The survey makes visible how strongly expectations about AI's pace and economic transmission differ between a lab workforce and a weighted US adult panel, a potentially useful input to research and policy debates. The evidence describes beliefs under a stated sample and hypothetical questions; it does not show that the expected capabilities or economic outcomes will occur. The linked scenario model has no assigned probabilities and omits material economic mechanisms.
