CART reports adaptive red teaming finds failures missed by static prompt replay

The CART preprint presents closed-loop red teaming in which a Challenger adapts probes using earlier results while a separate Judge scores the evidence. Across Frontier, JAH and Agentic case families, the authors report higher discovered failure rates and average risk scores than direct replay of seed prompts for targets with available baselines. The main comparisons fix Claude as Challenger and Gemini as Judge; static replay uses the same Judge and rubric. Budgets are 1,000 rounds for Frontier and JAH and 3,200 for Agentic. The paper reports the largest gap on tool-mediated Agentic cases and says observed test-volume share is not a measure of real-world failure prevalence. The result supports adaptive testing as a way to expose benchmark-conditioned weaknesses, not a claim that deployed models fail at the observed rates.

Evidence

  • Claimed — CART uses a closed-loop Challenger–Target–Judge design in which findings from earlier rounds guide later probe selection and adaptation, while retaining probe lineage and scoring records.
  • Claimed — Across Frontier, JAH and Agentic case families, the authors report higher discovered failure rates and average risk scores with adaptive testing than direct replay of stored seed prompts for targets with available baselines; the largest separation is reported in Agentic evaluation.
  • Claimed — The main comparisons fix Claude as Challenger and Gemini as Judge, and static replay uses the same Gemini Judge and scoring rubric; per-run budgets are 1,000 rounds for Frontier/JAH and 3,200 for Agentic.
  • Not established — The paper states that observed test-volume share is not real-world failure prevalence, capabilities and test sets differ across conditions, and Judge/Challenger pairing and risk-score calibration affect which evidence is found.

Strongest baseline

Direct replay of stored seed prompts, evaluated with the same Gemini Judge and rubric as CART. The main comparison fixes Claude as Challenger and Gemini as Judge; a separate Frontier-family experiment varies these roles. This controls evaluator identity for the static/adaptive comparison but does not establish that LLM-judged risk scores are ground truth.

Limitations

  • Results are paper-reported and depend on the selected Challenger, Judge, prompts, budgets, seed sets and scoring rubric; no independent replication was performed.
  • Observed test-volume share and failure rates are not real-world prevalence estimates. Adaptive and static conditions can exercise different capabilities and test difficulty.
  • Risk scores are LLM-Judge dependent. The paper reports stronger peer agreement on binary verdicts than on rationales and score calibration.
  • Each Challenger–Judge interaction cell in the role matrix is one aggregate run, so interaction effects are not separated from run-level randomness.
  • Larger seed banks, taxonomies and budgets increase retrieval, inference and validation costs.

What we still don't know

  • The adaptive-versus-static comparisons have not been independently reproduced with repeated seeds and independent probe generators or judges.
  • Adaptive and static probes can differ in capability coverage and difficulty, and the paper's composite risk score is not independently calibrated to real-world harm.

Why it matters

If the reported comparison holds, fixed benchmark prompts can understate weaknesses that appear when the test context adapts to earlier model behavior. Adaptive, provenance-tracked probes could change how model and agent evaluations allocate a fixed test budget. The paper's results are Judge- and setup-dependent and do not estimate real-world failure prevalence.
