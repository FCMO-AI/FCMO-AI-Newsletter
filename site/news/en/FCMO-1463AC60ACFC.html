OpenAI reports an automated Linux pipeline that reproduced 1,724 kernel vulnerabilities and developed patches for 948 unique LPEs

OpenAI describes a controlled prototype that scanned selected Linux kernel audit targets using an upcoming cyber model and agent pipeline. In its mid-October run, it reports 2,308 candidate findings, 1,724 dynamically reproduced vulnerabilities, 291 information-leak proofs of concept, 1,169 working LPE exploits and 948 distinct patches corresponding to 948 unique LPE vulnerabilities. OpenAI reports about USD 1.21 million in API-credit cost. OpenAI also reports that human review caught 20 wrong-vulnerability attributions among 200 apparent successes in an early run, after which it added an independent path check. The page itself is internally inconsistent: its overview and findings summary say 948 LPE exploits, while the detailed section reports 1,169 working exploits and 948 distinct patches. These remain first-party results from one workflow: independent reproduction, maintainer acceptance of all patches, transfer beyond the tested configurations and generalized security impact are not established.

Evidence

  • Demonstrated — OpenAI published a technical account on 4 November 2026 describing a prototype for Linux kernel vulnerability discovery, dynamic validation, exploitability assessment and patch generation.
  • Claimed — OpenAI reports that a mid-October run identified 2,308 candidate findings in selected Linux kernel targets, dynamically reproduced 1,724 vulnerabilities, generated 291 information-leak proofs of concept and 1,169 working local privilege-escalation exploits, then developed 948 distinct patches corresponding to 948 unique LPE vulnerabilities after variant analysis.
  • Claimed — OpenAI reports about USD 1.21 million in API-credit cost for the described run and says its audit targets covered about 7% of the kernel codebase; these figures apply to its stated prototype, target configuration and workflow.
  • Not established — The inspected account does not establish independent reproduction, maintainer acceptance of the generated patches, equivalent results on other kernels or threat models, or a general AI vulnerability-discovery rate.
  • Claimed — OpenAI reports that an early run counted 200 successful local privilege escalations, but human review found 20 reached root through a different zero-day than the assigned vulnerability; it says the later workflow tightened constraints and added an independent check that each final exploit used its assigned vulnerability.
  • Disputed — The same OpenAI page is internally inconsistent about the count: its overview and findings summary say 948 working LPE exploits, while the detailed exploitability section reports 1,169 working LPE exploits and 948 distinct patches, using 948 to count unique LPE vulnerabilities. The report does not reconcile this difference.

  • The account reports 1,169 working LPE exploits and 948 distinct patches but does not provide a per-finding public mapping in the inspected page.
  • Results and cost are from one provider-reported workflow on selected targets and do not establish performance or economics across kernels, models or threat models.
  • The report identifies 20 wrong-vulnerability attributions among 200 early apparent successes and describes a subsequent independent path check, but does not expose a per-finding public audit sufficient to independently measure final attribution error.
  • OpenAI’s overview/summary says 948 working LPE exploits, but its detailed section reports 1,169 working exploits and 948 distinct patches; the source does not reconcile its count wording.

Why it matters

If independently reproduced, this pipeline could expand the volume of kernel security findings defenders can validate and remediate. The report also shows that candidate triage, dynamic validation, exploitation assessment and patch review remain separate costly stages. The report’s own early false-success episode shows why exploit-path attribution must be checked separately from obtaining root. Its measured scale and cost are specific to one provider-reported run, so it does not establish a general discovery rate or cost advantage.
