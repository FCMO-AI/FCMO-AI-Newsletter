PUN reports noise-aware planning results on geometric 2D–4D benchmarks

A new preprint introduces Planning Under Noise (PUN), a neural operator that maps obstacle geometry, goal, cost and noise inputs to a desirability field, then uses bounded local search to guide stochastic replanning. On the authors’ 4D geometric benchmark it reports 95–100% success at 10.51–11.66 ms/start across four tested noise levels. The paper says compared methods share the saved test queries and noise sequence, but PUN timings use a B200 GPU while listed baseline timings use faster CPU implementations; only one trained model per dimension is evaluated. The method assumes control–noise matching, and the authors report no full robot/contact models and continued difficulty on adversarial mazes. These are credible but unconfirmed paper-reported results, not a demonstrated robotics deployment.

Evidence

  • Claimed — The authors introduce Planning Under Noise (PUN), which learns a geometry-conditioned desirability/value field from partially converged Walk-on-Stars Monte Carlo labels and uses bounded local search over the learned field for replanning under control execution noise. The derivation relies on a control–noise matching assumption.
  • Claimed — In the paper’s 4D replanning benchmark, PUN reports success rates of 100%, 100%, 100% and 95% across noise levels λ=0.25, 1, 2 and 4, with reported runtime 10.63, 10.51, 10.55 and 11.66 ms/start. At λ=4 the listed A* baseline reports 59.17% success and 62.44 ms/start; other baselines also have lower reported success and longer listed runtimes. These are paper-reported geometric simulation results, with PUN GPU timings and CPU baseline timings.
  • Claimed — Appendix D.1 says replanning methods share saved environment-goal queries and Gaussian noise sequences; the 4D set uses 480 maps with three goals and one start per goal. The authors report one trained model per dimension and map-level bootstrap intervals; repeated model-training seeds are not reported.
  • Demonstrated limitation — The paper states its experiments do not include full robot models with contact, are restricted to settings where control and noise are matched, and find adversarial maze-like geometries challenging. Its reflecting-boundary value field is also evaluated in collision-failure rollouts, which the authors say is not the exact value function for that execution model.
  • Not established — No independent reproduction, verified code artifact, physical-robot evaluation, contact-rich manipulation result, or transfer beyond the paper’s control–noise-matched geometric benchmarks is established by this review.

Strongest baseline

At λ=4 in 4D, RRT has the closest reported success rate among the listed methods (85.83% versus PUN 95%) and uses 38.31 ms/start versus PUN 11.66 ms/start. All listed comparator runtimes in this table use CPU implementations because they were faster than their GPU counterparts; PUN uses GPU timing, so the runtime comparison is implementation-and-device specific. A* is an important counterpoint at lower noise and reports 100% success at λ=0.25 and 96.67% at λ=1.

Limitations

  • The screened-Poisson derivation requires a control–noise matching assumption, and experiments are restricted to settings where the authors consider that assumption reasonable.
  • No full robot models with contact or physical robot trials are included; transfer to contact-rich manipulation is untested.
  • The authors state adversarial geometries such as mazes remain challenging because long corridors can produce weak or poorly resolved learned gradients.
  • The field is derived for reflecting boundaries, while experiments count obstacle collisions as rollout failures; the authors note this is not the exact value function for the collision-failure execution model.
  • Appendix D.1 reports one trained model per dimension; bootstrap resampling of maps quantifies query-set uncertainty but does not establish training-seed variability or independent replication.
  • Table 5 compares PUN GPU timings with CPU timings for the listed baselines; hardware and implementation differ, so the runtime ratios are not hardware-normalized.
  • No official artifact or independent reproduction surfaced in this activation’s bounded search; absence from search results is not proof that none exists.

What we still don't know

  • The 4D benchmark, reported success rates and runtime results have not been independently reproduced, and only one trained model per dimension is reported.
  • The paper’s key control–noise matching assumption and collision-failure execution evaluation limit transfer to realistic robot dynamics.
  • Published runtime comparisons use a B200 GPU for PUN and CPU implementations for the baseline methods.

Why it matters

A learned global value field paired with bounded test-time search could make stochastic planning over unseen higher-dimensional geometries less dependent on a per-instance PDE solve. The reported benchmark is promising and directly relevant to robotics planning research, but its transfer depends on a restrictive control–noise matching regime and has not been tested on contact-rich physical robots. Cross-device runtimes should not be read as a hardware-normalized speed comparison.
