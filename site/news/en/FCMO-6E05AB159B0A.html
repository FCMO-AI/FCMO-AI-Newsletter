What changed

The September 2026 OSWorld-Pro preprint introduces a process-based benchmark for computer-use agents with more than 300 tasks, 2,800 sequential subgoals and over 67,000 human annotations. Authors use human-aligned language-model judges to score subgoal fulfillment through trajectories. They report Claude Opus 5 at 75.7% on OSWorld-Pro versus 83.4% on OSWorld and identify failure patterns such as irrelevant actions and click mistakes.

Evidence

  • Claimed — Authors report a benchmark of 300+ tasks, 2,800+ subgoals and 67,000+ human annotations. Their reported top score for Claude Opus 5 is 75.7% on OSWorld-Pro versus 83.4% on OSWorld; they identify subgoal-irrelevant actions and click-based mistakes as process-level failure modes.
  • Demonstrated — The arXiv record identifies the paper and its submission/revision metadata. This confirms that the preprint was posted, not that its experimental claims have been independently established.

Strongest baseline

OSWorld end-state functional evaluation; reported Claude Opus 5 scores are 75.7% on OSWorld-Pro and 83.4% on OSWorld. Difference should not be read as a hidden error-rate estimate because task sets and evaluation regimes differ.

Limitations

  • Quantitative results and judge quality are author-reported; no independent reproduction or blind audit was inspected.
  • The 75.7% OSWorld-Pro and 83.4% OSWorld figures are from different task/evaluation regimes and are not directly interpretable as an absolute hidden-error estimate.
  • Process annotations may privilege a preferred path and miss valid alternative trajectories; rare GUI states may remain underrepresented.
  • Language-model judging adds model dependence and evaluation cost; agreement by failure type and operational usefulness are not established here.

What we still don't know

  • No independent reproduction or blind, failure-class-specific human audit of the LLM judges was inspected.
  • No test of whether process scores predict future task success or improve interventions was inspected.

Why it matters

A final artifact score hides where a long computer-use trajectory failed. Process-level labels may distinguish subgoal selection, action execution and recovery errors, making interventions more targeted; they also introduce annotation and judge-model costs and can mis-score valid alternative paths.
