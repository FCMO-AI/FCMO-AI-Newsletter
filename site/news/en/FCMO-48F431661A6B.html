What actually changed

The arXiv v1 paper describes Rosetta, a multi-agent pipeline that turns architecture-paper PDFs into a mathematical specification, executable Python model and plain-English interpretation. In 12 expert-reviewed papers, evaluators rated specification and interpretation quality 4 or 5 out of 5 for 10 papers and reported no significant hallucinations. A six-group author self-evaluation reported concrete paper changes in four cases. The paper also reports a broader 97-paper benchmark result, but its abstract, results section and conclusion give inconsistent denominators and percentages; that result is not summarized as settled.

Claim vs. evidence

  • CLAIMED — The authors describe Rosetta as an autonomous multi-agent pipeline that converts a research-paper PDF into a mathematical specification, executable Python model and plain-English interpretation, using first-principles derivation, separate functional/scientific verification and a three-run selector.
  • CLAIMED — For 12 selected architecture papers assessed by independent researchers, the paper reports specification-plus-interpretation scores of at least 4/5 on 10 papers and zero significant hallucinations; it also reports model scores from 2/5 to 5/5, including named specification or baseline failures.
  • CLAIMED — In a six-group author self-evaluation, the paper reports that Rosetta output led to revisions or new experiments in four active submissions and five evaluators said they would use it again.
  • DISPUTED — The paper gives inconsistent CS2 breadth-study denominators and rates: the abstract reports 56% of fit-screened papers and says 97 papers were scored; Section VI reports 60% of 97 unfiltered papers; the conclusion reports 56% across 78. The aggregate rate and denominator are unresolved within v1.
  • NOT_ESTABLISHED — The paper does not establish independent reproduction, transfer to other frontier models, or dependable numerical correctness across architecture papers beyond its reported studies.

Strongest baseline

Human evaluation of generated artifacts against source papers and paper claims; no matched competing model-generation system or independent replication is reported.

The caveat that matters

  • Single arXiv v1 preprint; no independent reproduction was conducted.
  • The 12-paper CS1 study uses human evaluators, but the paper reports that expertise was broad rather than deeply specialized and that evaluator scores vary by paper.
  • Model quality ranges from 2/5 to 5/5; the paper describes an incorrect baseline model and a model faithfully carrying forward a flawed specification.
  • The six-group CS3 results are a small author self-evaluation.
  • The experiments use Claude Opus 4.5 and do not establish cross-model transfer.
  • The CS2 breadth-study denominator and rate conflict between abstract, main text and conclusion.

What remains unknown

  • The author-reported evaluations and generated performance models were not independently reproduced.
  • The v1 abstract, breadth-study results and conclusion disagree on the CS2 denominator and Tier-A percentage (97 / 60% versus 78 / 56%, with another fit-screened 56% formulation).
  • All experiments use Claude Opus 4.5; portability to newer or different model families is not evaluated.

FCMO Lens

If the reported method holds up, automatically deriving auditable performance models could reduce the expert effort needed to inspect hardware claims and expose missing assumptions. The current evidence is one preprint, with small human-evaluation samples, disclosed model-quality failures and inconsistent aggregate benchmark figures.
