Paper finds asynchronous delivery order can change search agents’ acquired evidence and proposes schedule-stable coordination

A preprint reports that changing the delivery order of identical evidence packets can alter what search agents retrieve, with the difference persisting after all initial evidence arrives and through another search. Across controlled QA tasks, early commitment produced 11.6–23.8 percentage points of acquisition disagreement above same-order repeat variation in eight model-task settings. The paper proposes Confluent Evidence Acquisition (CEA), which waits for each subquestion’s assigned evidence and uses a schedule-independent rule to allocate shared searches. In question-only plans, the authors report acquisition disagreement near repeat variation across three benchmarks; accuracy was comparable to a complete-input control and improved over early commitment on 2Wiki and HotpotQA, but not MuSiQue. These are author-reported results in a frozen-corpus QA setting, not an independently reproduced finding or a demonstration across deployed agents.

Evidence

  • Claimed — The inspected preprint reports controlled experiments in which changing the delivery order of identical evidence packets redirected search choices; the reported acquisition disagreement persisted after input completion and through a second search.
  • Claimed — Across eight model-task settings, early commitment produced 11.6–23.8 percentage points of excess acquisition disagreement over same-order repeat variation. With question-only plans, the authors report that CEA reduced disagreement to 0.6, -0.2 and 1.0 points on 2Wiki, HotpotQA and MuSiQue, respectively.
  • Claimed — In the question-only-plan evaluation, the preprint reports that CEA improved accuracy over early commitment by 12.6 percentage points on 2Wiki and 3.6 points on HotpotQA, with a -0.1-point change on MuSiQue; results were comparable to the complete-input shared-history control.
  • Inferred — When tool results arrive asynchronously, completion-order scheduling can affect which evidence supports later reasoning, so evidence provenance and scheduler policy may need to be audited together.

Strongest baseline

Matched complete-input shared-history controls and early commitment after two arrivals, with identical questions, evidence packets, retriever and model settings; same-order repeated runs estimate stochastic variation. The paper also compares fixed-priority CEA allocation with first-ready allocation under low and high search contention.

Limitations

  • This is one author-reported preprint; the research desk did not independently reproduce the experiments or verify the formal claims.
  • The evaluations use frozen corpora and multi-hop QA tasks, not open-web research, changing source quality, long-running autonomous projects or deployed organizational workflows.
  • Most controlled results use DeepSeek-V4-Flash; cross-model checks use fixed subsets and GPT-5.6 Luna. Generalization to other model families, schedulers, tool APIs and runtime architectures is unknown.
  • The papers' estimated excess-disagreement measures depend on two repeats per order, selected questions and the authors' target-category/document-set metrics; other evidence-quality and provenance measures could yield different conclusions.
  • Question-only plans and retrieval-call ownership structure CEA's dependency domains. More complex plans, shared evidence dependencies and dynamically changing evidence were not established here.
  • The 21.6-point first-ready allocation effect is a high-contention setup with two missing supports and one shared search slot; ordinary contention frequency and production impact are unknown.
  • The timed comparison reports small final-response reductions in the measured staggered-delivery tasks; simultaneous arrival gives essentially no speedup, and external tool-service costs are not fully charged.

What we still don't know

  • The schedule-permutation and CEA results have not been independently reproduced, and no code artifact was inspected in this review.
  • The effect of asynchronous scheduling on evidence acquisition in open-web research and production agent runtimes is unknown.
  • The practical frequency and size of contention-induced acquisition divergence under real shared retrieval budgets are not reported.

Why it matters

The paper separates answer accuracy from reproducibility of the evidence path: two runs can receive the same information overall yet acquire different support because asynchronous timing changes which search happens first. If this mechanism generalizes, scheduler and tool-allocation choices become part of research-agent evidence selection and auditability. CEA suggests a design that lets independent reasoning progress while stabilizing both subquestion evidence and shared search allocation. Current evidence is limited to controlled multi-hop QA tasks and one preprint, so transfer to open-web research, production agents, other schedulers and real operating costs remains unestablished.
