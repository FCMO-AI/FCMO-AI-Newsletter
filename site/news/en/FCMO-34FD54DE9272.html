FIRE reports failure-derived runtime policies improve repeated agent task success

An arXiv preprint studies runtime policies derived from observed agent failures: targeted instructions or action denials trigger at risky execution states without changing model weights or user prompts. The authors report a randomized five-arm Terminal-Bench panel in which their frozen policies improve success on eligible tasks over baseline and timing-matched sham, while generic verify and reconsider prompts do not reproduce the result. Across an 87-task subset of Terminal-Bench 2.1, the authors also report higher two-attempt success for three model tiers. These are author-reported findings: the prespecified panel policy-versus-sham estimate is +25.0 percentage points with a wide 95% interval [7.1, 46.4] and p=0.061; two tasks were excluded because pinned images could not install the agent. The evidence is limited to English terminal tasks and has no independent replication.

Evidence

  • Demonstrated — The arXiv preprint describes runtime policies derived from observed task failures, implemented as targeted instructions or action denials triggered by task and execution state without changing model weights or user prompts.
  • Claimed — In the authors’ randomized five-arm panel on 14 policy-eligible Terminal-Bench tasks, the frozen runtime policies pass 17/28 attempts (60.7%), compared with 10/28 (35.7%) for a timing-matched sham and 11/28 (39.3%) for baseline. The prespecified policy-versus-sham difference is +25.0 points with a 95% interval [7.1, 46.4] and p=0.061; the estimate is large but imprecise.
  • Claimed — Across the authors’ 87-task Terminal-Bench subset and two attempts per task, policy-guided repeated success rises from 50.6% to 54.0% for Luna, 55.2% to 60.9% for Terra, and 64.4% to 73.6% for Sol. The complete suite excludes two of the benchmark’s 89 tasks because their pinned images could not install the agent.
  • Demonstrated limitation — The authors report a benchmark-specific result from frozen policies and English terminal tasks; policies were derived from failures in the same research program. The paper provides no independent reproduction or evidence of transfer to other domains, harnesses, languages or production agents.

Strongest baseline

For causal attribution, the authors’ timing-matched sham on the same eligible tasks is the primary comparator; generic always-verify and reconsider arms are secondary controls. The primary policy-versus-sham estimate is imprecise.

Limitations

  • All performance results are author-reported; no independent reproduction was located or performed.
  • The prespecified five-arm primary contrast has a wide interval and p=0.061; the 14 eligible tasks provide limited precision.
  • The two-attempt design measures observed repeatability but does not identify per-task success probabilities.
  • Complete-suite results cover 87 of 89 Terminal-Bench tasks after two image-installation exclusions.
  • Evaluation uses English terminal tasks, the authors’ selected models and harness, and policies derived from the same research program; external validity is unknown.

What we still don't know

  • No independent reproduction of the frozen runtime-policy portfolio or five-arm comparison was located. Attempt-level paper results remain author-reported.
  • Transfer beyond English Terminal-Bench tasks, these models, this harness and policies derived from this research program is unmeasured.
  • Two of 89 Terminal-Bench tasks were omitted from complete-suite comparisons because their pinned images could not install the agent.

Why it matters

If independently reproduced and transferred, harness policies tied to observable failure states could make agent solutions more dependable without retraining. The reported experiment offers a controlled but small, author-run result: its prespecified policy-versus-sham interval is wide, coverage is limited to English terminal tasks, and broad transfer is not established.
