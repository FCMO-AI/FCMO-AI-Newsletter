Evidence

  • Claimed — The authors introduce Into the Unknown, a benchmark of 15 computational follow-up research tasks proposed by 10 scientists, with task-specific direction and integrity rubrics across five scientific domains.
  • Claimed — Across 135 submission records from nine frontier agents, the authors report that none passes all three strict gates (integrity, completeness and sufficiency) and that 114 submissions fail the integrity gate under automated rubric grading.
  • Demonstrated — The paper describes scientist-authored rubrics and states that the benchmark's tasks, code and agent outputs are publicly available through linked author artifacts; this inspection confirmed the public official code repository and its task, artifact and trajectory structure.
  • Not established — These results do not establish the performance of AI agents across all scientific research or prove that every flagged integrity issue would be upheld by independent human adjudication.

Strongest baseline

The paper compares nine tested frontier-agent configurations against the same task-specific rubrics. It does not provide a general human baseline for completing these tasks, and the result should not be read as one.

Limitations

  • All benchmark outcomes are author-reported and were graded by automated judges using task-specific scientist-authored rubrics; the research desk did not independently regrade or reproduce them.
  • The benchmark contains 15 computational follow-up tasks from 10 scientists across five domains; the selected tasks do not represent all science, experimental work or autonomous research settings.
  • The headline denominator is 135 submission records, while the official dataset card previously inspected says four GLM-5.3 runs have no PDF; submission records and paper artifacts must not be treated as interchangeable.
  • The result that no submission passes all three strict gates depends on the authors' chosen rubric clauses and strict all-nodes-pass criterion; the benchmark is not an externally calibrated measure of general scientific ability.
  • A failure on this benchmark does not show that agents cannot assist scientists or perform narrower research tasks; it indicates that these submissions did not meet the specified follow-up standards under this setup.

What we still don't know

  • The integrity and research-quality judgments were produced by automated rubric graders; no independent human regrade or judge-agreement analysis was performed in this review.
  • The published total is 135 submission records, but four GLM-5.3 runs reportedly produced no PDF; the relationship between submission records, graded artifacts and the published denominator needs explicit accounting.
  • The benchmark covers 15 expert-selected computational follow-up tasks and five science domains; it does not sample the broader space of scientific work, experimental research or all agent/harness configurations.

Why it matters

The benchmark targets a gap between an AI-written research paper and a follow-up that is complete, scientifically directed and supported by verifiable execution. If its findings hold, integrity and evidence tracing are major bottlenecks for autonomous research agents. The result is limited to 15 computational tasks and automated rubric grading, and the authors' submission count includes four GLM-5.3 runs for which no PDF was produced.
