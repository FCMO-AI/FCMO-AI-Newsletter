What changed

A new arXiv preprint fits a joint scaling law for multilingual mixture-of-experts models across model size, training tokens per language, expert count and language coverage. The authors report improved prediction on held-out configurations and a smaller loss penalty when increasing coverage from 12 to 200 languages with more experts, at matched active parameters and tokens per language. These remain author-reported results from a controlled, sub-billion-parameter training grid.

Evidence

  • Claimed — The authors report that their repetition-aware joint scaling law predicts held-out configurations with mean RMSE 0.03319, 28% below their strongest baseline using alternative inputs (JointMoE with per-language training tokens, RMSE 0.04617). The fit uses 26,300 per-language loss observations from 64 primary configurations.
  • Claimed — At matched active parameter count and training tokens per language, the authors report that increasing experts from 1 to 32 reduced the mean loss penalty for expanding training coverage from 12 to 200 languages by 83% (0.0796 to 0.0135), averaged across four model sizes. External text-loss effects were directionally consistent, while downstream accuracy effects varied by task.
  • Not established — The study does not establish that the fitted law or 83% estimate transfers unchanged to frontier-scale multilingual training, other corpora or language mixtures, or production compute budgets; the main configuration grid uses sub-billion active-parameter models and the reported compute estimate excludes routing and communication overhead.

Strongest baseline

For held-out prediction, the strongest evaluated baseline after adapting inputs is JointMoE using per-language training tokens (mean RMSE 0.04617), compared with 0.03319 for the proposed law. For the language expansion result, the dense E=1 model is compared with the E=32 MoE at matched active parameters and per-language training tokens.

Limitations

  • All reported measurements are from one preprint and have not been independently reproduced.
  • The primary factorial grid spans 92.8–589.8M active parameters; one larger model is a stress test, not a frontier-scale training validation.
  • The main 64 configurations use one seed each; three-seed replication is limited to selected comparisons near 190M active parameters.
  • The 83% estimate is an average over four model sizes and a specific nested 12-to-200 language expansion at matched training tokens per language; task-level effects vary.
  • The study uses specific FineWeb/FineWeb2 language mixtures and its own tokenizer and sampling procedure.
  • Training compute is approximated as 6ND and excludes routing and communication overheads; full economic or production cost is not established.

What we still don't know

  • No independent reproduction of the scaling-law fit or matched multilingual coverage comparisons has been inspected.
  • The main 64-configuration grid uses one seed per configuration; multi-seed checks focus on a subset near 190M active parameters.
  • Logical training FLOPs omit routing and communication overhead, and the study does not measure frontier-scale serving or training costs.

Why it matters

If replicated beyond the studied scale and language mixtures, the interaction between expert capacity and language coverage could inform how training compute and stored parameters are allocated in multilingual models, reducing a measured loss trade-off from adding languages. The current evidence is a preprint study on a controlled, sub-billion-active-parameter grid; it does not establish frontier-scale or production economics.
