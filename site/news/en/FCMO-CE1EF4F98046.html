What changed

The authors analyze 8,199 runs over eleven days from an open-source SQL client’s agent mode, covering 39 locally served open-weight models and one hosted control across six task surfaces. They report that 1,590 of 2,100 model-attributed losses (75.7%) occurred after at least one tool call; the majority persists in 99.7% of model-clustered resamples. Five server-side changes, without model or prompt changes, moved six selected models by 6–21 cells out of 30. The paper cautions that its transport/clock/verification/capability loss-class ordering is specific to this corpus and does not generalize.

Evidence

  • Claimed — In an 11-day production corpus of 8,199 runs, the authors attribute 2,100 agent-mode losses to named models; 1,590 (75.7%) followed at least one tool call. The majority persisted in 99.7% of clustered resamples over models.
  • Claimed — The authors report that five server-side interventions, changing no model, prompt or sampling setting, moved six selected models by 6–21 cells out of 30.
  • Claimed — The paper reports a context-memory confound in which an uncapped 7.1 GB model loaded a 262,144-token context and held 51 GB on a 64 GB machine, producing runs that ordinary logs could misclassify as model timeouts.

Strongest baseline

Within the paper, pre-intervention runs on the same hardware, prompts and tasks are compared with selected post-intervention readings. These before/after cells were selected from low-scoring open cases and are not a randomized causal estimate.

Limitations

  • Single-product production corpus (LibreDB Studio) and six task surfaces; external generalization is unestablished.
  • Authors are contributors to the evaluated software. The paper releases artifacts, but this does not constitute independent replication.
  • Loss-class ordering varies under model-clustered resampling and is described by the authors as a property of this corpus rather than a general result.
  • The six before/after model readings were selected from open low-scoring cells and have only 30 runs each; authors report them as field observations, not effect-size estimates.
  • The paper identifies earlier work on tool-feedback and harness-mediated failures; its contribution is production-scale evidence and server-side diagnostic/intervention detail, not discovery that harnesses can matter.

What we still don't know

  • No independent group has audited the released corpus/scorer or reproduced the failure decomposition and server interventions.
  • The study covers one SQL client product; authors are contributors to the evaluated software. Transfer to other tools, tasks and server contracts is unknown.
  • Before/after readings were selected from low-scoring/open cells and use 30 runs per model; the paper explicitly warns these are field observations, not an effect-size estimate.

Why it matters

If the result transfers, tool-agent failures can be misattributed to model capability when interface and server defects are the binding cause; fixing one harness defect may improve several models at once. The paper is a single-product production study by contributors to the evaluated software, and its selected intervention readings do not establish general effect sizes.
