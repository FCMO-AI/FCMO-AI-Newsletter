Helen Jenne, Davis Brown, Jesse He and Henry Kvinge introduce OpenConjecture, a dataset of conjectures drawn from recent mathematics preprints, and analyze a random sample of 200 conjectures from 200 papers. Across three successive GPT-model passes, the authors report proposed proofs or counterexamples for 125 items; 84% passed a review by a new instance of the same model that generated each response. The paper explicitly does not treat those outcomes as expert-verified solutions. Its analyses flag cases where counterexamples appear to exploit missing assumptions or depart from authors’ intended meaning, and where proofs follow existing results. The work argues that mathematical-agent evaluation should assess fidelity to the stated problem, relationship to prior literature, attribution and novelty, in addition to formal or literal correctness. It is a first-party, non-expert-verified study, not a measured rate of newly solved open problems.

Evidence

  • Claimed — The authors introduce OpenConjecture, a dataset of conjectures extracted from mathematics arXiv preprints, and study one conjecture sampled from each of 200 randomly selected papers in a June 21, 2026 dataset snapshot.
  • Claimed — Across three sequential passes using GPT-5.6 Sol, GPT-5.6 Sol Pro and GPT-6 Astra Pro, the authors report proposed counterexamples for 70 conjectures and proofs for 55; 84% of proposed resolutions passed review by a new instance of the same model that produced them. The paper does not report these as expert-verified or newly solved open problems.
  • Claimed — The authors report that reviewers judged many first- and second-pass counterexamples to be standard or off-the-shelf constructions, that some may exploit missing assumptions or statement imprecision, and that some proofs follow closely from existing literature. These classifications rely on model review and non-specialist analysis rather than expert adjudication.
  • Not established — The study does not establish expert-verified correctness, novelty or attribution for the proposed solutions, nor a controlled comparison across model families, repeated runs or recommended harnesses. Its proposed-resolution counts must not be interpreted as rates of newly solved open problems.

Strongest baseline

No model-family benchmark baseline is presented. The study analyzes candidate outputs across its three sequential passes and compares their stated traits; its authors explicitly do not position the work as a controlled model comparison.

Limitations

  • Proposed solution correctness was reviewed by another instance of the same model rather than domain experts; shared model errors may affect both solver and reviewer.
  • The reported counts of candidate proofs and counterexamples are not counts or rates of newly solved open problems.
  • The study used three GPT models, one sampled attempt per conjecture in a staged protocol, and no controlled comparison of model families or their recommended harnesses.
  • Dataset labeling and filtering use language models; conjectures may rely on implicit context, missing assumptions or author intent that extraction does not preserve.
  • Some proof relationship, counterexample novelty and statement-fidelity judgments are based on LLM review or non-specialist analysis, not expert adjudication.
  • The linked dataset page and code repository are publicly accessible, but the files were not pinned or audited in this review; their evolving snapshot metadata is not fully reconciled, and the dataset viewer labels its license as “other”.

What we still don't know

  • Proposed proofs and counterexamples were not expert verified; the main correctness review used a new instance of the same model that generated each response.
  • The public dataset landing page and code repository are accessible, but they describe evolving snapshots with different displayed counts; the viewer labels the license as “other”. This review did not download the raw files, reconcile a pinned snapshot, or inspect a specific commit.
  • The study evaluates three GPT models in one protocol and does not measure run-to-run variability or compare model families and recommended harnesses; the dataset’s open/not-open filtering also uses language models.

Why it matters

A proof checker can establish that a proof matches a formal statement without establishing that the statement captures the mathematician’s intended conjecture, that the result is novel, or that prior work is credited. OpenConjecture’s paper makes these distinctions explicit in a sample-based analysis. Its candidate answers were not expert verified, and its reviewer was another instance of the solver model, so the results are evidence about evaluation risks and proposed methodology rather than validated discovery rates.
