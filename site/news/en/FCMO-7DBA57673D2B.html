Preprint reports that prompt-specific scorer error can alter LLM evaluation contrasts

An arXiv workshop preprint studies whether an automatic scorer can distort comparisons between prompt conditions even when its pooled accuracy looks high. In an exploratory UUP-Bench case study, a +0.093 Certainty-only-minus-Neutral contrast from automatic scoring became +0.023 under both the primary and secondary LLM raters; the primary-rater interval [-0.059,+0.053] included zero. The authors also report pooled F1 of 0.916 in a separate 288-response audit while arm-level false-positive rates ranged from 0.026 to 0.333. The paper argues that evaluations should audit each arm and test the target contrast on matched cases. All labels came from LLM raters rather than human ground truth; the study is limited to one constructed benchmark and has not been independently reproduced.

What changed

An arXiv workshop preprint studies whether an automatic scorer can distort comparisons between prompt conditions even when its pooled accuracy looks high. In an exploratory UUP-Bench case study, a +0.093 Certainty-only-minus-Neutral contrast from automatic scoring became +0.023 under both the primary and secondary LLM raters; the primary-rater interval [-0.059,+0.053] included zero. The authors also report pooled F1 of 0.916 in a separate 288-response audit while arm-level false-positive rates ranged from 0.026 to 0.333. The paper argues that evaluations should audit each arm and test the target contrast on matched cases. All labels came from LLM raters rather than human ground truth; the study is limited to one constructed benchmark and has not been independently reproduced.

Evidence

  • Claimed — The paper derives that automatic-scorer error in a reported two-arm contrast equals the difference between the arms’ signed scoring biases; high pooled accuracy alone does not establish that the contrast is preserved.
  • Claimed — On a seeded, model-balanced audit of 300 model–item keys in UUP-Bench, the paper reports that an automatic-scoring Certainty-only-minus-Neutral contrast of +0.093 became +0.023 under both a primary and a separate secondary LLM rater. The primary-rater interval was [-0.059,+0.053], spanning zero.
  • Claimed — In a separate balanced 288-response audit, the authors report pooled scorer F1 of 0.916 while the arm-level false-positive rates ranged from 0.026 to 0.333; the paper reports that uncertainty was large and its exploratory test did not establish unequal arm error rates.
  • Not established — The paper does not establish how often this failure occurs across external LLM evaluations, whether the comparison labels match human judgments, or that its recommended audit gates have been independently validated across tasks and evaluator systems.

Strongest baseline

The main empirical comparison uses the automatic pipeline against two separate LLM raters on identical model–item cases; neither rater is human ground truth.

Limitations

  • This is one exploratory author-reported case study on a constructed nonexistent-entity benchmark, not an estimate of how often scoring error distorts LLM evaluation generally.
  • Both the primary and secondary comparison labels were generated by LLM raters; neither is human ground truth, and their agreement does not settle label validity.
  • The balanced 288-response audit was small; the authors report wide uncertainty and did not find statistically established unequal arm false-positive rates in their exploratory test.
  • Independent reproduction of the paper’s empirical results and proposed protocol was not established in this activation.

What we still don't know

  • No independent reproduction or human-adjudicated label set for the reported UUP-Bench contrasts was inspected in this activation; the paper’s two labelers are LLMs and are not ground truth.
  • The case study uses six models and constructed nonexistent-entity questions in one benchmark; transfer to other evaluation tasks, scorer pipelines and prompt families is unknown.

Why it matters

The paper identifies a specific measurement risk: aggregate scorer accuracy can conceal different errors between prompt conditions, which may change the reported comparison. If this result holds in other evaluation settings, paired, arm-specific audits could prevent teams from interpreting scorer artifacts as model behavior. Its single exploratory benchmark and LLM-only labels limit what can be concluded beyond the studied setup.
