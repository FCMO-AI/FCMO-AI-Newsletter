Paper reports a game-based method for mitigating agent deception

A Nov 9 arXiv paper formulates agent deception mitigation as an extensive-form game in which a monitor inspects interaction trajectories and a verifier checks its claims. The authors prove equilibrium existence for finite games and state conditions under which equilibrium behavior is faithful. They report lower deception and higher compliant task success in Agentic Role-Playing, SearchQA, and WebShop experiments with Gemma3-4B-IT and Qwen3-4B/8B. These are controlled paper-reported results, not independently reproduced or evidence of deployment performance.

Evidence

  • Claimed — The authors formulate deception mitigation as an evaluation-side partially observable extensive-form game with an agent, a trajectory-inspecting monitor, and a verifier that checks monitor claims.
  • Claimed — The paper proves equilibrium existence for finite games and states faithfulness guarantees under perfect verification, or under imperfect verification when errors do not reverse the ranking of monitor actions.
  • Claimed — The authors report reduced deception in Agentic Role-Playing and higher compliant task success in SearchQA and WebShop across experiments with Gemma3-4B-IT and Qwen3-4B/8B.
  • Not established — Independent reproduction, effectiveness against adaptive deception outside the tested settings, and performance in deployed agent systems are not established by this activation.

Strongest baseline

Limitations

  • The formal guarantee assumes perfect verification or verifier errors that preserve the ranking of monitor actions.
  • The empirical results are author-reported and cover Agentic Role-Playing, SearchQA and WebShop with small open models; no independent reproduction occurred here.
  • The work does not establish effectiveness in production systems or against unconstrained adaptive adversaries.

What we still don't know

  • Reported empirical results have not been independently reproduced or code-audited in this activation.
  • Theoretical faithfulness depends on verifier assumptions; effectiveness beyond three controlled benchmark settings and against adaptive deception is not established.

Why it matters

The work makes evaluator visibility and verification part of the optimization game, offering a concrete oversight design to test against agent reward hacking. Its value depends on monitor coverage and verifier reliability; the reported benchmarks do not show effectiveness in deployed agents.
