Study reports correlated post-training hiring decisions can raise modeled systemic exclusion

A preprint studies ten base and post-trained language models in a simulated U.S. hiring workflow built from sampled occupational vacancy and career-profile distributions across 76 bachelor-level occupations. The authors report that eight of ten post-trained models became less likely to callback older applicants than their base counterparts, with a median negative shift of 4.7%. They define systemic exclusion as at least nine of ten models assigning callback probability below 0.5; under this measure, the aggregate rate rises from 5.6% for base models to 17.3% after post-training, which the authors attribute mainly to more correlated decisions. These are model outputs on generated profiles, not observed hiring decisions. The data are a snapshot of U.S. online labor markets, demographic signals are simplified, and the study does not identify which post-training stage caused the changes.

What changed

A preprint studies ten base and post-trained language models in a simulated U.S. hiring workflow built from sampled occupational vacancy and career-profile distributions across 76 bachelor-level occupations. The authors report that eight of ten post-trained models became less likely to callback older applicants than their base counterparts, with a median negative shift of 4.7%. They define systemic exclusion as at least nine of ten models assigning callback probability below 0.5; under this measure, the aggregate rate rises from 5.6% for base models to 17.3% after post-training, which the authors attribute mainly to more correlated decisions. These are model outputs on generated profiles, not observed hiring decisions. The data are a snapshot of U.S. online labor markets, demographic signals are simplified, and the study does not identify which post-training stage caused the changes.

Evidence

  • Claimed — The authors evaluate ten base and post-trained LLMs on simulated hiring decisions using generated vacancies and applicant profiles derived from U.S. labor-market distributions across 76 occupations.
  • Claimed — Eight of ten post-trained models show a negative callback-probability shift for older applicants relative to their base versions; the paper reports a median shift of -4.7%.
  • Claimed — With systemic exclusion defined as at least 9 of 10 models assigning callback probability below 0.5, the authors report an estimated rate increase from 5.6% for base models to 17.3% for post-trained models.
  • Demonstrated scope limit — The study uses generated profiles and a shared simulated workflow; it does not measure employers’ actual decisions or identify which post-training stage caused the changes.

Strongest baseline

Within-model base-versus-post-trained comparisons and base-model exclusion estimates under the same profiles and threshold.

Limitations

  • Results are author-reported model callback probabilities, not actual hiring outcomes.
  • Profiles and vacancies come from a temporary U.S. online labor-data snapshot and are simplified rather than full-text resumes and job descriptions.
  • Demographic signals are binary and use names and graduation dates, which may carry other socioeconomic cues.
  • The paper does not isolate the responsible post-training stage.

What we still don't know

  • No independent reproduction of the generated-profile experiments or estimates was inspected.
  • The experiment uses generated profiles and a shared simulated workflow rather than observed deployments or employer decisions.
  • The exclusion rate depends on 9-of-10 agreement and 0.5 callback thresholds; post-training stages are not separated.

Why it matters

Evaluations that look at average demographic differences one system at a time can miss the effect of many systems making similar choices. If supported in applied use, correlated selection could increase the number of people consistently screened out.
