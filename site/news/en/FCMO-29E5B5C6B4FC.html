Evidence

  • Demonstrated — The authors submitted the v1 preprint “FrontierPhysics: Evaluating Agents for End-to-End Physics Research” to arXiv on 4 November 2026.
  • Claimed — The paper reports 56 end-to-end physics-research tasks contributed by 42 physicists across nine domains and evaluates 22 models in 26 configurations. It reports that no configuration passed more than 46 of 168 rollouts; in the main text the highest Pass@1 is 27.4% for Claude Opus 5.5. The authors report manually correcting nine false failures among 168 GPT-6 Astra xhigh rollouts, changing its pass rate from 25/168 (14.9%) to 34/168 (20.2%).
  • Not established — The inspected preprint does not independently establish that its task set or pass-rate estimates generalize to scientific research outside the sampled physics projects; this activation did not reproduce the evaluation or inspect raw task and trajectory artifacts.

Strongest baseline

The paper compares its benchmark design with earlier physics and scientific-agent benchmarks; results are within its own FrontierPhysics v0.1 tasks and should not be treated as a matched universal capability ranking.

Limitations

  • All benchmark performance numbers are author-reported in an arXiv preprint and were not independently reproduced in this activation.
  • The same reviewer model, GPT-5.6 Sol, graded all rollouts; the paper reports a Claude Opus 5.5 regrade was more lenient on its own model family.
  • The paper reports nine false failures corrected during manual review of GPT-6 Astra xhigh rollouts, showing that the benchmark’s error rate and pass counts are sensitive to evaluation review.
  • This physics task sample does not establish performance on other scientific disciplines or real-world research programs.

What we still don't know

  • No independent reproduction or external audit of the task set, rubric judgments or model pass rates was inspected.
  • Although the paper says workspace and trajectory files are collected for Hugging Face datasets, this activation did not identify or inspect the corresponding public dataset and code release.

Why it matters

The benchmark tests whether agents can carry a multi-stage research project through to a correct, defensible manuscript, rather than solve a fixed problem or reproduce a supplied paper. If its task curation and grading prove robust, it could sharpen capability comparisons and expose where agents still need expert judgment. The current evidence supports reporting the benchmark and its authors’ results, not a general conclusion that today’s agents can or cannot perform scientific research.
