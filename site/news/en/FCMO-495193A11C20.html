A shared-inference cost study finds allocation rules shift tenant bills by 12–14 points

The paper introduces unalloc, which joins Kubernetes OpenCost allocations, LiteLLM gateway logs and OpenAI/Anthropic provider cost data to expose unattributed spend and reconcile it against invoices. Its accounting examples include synthetic billing fixtures: in one constructed deployment, owner labels only on leader pods left 66% of GPU spend unowned, while a fallback assigned 61% to a Helm chart label. Separately, a paper-reported H100/vLLM test found that token-based allocation assigned a retrieval-heavy tenant 12–14 percentage points more of a shared bill than equal time-share allocation across tested loads. The authors say neither meter is a ground truth; the repository describes one model and one two-minute run per load. These results show that labels, source overlap and allocation rules can change reported cost ownership, not that they change total compute expense or establish a uniquely correct cost split.

Evidence

  • Demonstrated — The paper and its public repository describe unalloc, a tool that joins OpenCost, LiteLLM, OpenAI and Anthropic cost data into a normalized ledger with owner attribution and invoice reconciliation. This establishes the published tool and method, not that every ledger total is correct for an arbitrary deployment.
  • Claimed — In a constructed one-month OpenCost deployment scenario using synthetic allocations, the authors report that labeling only LeaderWorkerSet leader pods left 66% of a deployment’s GPU bill unowned; a fallback assigned 61% to a Helm chart name and reduced the displayed unallocated share to 4%. These are synthetic case-study values, not observed customer billing.
  • Claimed — In the paper’s H100/vLLM test, a token meter assigned a retrieval-heavy tenant 12–14 percentage points more of the shared inference bill than equal time-share allocation across tested loads. The authors state that neither rule is ground truth. The repository describes one model and one two-minute run per load.
  • Claimed — The paper reports that combining overlapping gateway and provider billing sources can double-count spend, and that reading only one page of a billing API can omit a large share. The repository warns that source overlap and pagination must be checked for each deployment.

Strongest baseline

The paper contrasts token-based allocation with equal time-share and references Shapley-style energy attribution; it explicitly rejects treating either tested meter as a ground truth.

Limitations

  • The paper is a single-author, non-peer-reviewed preprint; all case-study outcomes are author-reported and were not independently reproduced in this activation.
  • The 66% unowned and 61% fallback figures come from a constructed one-month synthetic OpenCost scenario, not observed customer spend.
  • The token-versus-time-share result is from one H100 running vLLM, with one model and one two-minute run per load according to the public repository.
  • Neither token count nor equal time-share is a ground truth for assigning shared GPU cost; alternative rules can allocate the same fixed bill differently.
  • Combining OpenCost, gateway and provider billing feeds can double-count overlapping charges; the correct source subset depends on an organization’s actual coverage.
  • Attribution disagreement does not demonstrate higher or lower total compute cost, realized savings or better product economics.
  • The claimed failure modes and measurement differences have not been tested across multiple providers, GPU types, models, serving stacks or production organizations.

What we still don't know

  • The metering disagreement has been reported for one H100/vLLM setup and does not establish which allocation rule best represents resource responsibility or transfers across other servers and workloads.
  • The label-coverage example uses synthetic OpenCost allocations; no independently audited organization ledger or actual finance outcome was inspected.
  • The tool warns that gateway and provider sources may overlap and double-count, but how to identify overlap depends on organization-specific traffic routing.

Why it matters

As organizations share inference servers across teams and products, cost attribution can influence product unit economics, budgets and make-versus-buy decisions. The study makes the accounting boundary visible: attribution depends on owner labels, provider-source overlap and the chosen allocation rule. Its evidence is a combination of synthetic ledgers and a narrow one-H100 experiment; it does not demonstrate customer savings or identify a uniquely correct tenant bill.
