FCMO AI Newsletter — 2026-09-08

Executive read

Today’s strongest pattern is turning expensive intelligence into reusable state—then being disciplined about what that state actually proves.

Google DeepMind’s AlphaGenome Atlas is the clearest example. Instead of forcing researchers to repeatedly run a large sequence-to-function model against chosen variants, DeepMind has precomputed predictions for more than 9 billion possible single-nucleotide substitutions into an approximately 1-petabyte research resource. That changes the economics of hypothesis generation in genomics: expensive model inference can become a searchable scientific substrate. But the map is still prediction, not measurement, and independent evaluations already show that AlphaGenome’s quality can vary sharply by cellular context.

The same systems question appears elsewhere. SKILL.state reports that long-running agents can replace most replayed conversational history with a compact validated execution state, cutting cumulative tokens by more than an order of magnitude in its strongest long-horizon tests. RISE reuses already-generated RL trajectories to create a synthetic future teacher and extract denser supervision, but still spends substantially more training compute than plain GRPO. Gemini 3.8 Flash keeps a low token price while explicitly using more reasoning steps and tool calls on harder work, reminding us that price per token is not cost per solved task.

A new interpretability result adds a security twist: refusal-related activation directions appear transportable across transformer, state-space, recurrent and hybrid language models after representation alignment. That may make some safety probes more reusable across architectures—but it may make white-box steering attacks more portable too. The paper’s own control prevents an overclaim: detector-triggered activation steering does not outperform simply issuing a fixed refusal whenever the same detector fires.

The business/geopolitics slice is unusually concrete today. Mistral has directly confirmed a €3 billion Series D at a post-money valuation above €21 billion, led by Samsung, and says the capital will expand frontier research and training compute. That is consequential for European sovereign-AI capacity, but financing is an input to capability, not evidence of capability. The next meaningful numbers are commissioned megawatts, accelerator deliveries, training runs and model releases.

1. AlphaGenome Atlas: model inference becomes scientific infrastructure

AlphaGenome Atlas takes a strong sequence-to-function model and moves a huge portion of its inference workload upfront. DeepMind says the resource contains predicted molecular effects for more than 9 billion single-letter DNA substitutions, covering essentially three alternative bases at each position in the human reference genome, and occupies roughly 1 PB.

The underlying AlphaGenome model has a stronger evidence base than a normal product launch. Its peer-reviewed Nature paper describes 1-megabase sequence inputs and thousands of predicted molecular tracks spanning gene regulation. DeepMind reports that it matched or exceeded strong external methods on 25 of 26 variant-effect evaluations.

Atlas adds a new AlphaGenome Variant Impact score and indexed feature explanations so researchers can search the precomputed space instead of repeatedly invoking the model. The important systems idea is broader than genomics: if a scientific hypothesis space is enumerable and an expensive model will be queried repeatedly, there may be a point where precompute once, index well, reuse many times beats on-demand inference.

The limits are not cosmetic. Independent MPRA benchmarking found AlphaGenome correlations around 0.680 in HEK293T and 0.616 in HepG2, but performance across the tested architectures fell to roughly 0.147–0.185 in neuronal progenitors. Separate work also warns that realistic linked alleles can change sequence-to-function conclusions compared with testing one variant against the reference genome.

So the Atlas should be read as a high-value hypothesis generator, not a petabyte of biological ground truth. The decisive metric is not how many variants are scored. It is how many experimentally validated discoveries are obtained per unit of wet-lab effort, compute and researcher attention.

What would strengthen the case: independent calibration of the new AVI score on held-out CRISPR, splicing, MPRA and eQTL data; prospective disease-gene validation with complete denominators; and full accounting of Atlas build/storage/network cost versus repeated AlphaGenome inference.

2. SKILL.state: persistent agents may need operational state more than replayed history

SKILL.state proposes a simple runtime change. Each agent step receives the task specification, a compact structured state and the newest observation. The model can reason normally during that step, but only a validated state patch and action survive into the next one. Old chain-of-thought and conversational history are not continuously replayed.

In the authors’ Warehouse benchmark at horizon 100, SKILL.state reports 0.94 accuracy using 65,408 cumulative tokens. Full ReAct uses about 1.25 million tokens at 0.84, summarization memory about 1.08 million at 0.87, and a structured-state-plus-history baseline about 1.06 million at 0.91. At horizon 200, SKILL.state remains at 0.94 with roughly 122,000 tokens while the summarization baseline exceeds 6 million.

A particularly useful control holds the prompt budget near SKILL.state’s level. Sliding-window truncation scores 0.18, capped summarization 0.52 and LLMLingua-compressed ReAct 0.22, versus 0.94 for the explicit-state system. That weakens the easy explanation that the result is merely a side effect of shorter prompts.

The bigger confound is architectural: the state schemas are hand-authored by domain. Humans are effectively telling the agent what categories of information are important enough to survive. That may be exactly the right engineering choice, but it is different from proving that a model can autonomously discover sufficient state.

There is also a durability boundary. Information discarded today cannot be recovered tomorrow if its relevance only becomes obvious later. A stronger persistent-agent design therefore looks less like “state instead of history” and more like compact mutable operational state + immutable external event history available for selective retrieval and reconstruction.

3. RISE: reuse the trajectory, but count the extra compute

RISE—Recursive Improvement via Self-Extrapolating Policy Distillation—starts with an RLVR update, observes the direction that update moved the model, extrapolates farther along that direction to create a temporary synthetic teacher, and distills the teacher back into the student using the same rollouts.

The strongest comparison is not plain GRPO. The authors include GRPO-2×, which applies another gradient pass to the same rollouts. On Qwen3-8B Math Avg, GRPO-2× reaches 60.5, versus 62.5 for RISE-logit and 62.7 for RISE-weight. On Qwen3-1.7B the comparison is 47.3 versus 50.2/49.2. Three-seed aggregates retain gains larger than the reported run-to-run variation.

Agent results are also encouraging in one setup: versus GRPO, the weight-space variant improves ALFWorld from 75.0% to 84.4% and WebShop perfect-success accuracy from 63.3% to 74.2%.

But “no extra rollouts” must not become “no extra compute.” The authors report approximately 1.3× GRPO wall time at 1.7B and 1.6× at 8B. RISE also has a structurally important failure mode: if the verified-RL update points toward a hacked reward, extrapolation can amplify that bad direction. Aggressive extrapolation can diverge, and good anchor settings vary by model family.

The next experiment is straightforward: compare GRPO, GRPO-2× and RISE under equal rollout tokens, equal total forward/backward FLOPs and equal GPU-hours, then deliberately weaken the verifier to measure how aggressively RISE magnifies reward hacking.

4. Refusal representations may cross architecture boundaries

A new preprint asks whether refusal geometry is a quirk of transformer residual streams or a more general language-model representation.

On Falcon-Mamba-7B, the authors report a surface-controlled harmfulness probe reaching AUROC 0.95 at the selective-scan step-size representation and 0.99 at the mixer output, versus 0.54 for a surface-only probe. Across five base Mamba checkpoints from 130M to 7B, the relevant representation remains strongly decodable.

After a rigid Procrustes alignment between representation spaces, a refusal/harm direction learned on Llama can transfer to Falcon-Mamba. In the reported ceiling case, the transported probe reaches AUROC 1.00 on both unrestricted and surface-matched held-out sets. Causal ablations across tested transfer directions also suggest that removing the transported direction increases harmful compliance more than removing a mapped random orthogonal direction.

The security result is eye-catching but needs the paper’s own control attached to it. On Falcon-Mamba, detector-triggered steering reduces a prefix-injection attack from 98% to 5%; under an adaptive suffix attack, reported success falls 15% to 1% while the detector fires on all 100 attacked prompts. Yet a trivial policy that emits a fixed refusal whenever that same detector fires performs just as well.

So the real result is representation portability, not a superior new defense. That still matters. It suggests interpretability probes may transfer across architectural families with surprisingly little adaptation—and that white-box steering attacks may transfer too. The decisive falsifier is whether this survives different post-training recipes, rather than merely different architectures trained toward similar chat/refusal behavior.

5. Gemini 3.8 Flash: cheap tokens can still buy expensive tasks

Google’s Gemini 3.8 Flash release is a useful reminder that inference economics are becoming multi-dimensional. The introductory API price remains $0.75 per million input tokens and $3.75 per million output tokens, matching the prior 3.7 Flash introductory pricing, while Google reports materially stronger coding and agent performance.

At the same time, Google explicitly says the model can work harder on difficult tasks: higher effort can mean more reasoning steps, more iterative tool use and more generated tokens. Its model card also notes occasional slowness and timeouts.

That means “same token price” is not the same statement as “same task price.” For agents, the useful unit is becoming dollars and wall-clock per independently validated completion, including retries and tool calls. A lower-priced model that takes many more steps can lose economically to a more expensive model that finishes reliably in one trajectory.

This is likely to become a recurring evaluation mistake as configurable test-time effort spreads across model families.

6. Mistral’s €3B round: European AI gets more capital, not yet more demonstrated compute

Mistral directly confirms a €3 billion Series D at a post-money valuation above €21 billion. Samsung Electronics led the round, with the Scaleup Europe Fund managed by EQT and PSG Equity co-leading. Mistral says the financing will significantly expand frontier research and scale compute capacity for training powerful models, alongside infrastructure and commercial expansion.

This belongs in the observatory because capital at this scale can change who is able to finance frontier training and locally controlled European inference capacity. It also strengthens the connection between a leading European model lab, a major semiconductor company and Europe’s sovereign-AI strategy.

But the transaction itself proves no model improvement. Mistral has not disclosed how much of the €3B goes to training versus infrastructure or commercial expansion, nor the accelerator count, delivered megawatts, training FLOPs or model roadmap attributable to the round. Its separate plan for up to 1 GW of European compute by 2030 should likewise not be confused with capacity already online.

The useful scoreboard now is capital → accelerators → energized compute → training runs → released capability and economics. Anything earlier in that chain is potential energy.

7. The deeper pattern: full-system accounting is becoming unavoidable

Today’s strongest results look very different, but they fail in the same way if measured too narrowly.

  • AlphaGenome Atlas can reduce repeated inference only after paying the enormous one-time cost of constructing and storing the atlas.
  • SKILL.state can slash replayed context only if the compact state preserves what later decisions need—or durable source history exists for recovery.
  • RISE can reuse rollouts while still adding substantial forward/backward compute.
  • Gemini can preserve list price per token while spending more tokens, tools and time per task.
  • Refusal steering can look like a defense improvement until compared with the much simpler fixed-refusal policy using the same detector.
  • Mistral can raise billions without that capital yet becoming usable training compute.

The common research rule is simple: count the whole causal system, not the component that makes the graph look best.

That means build cost plus reuse for precomputation; write, read and repair cost for memory; rollout plus optimization cost for post-training; model tokens plus tools and retries for agents; detector plus intervention for safety systems; and financing plus actual commissioned hardware for industrial capacity.

  1. AlphaGenome Atlas / AVI: independent calibration against held-out functional assays and prospective disease-gene discoveries; measure validated discoveries per wet-lab budget.
  2. SKILL.state: delayed-relevance tasks, model-discovered schemas and deliberate state corruption, with an immutable event-log recovery condition.
  3. RISE: equal-FLOP and equal-GPU-hour reproduction, then controlled reward-hacking stress tests.
  4. Cross-architecture refusal: repeat transfer across models with intentionally different safety/post-training distributions and compare one-vector versus low-rank subspace representations.
  5. Gemini effort economics: measure complete dollars, latency, tool calls and retries per solved coding/agent task across effort settings.
  6. Mistral capital conversion: track accelerator deliveries, commissioned MW, training runs, model release cadence and open-weight/API economics rather than further valuation headlines.
  7. KVMem and state durability: keep testing whether latent cached history, compact operational state and immutable source history form a better three-layer memory stack than any one of them alone.

Bottom line

The frontier is increasingly about where intelligence lives between expensive computations.

A genome-scale model can become a database. An agent trajectory can become structured state. An RL update can become a temporary teacher. A safety direction can move between architectures. A fast model can buy more thought at inference time. Capital can become compute—but only after a long physical chain actually lands.

The opportunity is large because reuse compounds. The danger is equally clear: once model output is stored, compressed, extrapolated or reused, it becomes very easy to forget what was originally measured, what was inferred, what was merely cached, and what was paid for elsewhere.

The winning systems will not just preserve more intelligence. They will preserve provenance, uncertainty and full cost with it.
