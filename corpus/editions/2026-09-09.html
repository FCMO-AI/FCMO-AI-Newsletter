FCMO AI Newsletter — 2026-09-09

Executive read

Today’s strongest research pattern is that model quality cannot be separated from the control system around the model.

A checkpoint that looks better before post-training can finish worse after it. A verifier can improve a coding agent—or actively make it worse when the verifier is weak. An evolved agent harness can nearly triple task performance, then lose a large fraction of that gain when the model is trained to imitate trajectories produced under a different operating regime. Persistent memory can correctly store that a fact has been revoked yet still hand the revoked fact back to the model as authoritative context. Even a training-efficiency result that saves theoretical FLOPs may fail to save GPU-hours if the hardware cannot exploit the sparsity.

These are not the same mechanism, but they point to the same engineering rule: optimize and evaluate the whole causal loop, not an isolated component score.

The highest-consequence geopolitical item follows the same logic. U.S. agencies now allege industrial-scale extraction of frontier-model outputs by multiple China-based AI companies. The public evidence supports taking the access-and-extraction threat model seriously, but it does not yet establish how much capability, research time or training compute those outputs actually substitute for. Counting stolen or extracted tokens is not the same as measuring transferred intelligence.

1. Good Pretraining, Bad SFT: the best checkpoint now may not be the best checkpoint later

A new 30B-parameter MoE study tests a deceptively important question: if two pretraining checkpoints have consumed the same token budget, should the one with better loss and benchmark scores necessarily be preferred for the rest of the training stack?

In the reported experiment, no.

The CONSTANT and COOLDOWN runs share the first 6.7 trillion tokens and both finish pretraining at 7.5 trillion tokens. COOLDOWN differs by decaying its learning rate over the final 800 billion tokens. At that boundary it looks clearly better: train loss 1.627 versus 1.717, validation loss 1.648 versus 1.734, and benchmark aggregate 0.440 versus 0.415.

Both checkpoints then receive the same broad downstream pipeline: 100B tokens of capability-focused mid-training, 100B tokens of long-context adaptation and 10B tokens of conversational SFT. The ranking reverses. CONSTANT finishes at 0.360, while COOLDOWN finishes at 0.247. A weighted merge of 20 trailing CONSTANT checkpoints reaches 0.363.

The authors also give COOLDOWN substantially more tuning opportunity, including nine mid/long learning-rate configurations and 27 combinations once SFT factors are included. Its best reported result, 0.294, still remains below the single untuned CONSTANT and MERGE paths.

That sounds like a dramatic loss of trainability, but one correction matters enormously: much of the aggregate gap is caused by a severe HumanEval+ completion pathology. Remove HumanEval+ and the CONSTANT–COOLDOWN aggregate gap falls from roughly 0.113 to 0.016. For one COOLDOWN checkpoint, evaluating only the first generated code block raises HumanEval+ from 7.3% to 61.0%; the model had retained much of the code competence but repeatedly continued generating until the token cap.

So the paper does not prove that a better-looking pretraining checkpoint mysteriously destroys downstream intelligence. It does show something more defensible and still important: current loss and benchmark rank can fail to predict the quality of a checkpoint as a substrate for continued training.

The proposed diagnostic is “solution density”—how much good behavior survives small random perturbations around the checkpoint. CONSTANT and the checkpoint merge occupy locally more forgiving regions than COOLDOWN on the two tested tasks. That correlation is intriguing, but it is not yet a causal mechanism: it is measured on only GSM8K and MBPP, with one model family and one seed per setup.

What would strengthen the case: repeat the experiment across dense and MoE families, multiple seeds and different post-training recipes; separate generation/stopping failures from lost capability; and test whether a cheap perturbation-based diagnostic predicts downstream trainability better than present-day loss.

2. ExecCritic: a weak verifier can make an agent worse than having no verifier

ExecCritic separates software repair from behavioral test generation. Instead of allowing a repair agent to keep rewriting both its solution and its own success criterion, an independent test policy generates tests that remain frozen while source-only revisions proceed.

The cleanest result is not the system’s top score. It is the sign flip caused by verifier quality.

On SWE-bench Verified, the same base repair policy reports 61.2% resolution with no generated-test feedback. Give it weak base-Qwen-generated tests and performance drops to 57.3%. Give it stronger generated tests and it rises to 65.3%. With oracle fail-to-pass tests it reaches 69.4%.

That is unusually useful negative evidence. More feedback is not automatically more supervision. Bad authority can be worse than no authority.

The fully trained test+repair system reaches 72.6%, but that number should not be compared naively with the 61.2% no-test baseline: the full system includes role-specific training and additional generation/revision compute, and the authors acknowledge it is not compute-matched.

The practical lesson is broader than coding. Any agent architecture that relies on critics, judges, constitutional checks, simulations or synthetic tests needs to measure not merely whether the verifier exists, but its false-positive and false-negative rates and whether the acting policy can exploit correlated weaknesses.

A decisive next experiment would hold the model and total compute fixed while comparing mutable self-tests, an independent frozen verifier, an independently trained high-quality verifier and an oracle. The quantity to optimize is not “number of checks.” It is true hidden-objective success per unit of verification cost.

3. Co-Evolving Harnesses: expert trajectories can become bad training data after the scaffold changes

A Salesforce study provides another counterexample to the assumption that data produced by a stronger model is automatically good imitation data.

Across seven enterprise-agent tasks, evolving the operating harness around a Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B-Instruct student raises mean performance from 29.2 to 78.0. The stronger expert also benefits from the student-evolved harness, suggesting that the scaffold itself contains real task-solving value.

Then comes the surprising part. Full expert-trajectory imitation after harness evolution reduces the student from 78.0 to 63.1, and all seven tasks regress. Apply the same kind of imitation before harness evolution and it helps, moving the original model from 29.2 to 35.5.

The failure analysis is consistent with a distribution mismatch: planning failures rise from 1.1% to 14.6% after imitation, even while some knowledge failures improve. The teacher’s trajectories may be strong in isolation but poorly matched to the internal behavior that the student’s evolved harness now expects.

A more local on-policy correction method—asking for expert help at the student’s own failing state rather than copying whole off-policy trajectories—avoids the collapse and moves the mean from 78.0 to 79.7. That is a modest improvement, not a new capability explosion; its main value is preserving a strong policy+harness equilibrium.

The implication for post-training is sharp: training data quality is conditional on the policy and scaffold that will consume it. A globally stronger trajectory can still be locally wrong training data.

4. Don’t Drop Dropout: fewer training FLOPs plus one checkpoint with variable depth

A large layer-dropout study revisits stochastic depth with a much more systematic training recipe.

Across more than 2,400 experiments, models from 271M to 8.2B parameters and datasets up to 160B tokens, the authors report that dropping whole transformer blocks during training can match or improve dense-training loss while reducing executed training FLOPs by as much as 25% at fixed steps. The recipe combines per-sequence whole-block dropping, optimizer-transfer scaling, protection of earlier layers and a dropout schedule that decays to zero.

The resulting checkpoint is also depth-elastic. At inference it can support early exit, middle-layer skipping and self-speculative decoding, with reported speedups reaching roughly 1.5× under selected conditions and small accuracy loss.

The most important caveat is hardware. All reported pretraining experiments use Cerebras CS-3. That execution architecture may exploit dynamic layer sparsity very differently from conventional GPU clusters.

Therefore “25% fewer FLOPs” does not yet mean 25% fewer GPU-hours, 25% less electricity or 25% lower training cost. Communication, kernel efficiency, pipeline bubbles and dynamic-control overhead can consume the theoretical saving.

The critical replication is mundane but decisive: train matched dense and layer-dropout models on H100/B200-class GPU systems and on CS-3, then separately report FLOPs, wall time, achieved utilization, joules and dollars. If the advantage survives, variable executed depth becomes interesting not only as a training trick but as a way to build one model that can trade quality for latency dynamically at serving time.

5. Revoked but Still Authoritative: memory deletion must be enforced before context reaches the model

Persistent-agent memory introduces a lifecycle problem that ordinary retrieval benchmarks barely test: what happens when a fact was once valid but has now been revoked?

A released experimental harness covers Graphiti, Zep, Mem0, LangMem and Cognee across nine policy-conflict scenarios. Its central failure mode is architectural rather than linguistic. A memory store can retain metadata saying an old fact is invalid yet still return that record through the default retrieval path. Once stale material is assembled into the model’s context, a prompt saying “ignore revoked memories” is an extremely weak security boundary.

The study distinguishes retrieval success from downstream decision and guardrail-bypass success and compares prompt hardening, output filtering, store filtering, hybrid defenses and a backend-agnostic retrieval guard. The proposed guard enforces invalidity at the boundary where stored records become model context.

That is the right abstraction even if the specific benchmark effect sizes still need independent reproduction. Deletion, supersession and revocation are authority properties of the memory system. They should not depend on the reasoning model remembering which retrieved sentence is no longer allowed to count.

The next tests should add concurrent writers, partial deletion, replicated stores, multi-agent propagation and false-revocation controls. A secure memory layer must both block stale authority and preserve legitimate recall; simply deleting aggressively can create a different failure mode.

6. TASTE2: adding streaming speech without throwing away the text model

TASTE2 explores a useful multimodal design goal: extend a pretrained text model into live speech interaction while retaining most of the original language capability rather than rebuilding the whole system around audio.

The authors report 56.3% on LLaMA-Questions versus 57.3% for the Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct text reference, approximately 98.2% retained accuracy. The architecture supports incremental listening, streamed speech generation and interruption handling, so it is closer to a live duplex agent than an utterance-at-a-time speech wrapper.

But the latency boundary remains substantial. Mean time to first audio is reported at 2.701 seconds on two RTX A6000 GPUs after TensorRT acceleration, and the authors explicitly acknowledge that natural conversation remains challenging. Paralinguistic control is uneven as well: speaking-rate control is much stronger than several other expressive attributes.

The useful comparison is therefore not speech benchmark score alone. The real test is a matched ASR→LLM→TTS pipeline versus a text-aligned integrated speech model on the same base language model and hardware, measuring first-audio latency, interruption reaction time, semantic error, tool-task success, memory footprint and energy per conversational minute.

7. U.S.–China model distillation: strong evidence of extraction activity, weak evidence of how much intelligence it transfers

NSA, CISA and FBI released a joint advisory alleging industrial-scale frontier-model distillation campaigns by DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, Alibaba, MiniMax, StepFun and Z.AI. The agencies describe distributed querying through providers, clouds, aggregators and other infrastructure, including methods intended to obscure coordinated extraction.

This deserves attention because teacher-model output can function as a form of external training data. If it substitutes materially for expensive frontier training or research, then API access becomes part of the geopolitical compute and capability-diffusion story rather than merely a product-security issue.

But the evidence must remain carefully separated. The public advisory is an official U.S. government attribution, not a released forensic dataset proving every company/model claim. Anthropic’s earlier investigation independently supports organized large-scale extraction associated with DeepSeek, Moonshot and MiniMax, including more than 16 million Claude exchanges through roughly 24,000 fraudulent accounts, but that does not independently establish every new attribution in the government advisory.

Most importantly, billions of extracted tokens are not a measurement of capability gained. The public record does not tell us how many training FLOPs, research months or dollars were actually avoided, nor whether reasoning-rich teacher outputs transfer broad capabilities or merely improve narrow tasks.

The agencies also characterize Chinese-government awareness as likely. That must remain a confidence-qualified attribution, not be upgraded into public proof of state direction. And the advisory itself is not a sanction, export control or Entity List action.

The scientifically useful experiment is to hold student architecture and training compute fixed while varying teacher diversity, output type and quantity, then measure capability gain per teacher token and per dollar. That would convert a geopolitical argument into a measurable capability-transfer curve.

8. The deeper pattern: authority and state are becoming first-class model components

Today’s results keep finding failure modes in the same conceptual gap.

A checkpoint score is state about where training is now, not authority about where continued training should begin. A verifier produces feedback, but that feedback should not be trusted merely because it is executable. A teacher trajectory is evidence of one good behavior under one scaffold, not a universal policy target. A stored memory may have existed historically without retaining current authority. A theoretical FLOP reduction can exist mathematically without producing a hardware-level cost reduction.

This suggests a more mature unit of AI engineering:

model + state + authority + environment + verifier + cost model.

Evaluating only the model hides too much of the causal system.

  1. Checkpoint trainability: multi-seed dense/MoE replications and whether perturbation-based solution density predicts continuation quality.
  2. Verifier authority: compute-matched ExecCritic-style tests across domains where correctness is less executable than software repair.
  3. Harness-policy coupling: whether the expert-imitation collapse survives full-parameter tuning, larger models and independently designed scaffolds.
  4. Layer dropout: conventional-GPU wall-clock and energy replication, then scaling beyond 8.2B and into MoE training.
  5. Memory revocation: deterministic retrieval-only reproduction, concurrent writers, replication and multi-agent propagation.
  6. Live speech: matched end-to-end latency and energy comparisons rather than isolated speech quality.
  7. Distillation geopolitics: primary responses from named firms and China, provider telemetry, policy follow-through, and above all measured capability transfer per extracted token.
  8. September 9 research batch: finish chronological enumeration; several fresh candidates in agent memory, dynamic routing, RL environments, robotics and interpretability remain below publication confidence until deeper verification is complete.

Bottom line

The frontier is becoming less forgiving of component-level thinking.

A more capable checkpoint can be a worse training substrate. More verification can reduce true success. Better teacher trajectories can damage a co-adapted agent. Stored knowledge can remain technically present after its authority should have disappeared. Fewer FLOPs can fail to become cheaper compute.

The practical response is not to distrust every new technique. It is to insist on the right causal unit of measurement.

The model is no longer the whole system. The important question is whether the entire loop—state, authority, feedback, hardware and policy—produces the claimed result under matched conditions.
